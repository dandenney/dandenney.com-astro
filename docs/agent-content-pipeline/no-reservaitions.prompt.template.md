# Quillan Prompt Template — No Reservaitions

Use this as the canonical starting template for `prompt.md` in every No Reservaitions run.

```md
Write a finished No Reservaitions review body for Dan Denney's site.

The reader should feel as if a sharp, worldly companion was there with Dan: noticing the room, understanding why the stop mattered, caring about appetite, and finding human meaning in ordinary choices. Aim for an observant, unsentimental, lightly wry travel-and-food sensibility. Do not imitate a named writer's exact voice, catchphrases, or sentence patterns.

## Primary rule

Write from inside the remembered experience, not above it.

Concrete scene, people, place, history, appetite, and sensory memory come before abstract interpretation. Do not turn two orders into competing “theories,” “arguments,” or “cases” unless that language arises naturally from a well-established scene. If the venue name could be swapped for another without breaking the review, the draft is not specific enough.

## Voice
- Conversational, vivid, worldly, appetite-first, and emotionally present.
- Specific judgment is welcome; generic praise is not.
- Light profanity is acceptable when it earns the sentence rather than performing attitude.
- Use venue history or local context when it changes how the visit feels, not as a detached encyclopedia paragraph.
- Never invent atmosphere, flavor, texture, service, dialogue, reactions, history, or relationships.

## Venue facts you may rely on
- Title: <TITLE>
- Venue/entity: <VENUE>
- Address: <ADDRESS>
- City/state/country: <CITY>, <STATE>, <COUNTRY>
- Coordinates for frontmatter: <LNG_LAT>
- Official info URL: <INFO_URL>
- Corroborated public facts: <PUBLIC_FACTS>

## Resolved item research
- Resolved dishes/drinks: <RESOLVED_ITEMS>
- Items that remain ambiguous: <UNRESOLVED_ITEMS>
- Confidence notes: <ITEM_CONFIDENCE>
- For golf-tagged entries, this section can be sparse when food and drink were not central.

## Narrative anchors

### Firsthand memory from Dan
- Why they were there: <WHY_THERE>
- Companions or occasion: <COMPANIONS_OR_OCCASION>
- Room, setting, or arrival: <ROOM_OR_SETTING>
- Strongest sensory memory: <SENSORY_MEMORY>
- Visit date: <YYYY-MM-DD>
- Items, drinks, or bill: <ITEMS>
- Other observed details: <DETAILS>
- Golf/outing details when relevant: <GOLF_DETAILS>
- Visual corroboration, facts only: <PHOTO_NOTES>

### Researched narrative context
- Venue or building history: <VENUE_HISTORY_WITH_SOURCES>
- Owners, neighborhood, or regional role: <LOCAL_CONTEXT_WITH_SOURCES>
- Why those facts matter to this visit: <CONTEXT_RELEVANCE>

### Missing context and readiness
- Missing details: <MISSING_CONTEXT>
- Follow-up needed: <YES_OR_NO>

If follow-up is needed, stop and ask Dan one compact question covering the missing experiential details. Do not draft around the absence.

Never mention or imply how visual facts or research were obtained. Photos, screenshots, prompts, packets, and source notes are evidence, not subjects of the review.

## Dan-approved calibration

Use these as calibration for presence and specificity, not as phrases to copy:

- “Dutch Maid feels like the sort of bakery that existed before anyone decided bakeries needed exposed brick, Edison bulbs, and a social media strategy.”
  - Why it works: it locates the place in history and attitude immediately.
- “Sugar, caffeine, butter. Civilization has been built on less.”
  - Why it works: appetite, rhythm, judgment, and humor arrive in one compact beat.
- “The steak was the centerpiece, rich and tender, the kind of plate that makes conversation briefly stop because everyone has more important work to do.”
  - Why it works: the food is tied to a human moment at the table.
- “Birthdays don’t really need much. Good food, a beautiful room, a bottle worth lingering over, and someone you’re grateful to be sitting across from.”
  - Why it works: the meal resolves into why the night mattered.

Do not reuse their openings, metaphors, lists, cadence, or closing moves. Reproduce the qualities: a real room, a real appetite, a real reason for being there, and an earned emotional judgment.

## Writing movement

Let the material determine the form, generally moving through:
1. A real scene, historical fact, or reason for arriving.
2. The people, room, or local logic that made this stop particular.
3. Food and drink described through supported sensory detail and appetite.
4. What the meal revealed or why the memory survived.

Do not force all four movements into equal paragraphs. A bakery stop, bar drink, concert, golf outing, and birthday dinner should not share identical architecture.

## Hard constraints
- 3-5 short paragraphs, usually 180-420 words.
- No headings or bullet points in the returned review body.
- Mention at least two concrete specifics when the evidence contains them.
- Do not mention “the photo,” “the image,” “the picture,” screenshots, prompts, packets, research, or publishing.
- Do not flatten a resolved composed dish into a vague noun.
- Treat unresolved items cautiously or omit them rather than guessing.
- No generic recommendation formulas, forced caveats, “who should go” endings, or practical-takeaway boilerplate.
- End with an earned judgment, image, or emotional truth specific to this visit.

## Mandatory editorial self-check
Reject and rewrite unless every answer is yes:
- Does the opening place the reader somewhere real instead of announcing a topic?
- Would the piece break if the venue name were replaced with another?
- Does at least one paragraph connect food or drink to people, place, history, or occasion?
- Are sensory claims supported by Dan's memory, visible evidence, or reliable menu research?
- Does researched history illuminate the visit rather than interrupt it?
- Does the prose avoid abstract order-analysis standing in for lived experience?
- Does it avoid source artifacts and publishing language?
- Is the final judgment specific enough that this exact visit had to happen for it to be written?
```

## Why this template exists

Dan's approved revisions were more vivid than drafts produced from metadata-heavy packets. The difference was not stronger imitation language; it was scene, history, sensory memory, and human stakes. This template makes those inputs part of the writing contract while preserving factual grounding.
