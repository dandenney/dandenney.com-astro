"I never really got over you / I just learned to do without." Josiah Leming puts the whole confession of **Burn** in its first verse, and the distinction he draws is a hard one. Getting over someone means healing. Doing without means rationing, building a life around the hole and calling it fine. The narrator knows which one he did, and he says it plainly, which is its own kind of courage.

The story is short. Someone left, out of a clear blue sky, trading today for tomorrow. The narrator's response is the part that stings: he held his tongue, stood up strong, and paid the full price in sorrow. Pride and grief stand right next to each other, and neither wins. He's proud he didn't beg, and it's obvious the silence cost him. Then comes the line that names the stakes: the day you walked away "Was the last day of my youth." The lover becomes the border between the boy and the man in the mirror, and there's no crossing back.

The chorus is where the restraint gives. Every long-lost dream, every memory, gets boiled down to a lesson learned, and then the tears turn into fuel and someone strikes the match. The opening vow to burn this body down sits uneasily here. Is it a purge or a wish to be gone? The song doesn't decide, and it's better for it. Even the lyric's structure tells on him: the closing line of the last chorus calls "burn" twice where the earlier one called it three times, as if the breath runs out before the fire does.

What makes it land is that the music refuses to blaze. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through an arpeggio over a simple, sustained bass line, and the drums keep a plain kick, snare and hi-hat beat. The tempo is slow and the groove is gentle, almost easy. A lone voice sings in a mid-range with a slightly raw edge, earnest and direct, with no harmonies to soften it. The room sounds small and dry, close and intimate. A song about setting yourself alight, played as if the narrator is still holding his tongue.

That tension fits the record it lives on. Leming wrote "Burn" and produced and mixed *Endurance* himself, and the album was recorded in Nashville. It's a stripped-down, acoustic-leaning album that carries the Bonnevilles name, a band named for an old Pontiac he and bassist Josh Nyback drove around the desert. He's from Morristown, Tennessee. He dropped out of high school at 17 and toured the country living out of his car, and later came a brief stint on *American Idol* and a 2008 Warner Bros. deal. The song comes out of that Appalachian confessional strain of folk and Americana, where the plainest words do the heaviest lifting, and at just over two minutes it doesn't waste one.

It sticks because it gives you the difference between surviving a loss and getting over it, then plays that difference so quietly you can hear the match before it's struck.
