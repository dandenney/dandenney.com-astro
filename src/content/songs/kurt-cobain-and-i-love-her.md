Brett Morgen found a Beatles love song on a random tape in Kurt Cobain's archive of home recordings. He pulled it into his documentary *Montage of Heck*, and the raw, acoustic take of **And I Love Her** ended up on the deluxe soundtrack album. It's two minutes of one of the plainest love songs ever written, recorded at home by someone famous for anything but plainness.

The choice is the first thing to sit with. Lennon and McCartney's 1964 ballad doesn't plead or argue. Its narrator just reports good fortune: she gives him everything, she brings the kiss, he loves her. Far Out calls Cobain a notorious Beatles fan who covered the band only once on record, and notes that his distrust of Paul McCartney makes a cover of a McCartney song more interesting. That's a real tension. Admiration and suspicion can live in the same listener, and here the admiration wins out long enough to get the song on tape.

The lyric is built from simple opposites set in an old-fashioned word order: "Bright are the stars that shine / Dark is the sky." The inversion gives it the weight of a hymn, and the darkness shows up only as the thing that makes the light visible. Far Out hears Cobain's version as a goth-tinged reimagining, and that reading has a foothold in the words. The night sky was always part of the picture.

The best moment is a slip of address. The verses talk *about* her, in the third person, a little shy, a little formal. Then the song turns: "Hold you near me." For a line the narrator stops describing the lover and speaks straight to her. That shift is where the song shows how much it feels, and it passes as quickly as it comes.

Morgen compared the collection to Dylan's Bootleg Series. He said it shows a side of Cobain he couldn't fully explore in a three-piece band. The album gathers covers, demos, and spoken pieces recorded between 1986 and 1994. Cobain died in that last year, at 27. So when the song promises, again and again, that this love will never die, two feelings sit side by side and don't settle. The lyric is pure confidence. The listener knows how the story of the person singing it ends. Neither cancels the other.

You come away holding a vow of forever in a voice that didn't get one, and the song's tenderness makes that harder to put down, not easier.
