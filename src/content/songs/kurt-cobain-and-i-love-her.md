The only time **"And I Love Her"** speaks directly to the person it's about is once, in three words: "Hold you near me." Everywhere else she's "her," described to someone else, held at the distance of a report. Then for one line the singer turns and talks to her before turning back. Kurt Cobain covers the Beatles' 1964 song here, and in his hands that turn lands hard, because nothing in the recording is trying to sound sure of itself.

What's here is one acoustic guitar, strummed slowly, with a relaxed tempo that seems to drag a little behind the beat, and a voice in a low-middle range that sounds raw and a bit strained. There are no harmonies. You hear a small, dry room and a person sitting in it. The roughness doesn't fight the song. It changes what the song is about.

The lyric is almost pure plain devotion: "She gives me everything." It's a song with no conflict in it. No rival, no leaving, no doubt, just stars, a dark sky, and a promise that this love "Will never die." Sung cleanly, that promise sounds like a fact. Sung in a strained voice at a dragging pace, it sounds like something the singer needs to say out loud to keep believing it. The words stay certain while the voice carrying them sounds worn, and the recording never settles which one to trust. Every stanza lands on the title line, a statement made to nobody in particular.

This began as a home tape. Brett Morgen found the take on a random tape while digging through Cobain's archive of home cassettes for his documentary *Montage of Heck*, and used it in the film. It surfaced online in April 2015, came down, and then arrived officially as part of *Montage of Heck: The Home Recordings*, with a 7-inch pairing it with an early demo of "Sappy." Morgen described these tapes as another side of Cobain, an outlet he couldn't fully use in a three-piece band, and compared the project to Dylan's Bootleg Series. That fits. This is Cobain and a guitar in a small room with a love song, and the private setting gives the song a kind of shyness.

What stays with you is that single turn to "you." For one line, a voice that sounds worn stops describing love and speaks to the one it's meant for.
