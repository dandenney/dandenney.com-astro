Late in the lyric of **Deep In The Night**, Samuel T. Herring stops singing "I belong to you" and sings "And I belonged to you." It's one past-tense verb at the end of a love song, and then the present tense comes rushing back: "I belong, I belong / To you." The slip lasts a single line, but the song turns on it.

The verses are set in the small hours. The singer is stuck in a room, missing a partner who is "still asleep," and the missing seems to grow the quieter things get. Herring reaches for a big image, "I'm like a prowling lion, that sinking pride," and the pun does real work. A pride of lions, and pride in the plain sense, going under together. The restlessness is half animal and half wounded ego. The tender part comes later: "it's the only time when I find peace," seeing someone there, sound asleep. The chorus gives the whole self away with "And that's where I began," a person who only knows where they start by where someone else ends. Devotion and dread share the same room here, and the song never picks one.

Herring has said he wrote it believing their love would carry them to the end and that he would find his partner. The couple split at the end of 2021, and he told NME that hope didn't play out. The song later became track three on *People Who Aren't There Anymore*, a 2024 record whose story he has described as centred on the end of his relationship with the Swedish actress Julia Ragnarsson. Heard with that in mind, the slip into "belonged" carries more weight.

The music doesn't brood. A synth bass walks under a drum machine's kick, snare, and hi-hat. A pad holds sustained chords, and a clean electric guitar picks arpeggios over the top. The groove is steady and driving, the tempo moderate, the production polished and smooth. Press called it a slowed, romantic ballad, and it is one, but it keeps a pulse you could walk to. Herring's voice sits mid-range with a slight rasp, carrying long melodic lines under a touch of reverb. For a Baltimore synth-pop band built on big feeling, the delivery stays level and sustained, and that lets the words do the damage.

Albert Birney's black-and-white video shows the band performing in silhouette, which suits a song about someone you can see in the dark but not quite reach.

It sticks because it's a vow sung by someone who seems to sense, even while making it, that the person they're singing to is already slipping away, and you end up holding both feelings at once.
