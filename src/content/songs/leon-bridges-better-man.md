The promise in **Better Man** is so small it almost slips past: "I just wanna be a better man." Not a perfect one, not a new one. Leon Bridges opens by saying the narrator doesn't want much, and the modesty is the tell. Someone who has wrecked something is asking for as little as possible, hoping a small request is easier to grant.

The confession comes fast and without much shame. He admits to nights out with the Jezebels, "Under perfume sheets," a phrase that carries the smell of the room he's trying to leave behind. Then he can't resist a little self-portrait: a golden smile, a heart overflowing. Even mid-apology, he wants some credit for being lovable. It's an honest kind of mess. Regret and vanity share the same verse, and the song never sorts them out. By the second verse the posture drops. He's down on his knees, thirsty, running to her well, and the language turns toward scripture, the woman as water in a dry place.

Then the big gesture: "I'd swim the Mississippi river." It's a huge, old-fashioned vow, the kind nobody could keep. But asked about that line, Bridges pointed to his family's roots in New Orleans and a father who moved to Atlanta for work, back to New Orleans, and finally to Texas. The impossible swim has a real map behind it.

The band plays all of this as if forgiveness might already be on its way. A shuffle rolls at an unhurried pace, the snare snapping the backbeat while a walking bass line strolls underneath. Clean electric guitar picks out arpeggiated chords, and a keyboard holds long chords that warm the edges. Bridges sings smooth and clear, never straining, and backing harmonies from Brittni Jessie, Tyesha Simpson, Ansley Dougherty and Njia Martin fill out the phrases. The room matters too. The album was made at Niles City Sound in Fort Worth, founded by co-writers Chris Vivion, Austin Jenkins and Josh Block, a studio that works with eight-track tape and 70-year-old microphones. The recording has that warm, roomy feel.

Critics heard Sam Cooke and Otis Redding, and the debt is plain: the gospel-inflected plea, the sweetness laid over a sinner's admission. Bridges was once playing this on guitar to about ten people. When the song surfaced on the Dallas blog Gorilla vs. Bear alongside "Coming Home," the response was huge, and he signed to Columbia within about six months of the songs taking off online.

What sticks is the gap between the size of the sin and the size of the ask, and the song lets you feel how badly someone can want a second chance while still hoping you'll notice their smile.
