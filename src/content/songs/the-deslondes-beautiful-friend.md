**Beautiful Friend** is an apology that won't quite start. The verses keep opening on a condition: if you really want to know, if you ask just one time, then I'll tell you how I feel. The narrator has the words ready but needs the other person to make the first move. Dan Cutler, the Deslondes' bassist, wrote it while thinking about how to make up with his now-ex-wife after he'd upset her. You can hear that stalemate in the grammar. Someone is standing in the doorway, sorry, and still a little too proud to walk in unasked.

The chorus is where the guard drops. It doesn't beg for forgiveness or love. It asks for something smaller and harder: would you want to be a beautiful friend to me, because I want to be one to you. After a fight, that's the honest request. It means *let's be on the same side again*.

The middle verse is the song's best piece of writing. It tries out the escape fantasy and talks itself out of it in the same breath: "If I didn't have you to lose / I guess I'd be a free man." The phrase "you to lose" carries the fear plainly. Then comes the turn, a "heartworn" person asking what good freedom would even do, and the line that stays with you: "The cost of love that you never had." Freedom gets weighed against loneliness, and the narrator doesn't pretend the choice is close.

Cutler says the song grew out of the Stanley Brothers hymn "Jordan," and that hymn root shows in its plainness: short lines, a repeated plea, nothing clever standing between the singer and the confession. The band plays it as a relaxed, medium-tempo shuffle. Brushes swish on the drums, the bass walks, an acoustic guitar is fingerpicked underneath, and steel guitar lays long sustained notes over the top. The lead voice is clear and smooth, sitting in the middle of its range, with a second voice shadowing it in close harmony. The room sounds open and clean, so the song feels like a band in a room.

That's notable on *Hurry Home*, the 2017 record where the New Orleans band reached toward electrified, psychedelic soul. "Beautiful Friend" is one of their few true ballads, and it stays close to the front porch. Bassist Howe Pearson remembers someone proposing during a performance of it at one of his first shows with the band. A song written to patch up a marriage that didn't last became somebody else's beginning. That isn't a contradiction. Wanting to be good to someone is the part that carries over to other people.

It sticks because it catches the moment just before reconciliation, when you know exactly what you'd say and are still waiting, hoping, to be asked.
