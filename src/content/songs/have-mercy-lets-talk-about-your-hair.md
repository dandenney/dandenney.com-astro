The breakup in **Let's Talk About Your Hair** happens in a single pronoun. The song opens with two people going to bed and dreaming of better things: a god, a devil, the narrator, and a partner who "lost your wings." When that verse comes back late in the song, the dream is word for word the same, but the "we" has become an "I." Have Mercy never point at the change. They let the repeat do the work, and the bed is half empty before you notice.

Everything between those verses is a conversation that can't happen. The chorus opens on a knot of a line: "But I know that you know that I know that you don't really care." Each clause stacks on the last until there's nowhere left to pretend. Both people can see the end, and each can see the other seeing it. Then comes the dodge: "Let's talk about your hair and how it's grown." Small talk becomes the last thing the two of them can still share. It's a kindness and a surrender at once, and the line right after admits they're getting nowhere. By the final choruses even that shrinks. "And how it's grown" drops away, and the small talk runs out too.

What keeps the song from being pure resignation is the hope the narrator can't quite kill. Early on they admit they always wondered when the love stopped and still can't see it clearly. Then they ask about the man in the partner's dreams and add a short question: "And was it me?" Certainty that it's over sits right next to a wish to have been the one dreamed about, and the song never settles which feeling wins.

Brian Swindle has said Have Mercy's debut full-length, *The Earth Pushed Back*, is about one relationship he had, and this song plays like that record's still center. The band (Swindle, Andrew Johnson, Nick Woolford and Aaron Alt) made it fast in their hometown of Baltimore at The Magpie Cage, with J. Robbins producing, engineering and mixing. Swindle later called the record rushed, with two or three songs still being written in the studio. The album sits in the emo revival, and this song works like an argument the narrator holds with themselves, circling the same words until they wear smooth.

At the band's first headlining tour show in Baltimore, fans screamed these lyrics back at them. A song about two people with nothing left to say became something a room could shout together.

It sticks because most people have sat through the conversation where everyone knows and nobody says it, and this song hands you the knowing part to repeat until it almost feels like an answer.
