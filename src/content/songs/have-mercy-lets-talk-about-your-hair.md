The most devastating move in **Let's Talk About Your Hair** is how small the subject is. Two people have reached the point where the truth is out and nobody will say it, so the conversation turns to the most harmless thing in the room: "Let's talk about your hair and how it's grown." It's a gesture of surrender, and it's also a way of noticing time. Hair grows while you aren't paying attention. So did the distance.

Brian Swindle wrote the song, with Andrew Johnson, Nick Woolford and Aaron Alt also credited as composers. He has said the album it sits on, *The Earth Pushed Back*, is about one relationship, though it touches other subjects too. You can hear the tangle in the refrain's stutter, a chain of knowing that ends in not caring. It's a hall of mirrors built out of plain words. Each "know" piles on another layer of awareness, and none of it changes anything. The narrator wants credit for seeing clearly while admitting, just before the refrain, that he can't see clearly at all. Both are true, and the song doesn't settle it.

Then comes the line that gives the whole thing away: "Tell me about the man in your dreams / And was it me?" Under all the resignation sits a hope that hasn't died. The verse's dream of a god, a devil and the narrator casts the other person as the one who lost their wings, a fall the narrator half-blames and half-mourns. When the verse returns, "we" has become "I." That one pronoun is the breakup. Near the end, the hair line loses its second half, as if the small talk itself is wearing out.

The band plays it like a shout. The drums drive with a prominent snare and busy cymbal work, the bass sits on the roots, and one distorted guitar chugs palm-muted while a second carries a higher melody over it. The vocal is raw and slightly strained, with no harmonies to soften it. J. Robbins produced, recorded and mixed the album at The Magpie Cage in the band's hometown of Baltimore. The sound is thick, compressed, live in the room and unpolished. Swindle said the album was cut in five days and felt rushed, but he also called it his favorite record he'd performed on, with a rawer sound and more powerful songs.

It sits in the 2010s emo revival, and a 2013 profile compared the record's audience to fans of Brand New, Tigers Jaw and Taking Back Sunday. What the song does best is turn one ordinary sentence into a wound.

It sticks because you know the move: when you can't bear to ask the real question, you ask about something small, and then you ask the real one anyway.
