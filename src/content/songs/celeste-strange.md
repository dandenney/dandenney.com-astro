The cruelest line in **Strange** is the plainest one: "I am still me / You are still you." Nothing has been taken. Both people are standing right there, intact, and that is the problem. Celeste writes the end of a love affair as a kind of missing-persons case where nobody went missing, and the chorus keeps running the same arithmetic: strangers, then friends, then lovers, then strangers again. The circle closes on the word it started with, which is exactly how it feels.

The verses carry a quieter argument. The song opens on an attempt, someone who tried to see through the smoke and dirt and found it wouldn't clear, and it reads like a person still keeping score of their own effort. The song reportedly grew out of the 2018 California wildfires, where the loss of people and relationships brought Celeste's own personal experiences to the surface, and you can feel that haze in the opening image. Then the want gets more desperate: "I'd pull your strings for one more dance." It's a puppeteer's wish, a little ugly in its honesty, wanting control just long enough to get one more moment back. The song doesn't punish that impulse. It lets the grief and the self-justification sit in the same breath.

The arrangement stays out of the way. Acoustic piano rolls out slow, arpeggiated chords with a faint swing to them, and a double bass walks underneath. Strings, played by Sebastian Plano, hold long sustained notes rather than swelling for effect. The room is spacious, with plenty of reverb, and the whole thing is clean. Celeste sings alone, no harmonies, the delivery smooth and unhurried, as if raising the voice would admit too much. The Evening Standard called it a slow, smouldering ballad, and that's right: it burns low and never flares. That restraint fits the British soul and jazz-inflected torch-ballad tradition.

Celeste wrote it in Los Angeles with Wrabel and Jamie Hartman, who also produced. It came out in 2019 as the lead single from Not Your Muse, and it has followed Celeste since. Celeste had to talk the team into letting her play it at the BRITs, and has called it an Achilles heel, the one song people seem to want. That makes sense. In the last verse the silence creeps to the bedside and tells the narrator who they are, and the shock of it stops them when they try to stand. It's the most exposed moment on the record, and the chorus that follows doesn't comfort anyone. It just repeats the cycle.

**Strange** sticks because it lets you sit across from someone you once knew completely, both of you unchanged, and feel how much was lost anyway.
