The punchline comes before the pain. **Growing Sideways** opens with Noah Kahan pouring his trauma out on a pricey leather couch belonging to a sad-eyed therapist. The two of them argue about Jesus, find some middle ground, and within four lines he declares himself cured. The rest of the song is the bill coming due.

Kahan wrote it alone. It's track 10 on *Stick Season*, the album he started in his hometown of Strafford, Vermont, in 2020 and recorded at Guilford Sound in Vermont and The Study in Nashville, co-producing with Gabe Simon. He has said the record let him finally put out songs that were close to him, ones he never thought would be released. This song makes it clear why he doubted.

The writing is funny and wrecked in the same breath. He divides his anger into thirty parts and stores the worst of it in his liver, which plays like a bar joke until the next line: "I'm still angry at my parents, for what their parents did to them." That one sentence carries three generations of damage, and he answers it with a shrug: it's a start. Pride and hurt sit together here. He wants credit for showing up, and he knows showing up isn't the same as getting better. The second verse undoes the first. He's off the meds, riding a manic high, spending his savings at a Lulu and suffering in style.

The chorus names the defense outright. He ignores things, moves sideways, and forgets what he felt. Then comes the line that cuts: "I'm terrified that I might never have met me." Everyone around him is growing. The fear isn't that he's broken. It's that he's a stranger to himself. The bridge goes darker and decides it's better to die numb than to feel everything. By the last chorus, the certainty that there are worse ways to stay alive has shifted from his own knowing to the Lord's, as if he can't vouch for it himself anymore.

The music never flinches at any of this, and that calm is the trick. Strummed acoustic guitar, plucked bass and brushed drums hold a moderate, gently swaying groove in a warm, natural-sounding room. Kahan's voice is slightly raspy and breathy, earnest, with no harmonies to soften it. He sounds alone on the couch. *Stick Season* traded the pop production of his earlier records, mostly made with Joel Little, for folk. The confessional plainness recalls Paul Simon and Cat Stevens, the music his mother played at home, set down in New England.

It sticks because it lets you laugh at the same coping trick you might be using, then lands on "I guess I'll drive," which is half surrender and half the reason to keep going.
