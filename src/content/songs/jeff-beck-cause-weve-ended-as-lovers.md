Jeff Beck opens **Cause We've Ended as Lovers** by borrowing someone else's touch. The introduction mimics Roy Buchanan's tone and his trademark volume swells, notes that rise out of silence instead of being struck. The album credit spells out the debts: "dedicated to Roy Buchanan and thanks to Stevie." One name gets the dedication, the guitarist whose sound Beck was chasing. The other gets the thanks, the man who wrote the song.

That second debt has a story behind it. Beck said in 1989 that he was supposed to guest on Stevie Wonder's record in exchange for a song. He said Wonder gave him this one as an apology after "Superstition" came out first, because the record company blocked Beck from having it. The song had already appeared in 1974 on *Stevie Wonder Presents: Syreeta*, the album Wonder made with Syreeta Wright, his wife at the time. So a song about an ending reached Beck as a way of patching things up.

Beck dropped the words. What's left is the title and a guitar trying to say it. He played his "Tele-Gib," a Telecaster fitted with humbucking pickups, and the tone comes out clean and warm, with a little room reverb around it. The lead bends into notes and leans on vibrato the way a singer holds a vowel, wavering where a voice might give out. Underneath, a bass walks at a slow tempo with a gentle swing, and the drums keep time on brushes. Nobody crowds the guitar. The rhythm section gives the melody room to take its time.

That restraint is where the feeling lives. The title is resigned and already in the past tense. It doesn't argue and it doesn't plead, and the playing matches it. The phrases are long and they hang, and nothing is played just to show it can be. It sounds like someone handling a sore spot carefully, keeping their composure while it still hurts. The bends and the vibrato carry more ache than a wail would.

The record around it was a turning point. *Blow by Blow* was Beck's first album credited to him as a solo artist, cut at AIR Studios in London starting in October 1974. George Martin produced it, with Max Middleton on keys, Phil Chen on bass and Richard Bailey on drums. It's mostly instrumental, and it went to No. 4 on the Billboard 200, a jazz-rock fusion record that went platinum. On this track Beck takes a Wonder soul song and puts the guitar in the singer's place, with Buchanan's swells shaping how it speaks.

It sticks because the guitar never says the sad part out loud, so you end up hearing it in your own voice.
