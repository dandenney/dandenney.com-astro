The cruelest line in **To Hell and Back** is a piece of battlefield advice: "Let them fall face down if they must die / Making it easier to say goodbye." It's practical and it's awful. You don't look at the faces of the friends you lose, because you still have to get up and keep fighting. Sabaton tuck that line into a verse about Audie Murphy, one of the most decorated American soldiers of the Second World War, and it tells you more about what war cost him than any medal count could.

The song is built on that split. The chorus is a salute, all charging and attacking, a hero's march into hell and out again. The verses keep catching what the salute leaves out. The opening verse says Murphy hides his emotions, and the "Bright, a white light" section won't quite commit to glory: if war holds any, the song says, let it rest on men like him. The first time through, a parenthetical aside notes that dead men will never come back. The second time, that same spot ends on the men who went to hell and came back. The pride is real, and so is the doubt underneath it.

Then the point of view shifts. Near the end the song speaks as Murphy, calling listeners to gather round while he tells of cannons that still roar along the shore and are "still there when I sleep." Joakim Brodén and Pär Sundström wrote the song drawing on Murphy's own poem, "The Crosses Grow on Anzio," from his book *To Hell and Back*, and it is as much about his post-war PTSD as his combat. The final chorus moves into the past tense. He charged. He went to hell and back. The war is over, but the man is still carrying it.

Musically it's Sabaton doing what the Swedish power metal band is known for, turning history into something a crowd can shout back. A distorted, palm-muted guitar riff drives it, with a low bass line underneath and drums hitting hard on kick and snare in a steady march. High keyboard chords hang over the top. Brodén sings with a clear, strong, slightly raspy voice, declaring more than confiding. The production, by Peter Tägtgren, sounds polished and huge, like a hall built for the chorus.

Brodén described it as heavy metal meeting the spaghetti westerns of Ennio Morricone. That fits a story whose hero came home from the war and became a western movie star. Brodén has visited Murphy's grave at Arlington, and Murphy's son came to a Sabaton show in Los Angeles and gave the band an original poster from the 1955 film *To Hell and Back*.

You come for the fist-raising chorus and stay for the man inside it, a hero being cheered for the very thing that won't let him sleep.
