The plan in **Montana** is half-baked on purpose. The narrator wants to trade his truck for horses when he gets there and tear down every fence he meets, and he admits the logistics are an afterthought: "we work the details later / if we work them out at all." That shrug is the most honest line in the song. It tells you this isn't a relocation plan. It's a way of saying *let's not be here anymore* to someone you hope will say yes.

The trouble with here comes in sideways. Two angels show up in a dream to warn that "this whole town is going under," and later the narrator sounds worn out on the place itself. The old story keeps coming around "again," and he swears one more hearing will make him snap. "We've been beat so down," he says, before adding the brag that they "painted this whole town." Pride and exhaustion sit in the same breath. He had a good time here, and it's done with him. Montana is barely a place in the song. It's a big sky, back roads and no fences.

The phrase he keeps landing on is "Just be happy lonesome and free." It's a strange wish to make to someone you're begging to come along. Lonesome usually means without anyone. Here it means alone together, two people off the map, and "You and me" gets repeated until it reads as a promise he's trying to make true by saying it.

The music doesn't hurry him. Strummed acoustic guitar, a walking bass line and a simple kit beat settle into a relaxed country shuffle at a moderate pace. The sound is dry and direct, with nothing dressed up. Pierce Edens sings in a clear voice with a little rasp at the edges, and his delivery stays conversational, like he's making the pitch across the cab of the truck rather than from a stage.

The backstory adds a twist. Edens grew up on mountain folk in Western North Carolina, then got into punk and grunge as a teenager, and Indy Week heard that punk streak in this song's rebellious, free-spirited pull. The self-titled album it opens was fan-funded on Kickstarter and recorded in his childhood home in the Appalachians, which he bought and turned into a studio. So a song about leaving town for good was cut in the house he grew up in, a contradiction the song leaves alone.

It sticks because it captures the moment just before you leave anywhere, when the escape still feels like freedom and you're still waiting to hear whether they'll come with you.
