**Cursing Concrete** starts with someone swearing at the ground. "I cursed concrete," the song admits, and later the target widens to the streets running under the narrator's feet. It's a small, almost comic picture of anger with nowhere to go: you can shout at pavement all day and it won't move. Rumbleseat take that helplessness seriously. The song never pretends the cursing fixes anything.

What sits underneath is heavier. The narrator talks about wearing out their tongue trying to lift a burden that eats away at them, then asks a question with no answer, where the shame is, from a place pinned down in one hard line: "Shouting from a lot where the kids can't play." That's a dead space, a patch of ground meant for something and used for nothing, and the narrator is standing in it, loud. Then comes the walk back, replaying how things could have gone. Regret and defiance share the same few bars, and neither wins.

The turn is the hope. The wish to leave a mark and "stand strong even when I'm gone" moves the song from a bad night to a whole life, and the song says it outright: before I'm done, before I lay down. The best knot in the lyric is the promise to go off but still be on. That means losing it and staying present at once. It's the voice of someone who knows their own temper and has decided to keep showing up anyway.

The band plays it like a push forward. A strummed acoustic guitar drives at a medium-fast clip, a distorted electric holds long chords over it, the bass walks instead of sitting on the root, and the snare is right up front. The voices come doubled, moving together in unison or close parallel lines, so the confession sounds shared, like a room singing along. The mix is clean, with the electric guitar's grit as its rough edge.

Chuck Ragan wrote it. Rumbleseat was the acoustic side project he and Chris Wollard ran outside Hot Water Music, formed in Gainesville in 1998 with Samantha Jones on bass and vocals, all three singing. Punknews called them "acoustic whiskey-influenced campfire punk rock," which gets the Johnny Cash and Uncle Tupelo lean right. The album's material was recorded at Rob McGregor's Goldentone Studios in Gainesville between 1998 and 1999, and it waited until 2005 to come out as the band's only full-length, an album titled like an obituary. That suits a song so worried about leaving a mark after it's gone.

It sticks because it lets you be furious and hopeful in the same breath, and it hands you a line to shout back at the pavement with a few friends.
