A grieving person usually goes one of two ways: they feel too much, or they shut it all off. **Sweet Jane** is about the second kind, and about watching it happen to someone you love. Garrett Kato's narrator isn't the one in trouble. He's the one standing nearby, noticing that Jane has gone numb. The chorus says her heart is acting like it doesn't want to work right, and that she's in a bad enough place that "Now you tried everything / Just to feel something." There's no scolding in it. It reads like a diagnosis spoken by a friend who has run out of ways to help.

The song never says outright what put her there, but the later verses point to it. Her father has told her that someday he'll be re-made into a spirit living in her mind, which sounds like comfort offered in the face of an ending. Jane won't take it. She denies the reasons anyone lives and dies and settles on a flat fact instead: the river runs straight into the ocean. It's a bleak kind of peace, and the narrator doesn't argue with it. Earlier, the image of the home where she used to stay washing away in the pouring rain does the same quiet work. Something is already gone, and the song is set in the time just after.

What makes it bearable is how gently Kato handles it. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls under everything. Sustained bass notes and a simple kick and snare keep the slow tempo from drifting. Kato's voice is breathy and close, and the harmonies fold in tight around it, so the lines about numbness come out warm instead of cold. The production is clean and intimate. That fits the album's origins: it was cut in Byron Bay, in Kato's garage, his home studio and 301 Studios, recorded and mixed by Anthony Lycenko, with Ben Livermore on drums. Kato spent two years on the record, recording songs over and over and tending to them, he said, like a garden. You can hear that care in how little gets in the way.

Kato has described the album as being about his move from Canada to Australia and letting go of a past life, a personal diary of manhood and letting go. **Sweet Jane** fits that story from an angle. It's a song about someone else's letting go, sung by someone who has done his own. It sits comfortably in the Australia-based singer-songwriter tradition of low-key acoustic folk, unhurried and sure of itself.

The lyric closes on the chorus it has been heading toward all along, and the narrator's best line is the simplest: "Oh, my thoughts are still with you." He can't fix her, and he says so by staying.

**Sweet Jane** sticks because it lets you feel what it's like to love someone through their numbness: you can't reach them, so you keep your voice soft and stay close anyway.
