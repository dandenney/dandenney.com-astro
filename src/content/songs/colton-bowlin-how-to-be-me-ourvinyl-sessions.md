The guitar does two jobs at once on **How to be Me**. A fingerpicked, rolling arpeggio carries the melody while a bass line runs underneath it, so the song brings its own floor and its own motion with nothing else in the room to help. The tempo sits in the middle, unhurried. The voice comes in slightly raspy and mid-range, pitched like someone talking across a kitchen table rather than projecting to the back of a hall. There are no harmonies, no second voice to soften a line or tell you it'll be fine. It's guitar and voice, and that turns out to be the right size for what the song is about.

Colton Bowlin has said the songs on the How to Be Me EP came from a stretch when he was trying to sort out who he is, who he isn't, and who he wants to be, and that the record is about not having all the answers all the time. Holler called the EP a stripped-down set about a young man trying to find his way back to himself, picking through the pieces of a breakup and the weight of regret. Put that next to the title and the feeling comes clear. This isn't a song of arrival. It's the in-between state where you've lost the version of yourself you knew and haven't built the next one yet. The plain delivery fits that. There's no grand vocal move to claim a confidence he's said he doesn't have.

This session version is the opener of a four-song OurVinyl set cut in Nashville. The song's first life was produced by David Barbe in Athens, Georgia, for the three-song EP on State Line Records, and Bowlin named it, along with "Turn the Page," as one of the two songs he was most looking forward to from that record. Here the production is dry and close, with a natural room around it and no polish, so the rasp sits right up front. The relaxed groove keeps the regret from curdling into self-pity. It moves along like a man walking while he thinks.

Bowlin grew up in Albany, Kentucky, on the Tennessee line, working in his grandfather's feed mill and soaking up the old-timers' tall tales that later fed his songs. That puts him in the Appalachian strain of country and Americana, and the comparisons to Tyler Childers and Sturgill Simpson make sense. What stands out here is how far the mood sits from the tall-tale spirit he credits to the mill: the weight is on a young man taking his own measure. The set also includes "Josey Wales," from his 2024 debut, Songs from the Holler, so the session holds more than one side of him at once.

The song sticks because it gives you permission to not be finished, and lets you sit with someone who's honest enough to say so out loud.
