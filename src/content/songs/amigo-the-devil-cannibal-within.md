The joke that lands hardest in **Cannibal Within** is a suicide joke. The verse opens by announcing, almost like a weather report, that it turns out the narrator is depressed. Then it offers a fix: turn on the radio, cock it back. Then comes the caveat: "I just don't wanna blow my brains out to Hotel California." It's funny, and it's meant to be. But just before that, the song told you what the funny is for. Someone has noticed that this person uses jokes and joy to dodge the realization. The narrator hears the diagnosis and answers it with another joke. That's the trap, shown in one move.

The opening verse works the same way. The narrator always figured the great fear would be heartbreak, "Be love related, not fentanyl." There's embarrassment in that line, a life that was supposed to go wrong in a more romantic direction. What follows is the addict's loop told without self-pity: a heart beating off its rhythm, a vow to every god that this is the last time, and then the admission, before anyone else can say it, that everybody knows it's a lie. Shame and plain honesty sit side by side, and neither wins.

Then the chorus does something quietly generous. It trades "I" for "we." The parts we hate pile up "Until we find ourselves with more regret than blood," and the title's cannibal stops being one person's problem. Danny Kiranos, the Miami-born songwriter behind the name, has described a tiny cannibal inside everyone that feeds on doubt, jealousy and self-devaluation and grows until we lose the war. That war turns up again in the title of the album this song leads, his third, *Yours Until the War Is Over*. He told Spin the song began as a meditation on the harmful ways people fight aging. In his word, it started out "pompous" before it became one of the record's most powerful statements on regret.

The sound refuses to match the panic. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through arpeggios over a walking bass. The tempo is slow and steady, and everything is dry and close. Kiranos self-produced it in a backwoods bar turned studio, and that intimacy holds. The voice sits low-to-mid and a little raspy, carrying the melody with no harmony to lean on. This is dark folk and alternative Americana, murder-folk territory, with biggest influences Kiranos is said to share with Tom Waits and Leonard Cohen. Here the killer and the victim are the same person. The verse about the sea and the heart as a lonely hunter lets the jokes fall silent for a moment, and the song is at its bravest there.

It sticks because the punchline and the confession arrive in the same breath, so you laugh first and only then realize you've been shown how someone keeps going one more day.
