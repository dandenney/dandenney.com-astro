**Northern Attitude** opens on small talk. Noah Kahan runs through the questions you ask someone you haven't seen in years: how have you been, have you settled down, are you proud, where are the kids now. Then the verse stops asking and starts summing up. You build a boat, you build a life, and the losses follow in the same flat rhythm, as if they were just the next items on the list. Another stanza finds a person lost and getting high, alone and "Scared to live, scared to die." When the boat stanza comes back, "friends" has turned into "kids," and the portrait has gone darker. It's never clear whether Kahan is describing an old neighbor, a parent, or the life he can see waiting for himself. The song works because it never settles that question.

The chorus is where the feeling breaks open, and it's half apology, half alibi: "Forgive my northern attitude / Oh, I was raised out in the cold." Kahan is asking for patience and making an excuse in the same breath. The coldness is admitted, then blamed on the weather, on a place where the sun doesn't come up until summer. Kahan has called Vermont "one of the loneliest places to live in the world," and said he wanted to write about how that loneliness shapes the way people approach relationships. You can hear it in the chorus's opening worry about getting too close and not being what someone hoped for. That isn't standoffishness. It's fear of being found wanting. In the last chorus the pronoun flips to "If you get too close," and the defense now faces outward, as if the walls have finally gone all the way up.

The sound pushes against all that withdrawal. Acoustic guitar strums under an electric melody line, bass and drums drive at a medium-fast clip, and the mix is full and polished. Kahan's voice is slightly raspy and direct, so the confession lands without cushioning. A "(three, four)" count kicks the second chorus in like a band at a basement show, and the wordless "oh-oh-ah-ah" stretch gives everyone in the room something to sing who can't quite say the rest.

Kahan and Gabe Simon wrote and produced the song together, with Simon covering much of the playing. It opens *Stick Season*, the record Kahan began in his hometown of Strafford, Vermont, in 2020 and called a "love letter to New England." The record became his breakthrough and helped push a wave of folk-pop rooted in small places. Hozier later took the second verse on a duet version and kept Kahan's lyrics.

It sticks because it hands you a big chorus to sing along to, and the thing you're singing is an apology for not knowing how to let anyone in.
