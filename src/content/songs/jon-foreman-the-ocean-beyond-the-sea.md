The most telling line in **The Ocean Beyond The Sea** is the third: "Only the shipwrecked could ever find it." Jon Foreman builds the whole song on that reversal. Each verse names a place past the edge of the known world (an ocean beyond the sea, a forest beyond the trees, a valley above the sky) and then names who can't reach it. The fowler can't find the freed songbird. Humankind's towers can't find the valley. The rich man can't find the highway "beyond where power is bought and sold." It has the shape of a beatitude: comfort is promised to the people whose boats already went down, and kept from the ones with the means to go looking.

The feeling underneath is grief, named outright in the second line, where the far ocean "holds elixir for all who grieve." The song doesn't claim that grief has been healed. The narrator never arrives anywhere. Twice he says "I heard a whisper," and that is as close as he gets. The verses describe a cure, and the narrator reports a rumor of it. That gap between promise and arrival is where the song lives. Then come the paradoxes: "As soft as iron, as safe as lions," and later soft as thunder, cold as fire. Whatever is whispering out there is not gentle in any easy sense. It sounds like comfort that could also break you, and Foreman leaves both readings standing.

The music keeps the scale small so the lyric can be enormous. Fingerpicked acoustic guitar carries the slow, flowing pulse. A bass holds long notes underneath, and a string section lays sustained chords across the melody like a bed. Foreman sings alone, breathy and close, with no harmony to share the weight. Reverb opens the room around him, so the voice sounds near while the space feels wide. The refrain is the line that closes every stanza, "By the ocean beyond the sea," coming back like a tide.

It opens *Departures*, an album Foreman made entirely in 2020, which he called "a year of departures." It was recorded at Melody League Studios in San Diego and produced by Foreman, with additional production from Keith Tutt II and Aaron Redfield. He has compared his band Switchfoot to a megaphone. He said this record feels more like a late-night conversation with a friend, more confessional. That fits. This is the hushed, Christian-inflected singer-songwriter mode of his solo work, a voice telling you where the lost things went.

The song sticks because it doesn't hand you the cure, only the directions, and it trusts that if you are wrecked enough, you'll know the way.
