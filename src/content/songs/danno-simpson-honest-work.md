A bathroom is a strange place to settle a score. **Honest Work** was cut in a cramped apartment bathroom, and the room comes through: a clean, close, natural space where a fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls out bright arpeggios and Danno Simpson sings right on top of them. Guitar and voice sit at the center of it. It sounds like someone telling you a story across a small table.

By Simpson's own description, the story is a "hit piece" on an old boss. That description is the key to the song's feeling, and to its quiet trick. A grudge usually wants volume. Simpson gives it a moderate tempo and an easygoing sway. His mid-range voice has a bit of rasp and gravel, and he delivers it like conversation, not accusation. The anger is in the premise, but the music keeps its temper. That gap is where the song lives: resentment told at a relaxed pace, the calm of someone who has replayed an old injury often enough that it has become a tale. The choice to tell it rather than yell it is what lets a listener lean in instead of backing away.

The song has a backstory. Simpson came out of the Fort Collins DIY punk scene, where, he says, his punk-leaning songs got mistaken for country. He self-produced the *Honest Work* EP, five original Americana songs running about 18 minutes, and wrote the title track himself. He recorded it the year after his arrest and put it on streaming services, through Pothole Records, during the height of the pandemic. The title track then caught on slowly, finding country listeners and passing a million Spotify plays. It remains the biggest song in his catalog, ahead of later work like his 2025 debut album *The Kill Joy*.

His influences run through the Texas songwriter line of Guy Clark, Townes Van Zandt and Steve Earle, alongside the mountain music of the Rockies and Appalachia. He was born in Texas, raised in Georgia, and now lives on Colorado's Front Range. You can hear why people took his songs for country. The fingerpicking and the plain storytelling voice sit squarely in that tradition. What carries over from punk is the target: this is a song with an enemy, and it doesn't pretend otherwise. Jackson Earles plays fiddle on the track.

It sticks because it lets you sit with a grudge without being swallowed by it, and telling the story this calmly becomes its own small revenge.
