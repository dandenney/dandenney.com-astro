The song opens on a pile-up of refusals. Every line of the first verse leans on the same word, *never*, until it stops sounding like a statement and starts sounding like someone talking themselves out of hope. Then the next verse flips to a string of promises to keep showing up, in the dark, when the rivers rise, when the spider bites. That swing between giving up and hanging on is the whole emotional engine of **1000 Years**. Bush and Amy Lee don't pick a side.

The chorus is where the real confession sits. After crediting the other person with everything, saying they set the narrator free, the line turns on a single "but" and lands here: "I love you best when there are no fears." The wish to sleep for a thousand years is tender and sad at once. It's a love that only feels safe when both people are unconscious, when nobody can be hurt or disappoint. The gratitude is real, and so is the exhaustion underneath it, and the song lets both stand.

The verses get darker in small ways. The slide into "the ones you lost," the monkey on your back: these hint at dependence, at something both people keep reaching for. Even the chorus wobbles. The first time it's "you were all to me," the second time "all for me," a one-word shift from devotion to something closer to sacrifice.

The sound keeps all that under a slow, heavy calm. A clean electric guitar picks out arpeggiated chords, and long, low bass notes sit beneath them like a floor you can feel more than hear. The lead voice is breathy and a little strained, holding its notes long. A second voice floats above it, breathy too, so the two seem to be leaning on each other rather than trading lines. A kick drum keeps a quiet pulse. The production is polished and roomy, with plenty of air around every part, which makes the closeness in the lyrics feel more fragile.

Later the writing goes plain: up, down, in, out, then "We're sinking, slowly sinking." Near the close of the lyrics comes the strangest, most private line: "I don't want you to see what this TV says to me." It's a small, embarrassed admission, the narrator hiding even their distractions, and it leaves the sinking unresolved.

It sticks because it gives you a love so worn out that the kindest thing either person can imagine is rest, and it sings that wish gently enough that you understand why.
