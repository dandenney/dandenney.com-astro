The narrator of **68 (Live)** takes the wheel because his mother is falling asleep driving down 68, and he does it "just to get us back safe." That is the first scene of the song, and Nolan Taylor sets it down plainly. The verse opens by crediting her with his first cigarettes, then puts him in the driver's seat as she drifts off. The song never calls this neglect. It doesn't have to.

Taylor wrote it alone. He grew up in Blanchester, Ohio, raised by his father and older brother while his mother struggled with mental health and addiction, and he has said the song comes out of that painful relationship. The second verse is built from small, exact details of a house running on empty. A school bus he doesn't want to leave. Three days and he hasn't had much. And one line that does the work of a whole paragraph: "Oven was heating the house if our electric was on." The "if" carries it. The kid isn't complaining. He is reporting how things worked.

Then the chorus turns, and the voice of the song seems to shift from that child to the adult he became. "I'm tired of being nice" admits something before it threatens anything: he has been nice for a long time, probably longer than he should have been. The sharpest line is "You love me when you need a high," a whole history of being wanted for the wrong reasons, followed by "I'm done spending my money on you." The grief and the anger sit right next to each other and never settle.

The third verse gives the song its saddest kind of relief. When dad was off, they'd drive around "listening to tunes," because they had peace of mind if she couldn't find them. The car from the first verse becomes the getaway. "My whole life I've been running from you" ties the two together.

Musically, the setting stays simple. A steady acoustic guitar carries it at a slow, unhurried pace. Taylor sings in a clear, mid-range voice with no harmonies and nothing dressed up. The delivery is conversational, closer to someone telling you about it than to a performance. The room sounds raw, and you can hear it, which suits a song this unguarded. It belongs to the confessional alt-country of Tyler Childers, Sturgill Simpson and Zach Bryan, where the plainest line usually lands hardest. This live version came out in 2021, before the studio version in 2023, and it already sounds finished.

Look at how the lyric ends. "I'm through with you" comes back over and over, as if saying it once isn't enough to make it true.

The song puts you in the passenger seat beside a child who had to be the adult, and then makes you watch that child, grown up, still trying to say goodbye.
