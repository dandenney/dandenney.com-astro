The most telling moment in **I Guess** comes in Arabic. Saint Levant switches out of English to tell a lover, again and again, to take it easy and that he understands her. Then he raises the stakes and swears it on his mother's life. In the English verses he is sarcastic and guarded. When he reaches for his mother tongue, the defenses drop and he simply pleads to be believed.

The hook, which Playyard sings, sets up the hurt. Someone is out "living it up" and still thinking of the two of them in the club. She once said it was never enough. The reply is a shrug with a bruise under it: "I guess, that I'on't know shit." It's pride talking, conceding the argument so he doesn't have to admit how much it stings. Rolling Stone describes the song as a man questioning whether his lover really knows him. Saint Levant called it a bedroom song, and the verses are frank about desire. But the ache keeps showing through. He says he has a lot on his mind and needs to share the pain. Twice he says he doesn't think she really wants him, and each time he leaves a door open after it.

Then comes the line that widens the frame: "I was 17 when I left home." That's Saint Levant's own story. He was born in Jerusalem and left for the U.S. at 17, and he pictures himself setting out in Timberlands and headphones. All of a sudden the doubt about being known runs deeper than one relationship. Here is someone far from where he started, wondering if anyone has the full picture.

The track itself is spare and close. A synth bass and a dry drum machine with a firm snare hold a laid-back, unhurried groove. A short plucked melodic figure repeats on top, and the track carries a lead guitar riff, courtesy of Playyard (producer Henry Morris, his college friend) and Saint Levant. Nothing is soaked in reverb, so the rapping sounds conversational, almost spoken across a pillow. Morris and Saint Levant wrote the bass line first. Saint Levant's father then suggested sampling Timbaland and Elton John's "2 Man Show," and the lyric about a "regular two-man show" reads like a wink back at it: two friends making the most of a small setup.

It's easy to see why Rolling Stone heard Brent Faiyaz in it. The song also comes from someone who started listening to Arab rappers like Shabjdeed and Marwan Moussa in his teens. That background helps explain why the frankness about sex and pain doesn't seem forced. He's speaking plainly about the things that usually go unsaid.

What sticks is how the song flips between two languages, a shrug in English and an oath in Arabic, so you can hear how badly he needs to be understood.
