"Take your time she's only burning." A photographer's instruction, said as calmly as a note about lighting, and it's the coldest line in **Baby's On Fire**. Brian Eno builds the song around a woman in flames and a crowd that treats her as a subject. Laughing boys wait for photos. Rescuers are told to talk about something else. The wind is asked to help the fire along. Nobody reaches for her. The cruelty isn't the fire itself. It's how ordinary everyone stays around it.

Eno once said the song starts out sounding sinister but uses very ordinary words, sung with an incredible amount of passion. He said he picks words for their vowels and their aggression more than their meaning, and joked it could just as well have been "Baby's on hire." Even taken at his word, the attitude comes through in the sound of the words. Nursery-rhyme doubling falls on the rowers, the wind and the snipping photographers, playground chants for a scene of someone being hurt. Then comes the surreal detour to Juanita and Juan, who sell second-hand tobacco while Juan pockets whatever is left in the ashtrays. It widens the frame to a world where everyone is scavenging something.

He wrote it right after leaving Roxy Music, and one writer has called it bitterly cynical. The narrator's own wound flickers through in "I could be half the man I used to." Spite and self-pity share that line, and the song doesn't sort them out. The final verse shrugs at the whole spectacle: "But any idiot would know that."

The band plays it straight, which is what makes it unnerving. A drum kit drives a steady beat with the snare out front. John Wetton's bass repeats a simple line. A distorted guitar holds sustained chords while a high synth pad shimmers overhead. The room sounds raw and a little distant. Eno's voice is clear, slightly nasal and detached, with no harmonies to soften it. The track was recorded in September 1973 at Majestic Studios in Clapham, with Paul Rudolph on guitar and Simon King and Marty Simon also credited. It belongs to Eno's art-rock and experimental pop stretch between Roxy Music and the ambient records.

Then there's Robert Fripp. His solo runs about three minutes over shifting drum accents and keeps going straight through Eno's return to the mic, refusing to follow the beat. Fripp said he played it fresh off a plane from America, exhausted. Surrounded by bystanders who can't be bothered, the one player who won't fall in line ends up as the song's only real disturbance.

The groove makes you one of the onlookers, nodding along while someone burns, and the small guilt of enjoying it is exactly what keeps pulling you back.
