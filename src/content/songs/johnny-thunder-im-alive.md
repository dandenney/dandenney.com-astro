Johnny Thunder opens his list of negatives with "I'm no stone," and the backing voices answer that he's alive. Then he says he's no rock, no piece of metal. It's a strange list to have to make. Nobody needs to announce they aren't made of metal unless someone has been treating them that way, and that's the pressure under all the joy of **I'm Alive**. The song is a shout of being here, and it's also a case being argued.

The song came from Tommy James and Peter Lucia of Tommy James and the Shondells, and James is credited with the arrangement and "musical concept" on Thunder's 1969 single for Calla, a small New York label. Thunder was six years past "Loop de Loop," the 1963 Teddy Vann production that reached No. 4 on the Billboard Hot 100. This was a different animal. Thunder later told the writer Wayne Jancik it was his first true rock effort, and you can hear him leaning into the change. The voice is clear but carries a raw edge, pushed forward and full of energy. The band drives at a medium-fast clip: a prominent snare, a bass walking underneath, an electric guitar playing rhythmic chords, and a keyboard holding long ones that keep the whole thing warm. Backing singers double and harmonize with the lead, so the claim never sounds like one person alone. It sits where garage rock, soul and funk-rock meet.

The lyrics are mostly one phrase, repeated until it turns from statement into chant. In between, they slip in the reasons it needs saying. "Who cares if my hair is a little bit long" waves off a scold. The line about being red and yellow and black and tan stretches personhood across a list of colors. And then, a few lines after he's settled into doing his things, comes the tell: "You better believe me." That's not the voice of someone sure of being believed. The pride and the plea sit in the same breath, and the song never settles which one is winning. It just says the word again.

Thunder cited Bob Dylan's remark in Rolling Stone in 1969, in an interview with Jann Wenner, that he'd heard "I'm Alive" on the radio and counted it among the most powerful records he'd ever heard. The single still didn't sell enough to keep Thunder's career going. That makes the lines about doing his thing and singing his song land a little harder now. The record insists on a life the market didn't make room for.

By the time the refrain has piled up on itself, it has stopped being information and become breathing, and you end up chanting it along with him, half celebrating and half making sure.
