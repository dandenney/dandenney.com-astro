"Fit me in your pocket" is a strange way to open a love song, and a telling one. The narrator of **calico** isn't asking to be held or chosen. The ask is to be made small enough to carry. Alec Duckart, who records as Searows, builds the whole song around that kind of offer: affection that keeps shrinking itself so it won't be too much.

The chorus makes the bargain plain. "I'll be anything you turn me into," Duckart sings, then promises to be the other person's favorite song. It sounds generous, and it is. It's also a little frightening, a self handed over to be reshaped. The lines next to it are about shelter, a feeling you come home to, a place to rest "all you've been standing on." Care and self-erasure sit in the same breath, and the song never sorts out which one it means more.

The verses run on maybes. Maybe this was an answer, maybe it's the end of the good part, "or maybe it's not." The hedging is the confession. Someone braced for loss learns to say everything twice, once hopeful and once guarded. The second verse drifts into a sunny afternoon, an offer of tea, a dance to "Tombstone Blues," and "run away with me," the boldest line in the song, tucked between small domestic pictures as if it might pass unnoticed. Then comes the regret: "Maybe I was letting go too quick." The narrator wants another chance and isn't sure who gave up first.

The sound matches the shyness. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through clean, bright arpeggios at a slow, easy pace, and Duckart's voice is soft and breathy, sung plainly with no harmony to lean on. The recording is dry and close, the kind of small-room intimacy that puts the singer right next to you. Duckart also plays bass and programs the drums. Jonathan Pearce, guitarist of The Beths, co-produced and mixed it and is credited with Wurlitzer, synthesizer, banjo and reed organ. The lyric closes on a run of wordless "mm"s, as if the narrator has said as much as can be risked.

It sits squarely in Portland's confessional indie folk. It's one of five songs on the flush EP, which Duckart wrote over about two years and released because he still cared about them, even knowing they wouldn't fit his next album. That fits a song this unwilling to let go.

What sticks is the offer at its center, and you feel both halves of it: how sweet it is to be wanted that badly, and how much the narrator is willing to give up to stay.
