The whole song turns on a request that sounds like a command until you hear the last word. "Make me bow down / To it please," goes the end of each chant. That "please" gives it away. The narrator has spent the verses sounding like the most powerful person in the room. Here the voice asks to be overpowered, and it's polite about it.

**Bow Down** is track nine on SPINALL's sixth album, *TOP BOY*. Spinall produced it alone and wrote it with Amaarae. He has said the album's title honors people who beat the odds with whatever they had on hand. The song's opening fits that brief in its own way. It asks for money in naira, cedi and dollar, three currencies in a row, and it wants more, not less. Loving and enjoyment are named as the reason for being here. It's a pan-African wish list, delivered with the ease of someone who expects to get it.

Then the song slides from wealth into worship. The narrator claims to be the holy spirit to the godless and warns off anyone trying "witchy witchy" tricks. A lover tells the narrator to whine, and the moment flips the religious language around: "Feels like Gods giving praise to me." The narrator isn't the one praying. The narrator is the one being prayed to. A few lines later comes the admission of being lost in the sauce and lost in thought, a slave to the very thing being commanded. Pride and surrender sit in the same stanza, and the song never chooses between them.

The other verse is pure play: a Bee Gees joke, Fiji Water as a swimming pool, a wink that none of this stays PG. iMullar praised Amaarae's performance, said a second verse marks Amaarae as one of the voices of alternative music, and named the track a standout. African Folder heard Spinall nodding to the alté scene by bringing in the Ghanaian singer. Afrocritik was cooler, calling the track slightly disappointing and saying the sparse instrumentals didn't quite match Amaarae's presence. iMullar heard trumpet horns in the production.

That disagreement is part of the appeal. This is a DJ's record that leaves room for a guest to take it over, and Amaarae does. At two minutes it's a quick visit, two verses and a chant, with no time to explain itself.

What sticks is that "please": a voice sure enough of its own divinity to beg for someone who can bring it to its knees, and you end up wanting that too.
