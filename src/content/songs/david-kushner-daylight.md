The confession in **Daylight** is that confessing doesn't work. David Kushner writes from inside a habit he knows is wrong, and the chorus gives the whole problem away in its first line: "Oh, I love it and I hate it at the same time." It's not a riddle and he isn't trying to solve it. It's a plain statement of where he stands, sung over and over until the repetition starts to feel like the trap itself.

That feeling arrived fast. Kushner wrote the chorus in a Los Angeles studio in January 2023 while taking a break from a different song. He sang the melody first in falsetto, a first pass he called a "vomit vocal." The pre-chorus came a couple of days later, and the rest a week after that, making it one of the fastest songs he's written. Producer Rob Kirwan was captivated by the demo and wanted to produce it. He handled programming, drums and synthesizer, with co-writer Hayden Hubers on piano and Rick Hornby on guitar.

The lyric never lets the narrator be alone with the guilt. The first verse describes two people sharing a lust "that we both share," souls tied by pride as much as by guilt, and the chorus keeps the pair together: "You and I." Kushner has said the line "You and I drink the poison from the same vine" holds the whole concept, and that it tells the world we're all walking through darkness together. That's the generous reading. The darker one is in the song too: company makes the habit easier to keep.

The second half turns toward prayer. "Lord" shows up, and the narrator is on his knees asking for mercy he isn't sure he's earned. The best image is the simplest: "Try to follow your light, but it's nighttime." It's an excuse and a plea at once, and he means both. Watch the pre-chorus, too. The first time, he can't resist the darkness. The second time, after begging forgiveness, he says he might. It's a small word change, and it's as close to hope as the song gets. Then the chorus comes back twice more, and he's running from the daylight again.

The lineage is easy to hear in the writing: church language put to work on desire, in a gothic-tinged pop ballad that critics and fans set beside Hozier and Bon Iver. Kushner cites Hozier and Lana Del Rey as influences, and the song went viral after he tagged Hozier in teaser posts on TikTok and Instagram and fans picked up the comparison. It debuted at No. 48 on the Billboard Hot 100 and later appeared on his debut album, The Dichotomy, a title that could have been this chorus.

It sticks because it doesn't pretend the wanting will stop, and you recognize that honest, stuck place where you know better and go back anyway.
