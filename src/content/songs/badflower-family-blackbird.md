The math in **Family (Blackbird)** is brutal and simple: "First 15 years I'm the favourite son," Josh Katz sings, and then the second half of a thirty-year life arrives as its mirror image, the hated one. There's no event in between, no single fight that explains the turn. That's the most honest thing about the song. Estrangement rarely has a date. It accumulates, one missed call and one unsent birthday card at a time, until a person looks up and can't find the way back.

Katz has said he spent years trying to understand why he pushes away the people he cares about most, and why being with his family as an adult feels so uncomfortable. The lyrics don't solve that. They circle it. The narrator is drunk and missing home, dreaming of a version where "everyone still likes me," and the confession comes in a flat, damning list: he made his sisters cry, he calls himself a shitty brother, nobody's friend. Then comes the line that holds the whole ache: "I never say 'I love you' even though I want to." Wanting and doing have come apart, and he can feel the gap without being able to close it.

What keeps the song from becoming pure self-flagellation is that it won't stay in one emotional key. Guilt keeps sliding into grievance. "This is all my fault" sits a few lines from "Is everyone against me?" Later the narrator goes further, scoffing that "we" only say "I love you" because that's what we're supposed to, then insisting, "And most families lie, but I meant it every time." That contradicts what he said earlier, and the song lets it stand. Self-hatred and self-defense live in the same body here. Anyone who has been the difficult one at the holiday table will recognize both.

This is the orchestral reworking of the lead single from *This Is How the World Ends*, and it pulls Badflower well away from their Los Angeles hard-rock swagger. A clean electric guitar picks out arpeggiated chords over bass and a steady, unhurried drum kit. The production is polished and open, with room around each instrument. Katz's voice is a little raspy, held long and mournful in the middle of his range, and a quiet harmony slips in behind him on certain words, as if someone else were briefly carrying the weight. The repeated cry of "What happened to this family?" lands harder at this pace. There's nowhere for it to hide.

Katz has said the song is hard to sing live. He also said that after writing it, he sent it to his family and started having the conversations the narrator can't have. The song reads like a bridge that got built while its writer was still working out how to cross it.

It sticks because it says the thing you've been too proud or too scared to say to your own people, and it lets you hear how much it costs to say it.
