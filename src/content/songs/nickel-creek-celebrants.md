"My God, it's good to see you" is how **Celebrants** begins, and it sounds less like a lyric than the first thing out of someone's mouth at the door. Nickel Creek put that greeting at the top of their fifth album, their first record since 2014's *A Dotted Line*. It was shaped by the pandemic shutdown and by something like 75 or 80 days of writing together before a month in the studio. The song is a reunion. What makes it sting is that it's also an apology.

The chorus raises a glass to the gatherings and then admits that everyone took them for granted. The relief and the shame arrive in the same breath and never settle each other. A later verse goes further and becomes a real confession: "That was me preaching communion / Even as I prayed for space." The narrator wanted solitude, got more of it than anyone asked for, and has about lost the taste for it. That's a hard thing to say out loud at a party. The song doesn't tidy it into a lesson. Even the homecoming takes effort. It takes a couple of cocktails just to pick up the threads, the slightly awkward drinks it takes for old friends to find each other again.

The band plays it like the feeling it describes. Acoustic guitar strums at a relaxed, steady clip over a walking bass line, and the drums keep a simple beat. The mandolin plays melodic lines, and a fiddle holds long, sustained notes. The lead voice is clear and conversational. Other voices fold in, one of them on a higher harmony, until the sound is a blended crowd. That's the lyric's point made in sound: one person's confession, held up by a room of people singing along. Eric Valentine recorded the album at RCA Studio A in Nashville, and Sean Watkins said Valentine placed microphones everywhere, some 30 to 40 feet behind the band. You can hear the room breathe around them.

This is progressive acoustic bluegrass, the string-band language the trio built their name on, leaning toward Americana. The real move is in the closing image. Heaven has always been in this cathedral, the song says, the one everyone rebuilds nightly together. It isn't a church you inherit. It's one you have to put back up every time you show up for each other.

It sticks because it lets you feel two things at once: the joy of walking back into a room full of people you love, and the quiet guilt of knowing you didn't love it enough the first time.
