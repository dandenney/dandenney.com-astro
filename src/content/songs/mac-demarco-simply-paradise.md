Near the end of **Simply Paradise**, the song stops pretending to be a song and turns into two guys talking. One of them leans on Madonna for authority: "Italian do it better," Madonna said. The other comes back half-convinced and lets her have the point. It's a throwaway, and it's the best moment on the record, because it gives away what the whole thing is really about. This is a love song about a woman neither singer seems to know, sung by two men who clearly like each other a lot.

The setup is borrowed on purpose. Ryan Paris has said he and Mac DeMarco got the idea of doing it like Michael Jackson and Paul McCartney's "The Girl Is Mine," and the song keeps that duet's habit of two voices trading lines. The boasts are cartoonish. "Don't try, don't try to have her," one warns. The other gets told "your love is ordinary," the kind of insult you only throw at a friend. That's the emotional trick. Every line is possessive, but the feeling underneath is warmth, maybe even gratitude. The rivalry is just the excuse to stand at the same mic.

The backstory makes that warmth easy to believe. In his 2014 Pitchfork documentary *Pepperoni Playboy*, DeMarco praised Paris's 1983 Italo-disco hit "Dolce Vita" as an inspiration and danced down the street to it. Paris saw the video and invited him to do a remix together. DeMarco replied in a 2016 video that he was interested, and the two didn't work together until 2023. That September, Paris had him out to his home by the beach outside Rome. The song and its video came out of three nights there, and Paris says they finished the song in a few hours. Kiera McNally, DeMarco's longtime partner, shot the clip. Under the swagger, you can hear a fan who finally got the call.

The band doesn't hurry any of this. A clean electric guitar picks out arpeggiated chords, the bass walks a simple line, and the drums swing easy at a moderate tempo, with the snare pushed forward. The vocal is clear and mid-range, smooth and in no hurry, with a little reverb around it, and the production stays polished. The Fader called it bittersweet, crushed-velvet R&B, and that fits. Nothing in the groove is fighting over anybody.

The lyrics are thin, and some lines barely make sense. The song doesn't hide that. It's built from yeahs and the two-word title, and that loose, half-finished quality is part of the charm.

What sticks is the gap between what the song says and what it means: two men insist the woman is theirs, and you end up hearing a long-delayed friendship finally getting its turn.
