By the last verse of **Mexico**, the narrator has been listing his baby's loves for so long that it takes a moment to notice he's finally made the list himself. "My baby loves my money and Mexico," Gill Landry sings, and there it is: the only place he shows up in her world is his wallet, filed next to a country she'd rather be in.

The whole song runs on that list. Every verse stacks up what she loves, and every verse lands on the same flat refrain: "But my baby she don't love me no more." The "my baby" lament sits comfortably in blues-inflected folk, but Landry fills it with a Louisiana grab bag of charms and vices. She loves a talisman and a holy ghost, moonshine, stolen pearls, the burglar's wine. She also loves "lotus swamp banjos under cypress groves," which isn't an accusation at all. It's a picture he clearly loves too.

That mix is where the song gets interesting. Half the list reads like a case against her: addiction, a dungeon, a cop with a brutal touch. The other half is tender, blood red roses and December snow. The narrator can't decide whether he's indicting her or still admiring her, and the song never makes him choose. Knowing someone this well, down to the strange things that move them, and getting nothing back is its own kind of hurt. The money line stings because it's the one place he lets the bitterness show plainly.

The band plays it easy, which makes the bruise sit lower. An acoustic guitar keeps a steady rhythm with the bass line folded in, and a second acoustic picks out a counter-melody. Brushes push a relaxed shuffle at a moderate walk. A steel guitar holds long notes over the top, the closest the arrangement gets to crying. Landry's voice is a little raspy and pitched like conversation, with no harmonies to soften it. The room sounds open and warm.

The song comes from Landry's solo debut, recorded in 2006 and produced with Nick Jaina. Jason Powers engineered this track and Adam Selzer mixed it. Landry is a Louisiana-born former street busker who played with Old Crow Medicine Show, and the songs grew out of, among other things, a hard breakup with a one-time fiancée. "Mexico" doesn't need the backstory, but it fits the tone of someone taking stock of a steady thing that's gone.

What keeps you coming back is that refrain doing double duty, as every catalog of her loves turns into a quiet count of what the narrator never got to be.
