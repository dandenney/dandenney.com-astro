The fireflies show up first, keeping the narrator company for a smoke after dark, and that small, almost sweet image is the last gentle thing in **Midnight Demon Club** before the self-accounting starts. Then comes the chorus, which says the quiet part out loud: "I-I hate myself / But I'm loving this." Both halves are meant. The song doesn't ask you to pick one.

That's the feeling Highly Suspect build the track around: the odd pleasure of a bad night spent alone with your worst opinions of yourself. The sharpest line is "But I'm so good at judging it." It catches someone who can't fix their life but has become an expert critic of it, and who knows that's a dodge. The verses keep turning the knife on themselves. The narrator wishes someone could have been more than a friend, calls themselves a piece of shit, then adds that at least they understand it. Self-awareness becomes a kind of armor. When the narrator tells everyone else how great they are, it sounds less like praise than like a wall going up. Envy, longing and contempt sit in the same breath, and none of them wins. By the last stretch, "loving this" has slid into feeling good about yourself while the demons are out. The narrator never fixes anything. They just find the night more comfortable than the day.

The band plays it straight and loud. The drums drive a steady, fast beat with a prominent snare and crashing cymbals, the bass digs a low line underneath, and the guitar grinds out a distorted riff with some sustained chords. The voice is raw and a little raspy, full-throated, with some shouts mixed in. The production keeps the grit in, so the whole thing sounds thick and unscrubbed, which suits a song about not cleaning yourself up.

Drew Fulk co-wrote it with Johnny Stevens, Rich Meyer and Ryan Meyer, and co-produced it with the band. It's the title track of the album Stevens described as a second chance at life, tied to the death of a loved one, a record about having fun because life is too short. In that light, "loving this" reads as a choice to enjoy the wreckage rather than drown in it. The writing can be blunt to a fault, and the demon chorus gets a little muddled in its middle lines. But the bluntness is also the point. Nobody talks to themselves in polished sentences at 2 a.m.

The song sticks because it lets you admit that tearing yourself apart can feel good, and it doesn't make you feel worse for liking it.
