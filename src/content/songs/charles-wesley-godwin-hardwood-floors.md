The narrator of **Hardwood Floors** doesn't ask for much. Stay downstairs, watch the blue dress come down the steps, then take a hand and dance. Charles Wesley Godwin has said the song was inspired by a date night with his wife, and that it's about living in the moment. The song is that, but the moment is hard-won. Before the first chorus arrives, the narrator admits "it seems like we're always fightin' time," and the only thing keeping hope alive on the bad nights is a thought carried "across state lines."

That's the feeling under all the motion: a dance as a defense against distance. The chorus shows it best. "And pretend there ain't nobody else in town," the narrator sings, and then, a line later, the pretending drops away. There ain't nobody else around. The wish turns into a fact just by being said again, and the refrain keeps repeating until it sounds like a vow. In the second verse the narrator lets slip how rare this is: "for once my blues have past." *For once.* The joy here is real, but it's the joy of someone who knows the blues are usually waiting. When a partner's eyes go "reading what lies behind," the answer is a gentle dodge: it's just love, don't you mind. Tenderness and something unspoken share the same breath, and the song doesn't sort them out.

The band keeps it moving so the worry never settles. Acoustic guitar strums steadily over a walking bass line and a shuffle beat with a firm snare backbeat, at an easy middle tempo built for two people on a hardwood floor. A fiddle carries a prominent melodic line. Godwin sings alone, with no harmonies, in a clear voice with a little rasp at the edges, delivered plainly, like he's talking to the person in front of him. The recording, produced and mixed by Al Torrence at Music Garden Studios, sounds clean, with a natural room around the instruments.

Americana Highways called it a honky tonk number, and that fits. Godwin also credited Ryan Bingham's work as an influence on its sound, and said it took a while to get the song where he wanted it. It sits on *Seneca*, his debut solo album, which he calls the "autobiography of an Appalachian boy," written entirely by him while the Morgantown, West Virginia native was living in Ohio. Knowing that, the state lines in the first verse carry a bit more weight. The song went on to become a success on streaming, and it isn't hard to see why.

The song catches you in that small, defiant decision to treat one evening as the whole world, made by someone who knows exactly how rarely that works.
