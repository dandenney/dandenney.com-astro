"Made a buck off my soul" is a strange thing to confess in a love song, and **i am not who i was** puts it in the second line. Chance Peña doesn't dress it up. The verse lists the facts of a touring life (the road, the crowds, the stories sung for money, the long stretch since home) like someone emptying their pockets onto a kitchen table before a hard conversation.

The conversation is the chorus, and it's built as a pair of questions. If I fly too far, will I still have a place with you? And then the title, turned into a plea: "Will you love me for who I am, not who I was?" It's a reasonable thing to ask and an unfair one, both at once. The narrator wants to be accepted as changed, but he's the one who left, and the person at home never agreed to the new version. The song lets both of those sit. It never decides whether he's owed that love or just hoping for it.

What keeps it from turning self-pitying is how much tenderness he gives the other side. The second verse admits the absence is hard on them and that he's counting on them not losing faith. He also insists, almost defensively, that he meant it when he promised to come back. Then there's the line about fame: the spotlight "Could never drown you out." That's a compliment and a bit of self-reassurance, the kind of thing you say partly so you'll believe it. At the very end the title question comes back once more, alone on its own line, and that's where the doubt finally shows.

The sound matches the plainness. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through steady arpeggios. A slightly breathy lead sits right up front, and a second voice shadows it, often a third above. It's clean and dry, one guitar and two voices with nothing to hide behind, the way a letter written from a motel would be.

Peña wrote it with Hayd and Jeremy Fedryk. He has said it came out of a session for other songs, when Hayd reacted to something he was playing, then came back and they started humming ideas. Peña produced it himself, and he has said he produces and mixes his own music partly out of necessity. The song lands in folk-leaning singer-songwriter territory, and it later became the fifth track on his debut album, *Ever-Shifting, Continual Blossoming*, which Atwood Magazine called soul-stirring folk.

It sticks because it asks the question everyone who has outgrown a place is afraid to say out loud, and then leaves you waiting with the narrator for an answer that never comes.
