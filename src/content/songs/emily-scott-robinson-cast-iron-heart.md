In **Cast Iron Heart**, the whole argument rides on a skillet. Left out in the rain, rusted, written off, it comes back with a little love and shines like new. Emily Scott Robinson builds a love song on that image, and she never strains it. "Got a lot of salt and seasoning," goes one line, and it works both ways at once. It is cookware, and it is a person admitting they've been through things.

Robinson has said she wrote it to celebrate love that aging and loss have changed, a pushback against all the songs about young love that hasn't been tested yet. You can hear the pushback in the lyric. The line about people thinking this couple is past its prime gets answered right away with the claim that they arrived just in time. That's pride, and a little defiance. But the bridge drops the guard: live long enough and your heart breaks a thousand times, so why do it alone? The song offers companionship the way someone offers it after they've learned what being alone costs.

The tenderness is in the chores. There's coffee made strong at dawn, butter in the pan, a kiss for calloused hands. Then comes the most loving detail in the song, one most love songs would edit out: the lines around a loved one's hazel eyes. Wrinkles get praised right beside a magnolia bloom. The song doesn't flinch from age. It looks right at it.

It doesn't settle every doubt, and that's to its credit. "Maybe we're a vestige of the past," goes one line, and then, "Maybe we're just built to last." Both maybes stay on the table. The chorus, a love that deepens as every season comes back around, keeps repeating like a vow said until it holds. The song returns to its first verse at the close too, rust and all, coming back around itself.

The sound stays out of the way. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through slow arpeggios. Brushed drums and a simple bass line keep a relaxed sway, and the room sounds natural. The lead voice is slightly nasal and conversational, with a shade of melancholy. A second voice settles in beside it in harmony. Robinson has said John Paul White's voice carries depth and gravitas, and the pairing does what a duet like this needs: two weathered people singing the same promise. Josh Kaufman, who has worked with Bonny Light Horseman and Anaïs Mitchell, produced the album. It's a fitting focus track for a record on the label John Prine founded.

It makes you look at the scuffed, used-up things in your own life, a pan or a face, and see the years of care in them instead of the damage.
