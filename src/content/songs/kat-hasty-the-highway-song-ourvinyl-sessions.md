Late in **The Highway Song**, Kat Hasty lists the ways an old love keeps coming back: a sleepless night, a cocaine habit, and a heartache song. "It comes in the form of a heartache song," the narrator admits, and the song quietly confesses that it is its own symptom. That's a sly bit of writing, and this OurVinyl session version makes it plain. With one voice and a fingerpicked guitar in the room, the admission has nowhere to hide.

The story starts at 17, with a man who didn't love the narrator back. Everything after is an attempt to fill the space he left, mostly by breaking other people's hearts. The chorus is where the self-justification lives. There's a nameless highway, Mary Jane, one bummed cigarette that turned into smoking like a train, and a man from Oklahoma who got told he was loved. Then the shrug: "But I was born for leaving." It sounds like fate, but the verses have already shown it's a wound wearing fate's clothes. The cigarette detail does the same work in miniature. One casual borrow becomes a habit, and the leaving seems to work the same way.

The verse after the first chorus is the most tender stretch. Tired and alone, the narrator looks at the first man's pictures and decides he's better off by himself. Pride, grief, and a kind of generosity all sit in that decision, and none of them wins. He is still "everything that I have ever known," which is a devastating thing to say about someone who never loved you back.

Hasty wrote the song in her head on a drive to Fort Worth. She started singing a line while driving and had the whole thing by the time she stopped for gas and food, then typed it into her phone. She's called it probably her favorite of her songs, and the one she screams in the car. Here it isn't screamed. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar walks a steady bass line at a slow, flowing pace. Hasty's voice is clear and a little breathy, with no harmonies, and the sound is dry, in a room that feels small and close. The delivery is direct, almost plain, which suits a narrator too worn out to dramatize.

It's West Texas songwriting, and Hasty's sound has been described as a mix of Bob Dylan, Miranda Lambert inspiration, and the West Texas lifestyle. You can hear the folk-rock storyteller in the way a pile of small details carries the feeling. The song closes by repeating that vow of leaving, and by then it no longer sounds like a boast.

The song sticks because it lets you hear someone call running away a destiny while every other line shows it's still the same heartbreak from 17.
