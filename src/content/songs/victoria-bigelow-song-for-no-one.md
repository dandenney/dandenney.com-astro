Each verse of **Song for No One** ends on the same word, and each time the word lands differently. First it's a retort. The partner says it's all in your head, baby, and the narrator answers that this is the problem. Then it turns defensive, a shrug that dares you to object. By the third verse it has become a release. "Now I'm not your problem," Victoria Bigelow sings, and the line carries more feeling than its few words should hold.

What comes before it is a portrait of someone shrinking to fit another person. The song opens with a wish to feel what a partner feels when stoned, and an admission that the narrator has never been where the partner goes. One hit is enough to convince the narrator that everyone is bored of them. The second verse holds the sharpest image: "You told me I am more fun with a drink in my hand." The answer is to drink as much as possible, a self-erasure that sounds like the obvious thing to do. Bigelow doesn't ask you to judge either person. She sets out the anger, the depression, the eagerness to please, and lets you sit inside them.

Then the relief. The narrator says they've never felt such relief as the day the other person turned away, and the song doesn't argue with that. But relief isn't the last word. The song skips a traditional chorus: three verses build to an outro that does the chorus's job. That outro is a vow, "I'll never open myself up like this again," repeated until it sounds less like a decision than a spell. Between the repetitions come memories trailing the narrator through the streets and a new touch that registers as nothing at all. Freedom and numbness share the same breath, and neither cancels the other.

Bigelow's music takes cues from the desert and Mazzy Star. The desert is literal. She has said the songs on the EP this track later joined began after she left Nashville, a city she disliked, and that she wrote them after working through some grief. They arrived within days of each other. She and her partner, Devan Skaggs, have made EPs together in their home studio. She has named Aimee Mann and Alanis Morissette as early influences, and you can hear that lineage in the writing's pointed turns, as when a single word gets weighed three different ways.

Bigelow has said a song for no one is a song for everyone. That fits a track about being told you're more fun with a drink in your hand, then finding yourself nobody's concern. Once you've felt the third "problem" land as both escape and wound, the vow that follows sounds like someone promising never to feel again, already knowing the promise won't hold.
