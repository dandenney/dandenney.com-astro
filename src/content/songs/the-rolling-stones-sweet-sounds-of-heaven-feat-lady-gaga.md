The hardest line in **Sweet Sounds Of Heaven** isn't a hymn. It's a refusal: "No, I'm not, not goin' to Hell / In some dusty motel." It's the bluntest thing in a song otherwise full of blessings and falling light, and it gives the whole thing its spine. Around it sit prayers for the hungry, the smell of heaven, children singing about the land they were born in. Then the narrator plants a foot and says this isn't how the story ends. The defiance and the devotion never settle their argument, and the song is better for leaving them both standing.

Mick Jagger called it kind of like a gospel song that also has personal things in it, and that's an accurate map. The gospel part is all over the surface: bread and wine, Father and Son, a congregation told to sing, shout, and stand up proud. The personal part leaks out in the middle of the communion. The narrator wants to laugh and cry in the same breath, wants to be drenched in someone's heavenly love, and admits to "finally, finally quenchin'" a thirst that sounds like it has lasted a long while. That doubled "finally" carries more relief than any of the praise.

The band plays it slow and patient, a relaxed gospel shuffle built on a steady kit with a prominent snare, a simple walking bass, and electric guitar holding long chords while piano plays underneath. The lead vocal is slightly raspy, a little frayed, never pushed. The room sounds spacious and warm, with some reverb around everything, which fits how it was made: the Stones cut it largely live at Henson in Los Angeles, playing together in one room, with Steve Jordan on drums and Keith Richards on bass. The higher voice that harmonizes with the lead means the prayer isn't one person's anymore.

The people in that room explain a lot. It started as a piano piece of Jagger's, and producer Andrew Watt heard a guest in it and invited Stevie Wonder, who plays Rhodes, Moog and piano here and who toured with the band back in 1972. Lady Gaga was recording in the same studio, walked in on the session, curled up on the floor in front of Jagger, and started singing oohs and ahs into a mic someone handed her. It has the same churchy reach as "You Can't Always Get What You Want" and "Shine A Light," and it comes from the band's first album of original material since Charlie Watts died. Wonder heard it as saying hello, not goodbye.

The line the song leans toward is this one: "Let the old still believe that they're young." It's tender and stubborn at once, a blessing asked for by people who know exactly how old they are.

What sticks is that the song lets you feel two things at once, the grace of being carried and the plain refusal to go quietly, and it never asks you to pick one.
