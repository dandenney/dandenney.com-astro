Nearly every line of **Blessing** arrives twice. Alex G says it, and something says it back: "Every day (every day) / Is a blessing (is a blessing)." On paper it reads like a congregation answering a preacher, the call-and-response of a church service. Sung in a whisper over cavernous synths, the echo sounds less like a crowd and more like a voice trying a phrase on to see if it holds weight. Repetition is how prayer works, and it's also how you talk yourself into something.

The words are almost a children's hymn. Mud, fishes, a flood. The image that matters is the conditional: "If I live (if I live) / Like the fishes (like the fishes)." The fish don't escape the flood. They belong to it. The salvation offered here is to stop fighting the water and become something that can breathe in it. That's a strange, slightly unsettling idea of grace, and the song never explains it. It just keeps repeating it.

Alex G has said most of the God references on *God Save the Animals* came from people close to him who had recently become religious, and that writing the songs helped him work out what faith meant. He also told NME he was drawn to the emotional weight of religious language. That's the feeling in "Blessing": standing just outside someone else's belief, borrowing its words, and finding they carry more than expected. Gratitude and dread share the same few lines. Calling every day a blessing while walking through mud is either faith or stubbornness, and the song leaves both possible. The little "huh" that slips in after "every day," "if I live" and "through the mud" reads like someone half-questioning what they just said.

He named Audioslave as an inspiration, which is a surprising touchstone for an artist who started out making bedroom-recorded indie rock in Philadelphia. One critic heard 90s alt-grunge in that whispery vocal, and the comparison fits a song that borrows big-room weight for something this uncertain.

This was the album's first single, released before the record was even announced. The album was also the first Alex G record made solely in professional studios. Jacob Portrait engineered this track at Clubhouse in Rhinebeck, New York, with Samuel Acchione on guitar, John Heywood on bass and Tom Kelly on drums. They're the same players who join him on stage, a lineup unchanged since 2016.

"Blessing" sticks because it hands you a comforting phrase and lets you feel how much fear it takes to keep saying it.
