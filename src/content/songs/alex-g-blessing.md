The whole lyric of **Blessing** is eight short phrases, and every one of them gets said twice. A lead voice offers a line and a second voice hands it straight back, sometimes in unison, sometimes an octave apart. It plays less like a chorus than a responsive reading, one person leading and another repeating, and neither seems sure who is convincing whom. "Every day is a blessing," it begins, and the echo arrives before the thought has time to settle.

That doubling is where the feeling lives. The blessing is in the present tense, but so is the trudge through the mud. The rescue is only conditional: the singer will rise from the flood if they can learn to live "like the fishes," which means going under instead of fighting the water. That is gratitude recited as a way through, not a report from the other side. Then there are the little "huh"s tossed in at the ends of lines, offhand and almost a shrug. They keep the hymn from going pious, and they sound like someone half-embarrassed to be praying out loud. Alex G told Pitchfork that several people close to him had become religious, and it made him wonder what they'd found. He has said he isn't religious himself, and that he reaches for this language to think about forgiveness and growth. Heard that way, "Blessing" is someone trying on another person's prayer, saying it aloud to see if it holds. Hope and doubt stand next to each other, and the song never decides between them.

The delivery is mostly spoken, raw and close, breaking into melody only now and then. Reviewers heard it as a whisper. Underneath, a clean electric guitar arpeggiates, the bass keeps a simple foundational line, and the drums hold a steady beat at a moderate, unhurried pace. A sustained synth pad hangs over all of it, and the small voice seems to be chanting against something much bigger.

That pressure is the lineage. Alex G told NME the song was inspired by Audioslave, an unlikely source for a Philadelphia songwriter who came up as a Bandcamp bedroom recorder. One critic heard the whispery alt-grunge of the 90s. What "Blessing" borrows is the weight, the sense of heavy, distorted air bearing down. What it bends is the response. Where that music would howl, this murmurs.

It's also a song about leaving the room. *God Save the Animals* was the first Alex G album made with outside engineers in professional studios rather than on GarageBand at home. He wanted, he said, "a routine that was outside of my apartment." Samuel Acchione plays guitar here, John Heywood bass, Tom Kelly drums. Alex G co-produced with Jacob Portrait, who mixed the record at SugarHouse in New York. "Blessing" was the first single, released before the album was even announced.

It sticks because it lets you say the hopeful thing before you believe it, and then hear someone say it back to you.
