Most love songs promise forever. **I Like The Way This Is Going** won't go that far. Eels hang the whole song on a phrase about direction, not arrival, and that small hedge is where the feeling lives. The narrator doesn't swear devotion. The narrator notices that things are trending well and says so, again and again, like someone afraid to jinx it.

The verses are a list, and the list is plain on purpose: a toothy smile, the color of someone's hair, the way their pants fit, how they stand and how they sit. Nothing grand. The best stretch is the most domestic one, watching TV together and then going to bed, so that you can "Get up and do it all again." That line carries the song's quiet thrill. Repetition, which sinks so many relationships, is the reward here. Even the weather gets a mention, as if the narrator knows conditions change and is choosing to be glad anyway.

Then the past shows up, briefly. The narrator says they don't care about it, and adds that "None of it was made to last." It's a shrug, but it costs something. You don't wave off old wreckage that casually unless there was wreckage. The contentment and the old damage sit in the same verse, and the song never asks which one is truer. The answer it lands on is to look at "who you're knowing," the person in the room right now.

That fits where the song sits. Mark Oliver Everett wrote and produced it for *Tomorrow Morning*, an album recorded in 2009 at OneHitsville, U.S.A., in Los Feliz. The album is the last of a trilogy after *Hombre Lobo* and *End Times*. Everett called it "The redemption," and he said that after *End Times* he didn't want to go any further down. The only way left was up. This song is what up sounds like when it's still a little wary.

The arrangement keeps things easy. Acoustic guitar strums at a relaxed, moderate pace. A walking bass line strolls underneath, brushed drums give it a slight swing, and a clean electric guitar threads a melody around the vocal. The voice is slightly raspy and conversational, sung as if across a couch rather than from a stage. The sound is clean, polished and uncluttered, with room for every part to be heard. On a record built with drum machines and tape loops, this track gets by on guitar, bass and brushes, and that suits a song about small comforts.

It runs short and never pushes, and that restraint is the point: it lets you feel how hopeful it is to say out loud that so far, things are good, after a long time of not being able to.
