The most honest word in **notice me** is "damn." It opens the chorus's last line, "Damn, can't believe you notice me," and it gives away more than any verse does. Everything around it is built to sound cool. That one word is the sound of someone who still can't believe their luck.

The first verse runs on bravado. Hands in jeans, talking dirty, making a scene on a dance floor and refusing to stop until someone tells them to leave. Then the chorus arrives and the swagger turns into astonishment. The song keeps both feelings in the same body. There's the person who wants the whole room to watch, and the person who has always assumed bad luck was coming. Neither one cancels the other, and that's why the chorus feels sweet rather than smug.

The track itself stays light on its feet. A drum machine keeps a steady, driving beat with a prominent snare. A bass guitar loops a simple line, a synth pad holds the chords underneath, and a clean electric guitar picks out arpeggios over the top. The tempo leans quick, with a slight swing that keeps it from marching. Benji Miller and Happy Perez produced it. ROLE MODEL, the name Tucker Pillsbury records under, sings in a clear, slightly nasal voice with a rhythmic, almost conversational cadence, closer to talking someone into something than belting at them. The mix is dry and polished, and every part stays distinct.

BENEE, who co-wrote the song with Pillsbury and Perez, takes the contrasting second verse. Her voice is breathy and higher, and her phrasing stretches out where his stays rhythmic. Her verse turns inward: "Only thought I'd have you in my dreams." It's the same disbelief, softer and more private, running through brown eyes and a whisper. Pillsbury has said he had her track "Glitter" on repeat in his car, and you can hear why he wanted her. She makes the crush feel mutual. She's the only guest on the EP *oh, how perfect*, and the song is better for having two people stunned by each other.

Then comes the confession that explains the whole thing: "Always had bad luck, never got this far." The song sits in the confessional bedroom-pop scene of Gen Z singer-songwriters, a scene one critic tied to the teen angst of *Booksmart* and *Euphoria*. This song bends that mood toward the grin instead of the gloom. Pillsbury later released an acoustic version, with a video of him and a guitar in an empty school auditorium, and that room fits. It's where you go to dance when you're not supposed to.

The song captures the rush of being chosen by someone you only expected to have in your dreams, and that "damn" lets you feel the swagger and the disbelief in the same breath.
