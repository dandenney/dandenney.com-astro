The title reads like a goodbye, but the chorus says otherwise. In **So Long, Honey**, "so long" is about time, not farewell: you can only drift on the wind for so long before you come back down. The narrator isn't seeing anyone off. He's settling in to wait, and the whole song lives in that gap between letting go and refusing to.

Taylor Meier and Evan Westfall wrote it for Caamp's self-titled 2016 debut, a record Relix called sparse and self-produced. Westfall has said that on the first album the two wrote sitting across from each other, or Meier brought in a mostly finished song, and the sound here has that two-people-in-a-room closeness. The fingerpicked acoustic guitar is bright and clear. A banjo joins it. The bass keeps things simple, and the drums hold a relaxed, slightly swung mid-tempo with the snare landing on two and four. The room is dry and close, the take unpolished. Meier sings in a slightly raspy mid-range, conversational more than showy, as if he's talking someone down rather than performing for them.

The opening verse sets up the picture the song keeps returning to. Throw a rock and something breaks. Throw a feather and the wind takes it. The person he loves is the feather, and the narrator knows better than to try catching it. That's why the chorus lands so gently: "And when you do, I'll be hangin' around." It's a casual phrase carrying a lot of weight, patience dressed up as loitering, so the want doesn't sound like need.

The second verse drops that guard. There's smoke in the room and a piano that doesn't play, and then a dry little correction: "It never did." Nothing in this place ever worked quite right, and he's stayed anyway. Right after that comes a plain admission, the feeling he gets when this person is in the room and the wish that they'd stay. Pride and longing sit side by side, and the song never makes him pick one.

Then it turns outward. The last section drops the lover entirely and becomes a blessing for everyone else: "Be good to your mothers," because they did the best they could, and bleed for your brothers, because they would for you. Westfall has said the album holds hidden nods to the nicknames and friends the band ran around with, and this coda sounds like a toast to a crowd like that. The "Lord knows" gives it a churchy lift, and the wordless oohs carry it out. The heartbreak doesn't get resolved. It gets folded into a bigger loyalty.

That's the move that sticks: a song about waiting for one person ends by telling you who will still be there while you wait.
