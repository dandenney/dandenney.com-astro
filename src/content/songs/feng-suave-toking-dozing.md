**Toking, Dozing** opens on a theological question nobody asked for: if there's an afterlife, does anyone there hear the dolphins? "But what about the dolphins," the song wonders, and before the thought can turn heavy it gets shrugged off with one word: "bummer." That move is the whole song. A real worry shows up, gets named, and gets set down again because holding it is too much.

Feng Suave, the Amsterdam duo of Daniël de Jong and Daniel Elvis Schoemaker, wrote it with Adura Sulaiman. The band has explained it as a song about people who get high and drift off because they can't find a way to care about dying dolphins, deforestation, war and famine. They framed it as a tug between looking after your own head and body and paying attention to everything else. The song doesn't settle that argument. The narrator isn't proud of checking out, and isn't ashamed enough to stop. The chorus is a self-diagnosis: no emotions, nothing much to think about, "Just a hollow moment," a couple more and then done. It reads as an excuse and a confession at once.

The second verse sharpens the irony. We were never quite at home in paradise, the lyric says, but the CGI is just unbelievable, so "turn your televisions on again." It's sarcasm aimed outward and inward at the same time. The narrator mocks a culture of distraction while lying right in the middle of it.

The sound sells the sedation. A clean electric guitar picks out arpeggiated chords under a light strum, the bass walks, and the drums sit in a relaxed, swung groove, snare on two and four, closed hi-hat ticking along at a medium-slow pace. The production is clean and polished, open and unhurried. The vocal is breathy and easy, the lead doubled so it floats a little. The New LoFi called the single an anthem of yacht-rock laziness, and that fits the 1970s soul smoothness the duo folds into its Amsterdam psych-pop. Then the chorus ends in wordless na-nas, the language giving out exactly where the feeling would have to be spoken.

It later turned up on the Warping Youth EP, which keeps returning to death against bright, catchy arrangements. Here that contrast is the point. The prettiness is the numbing agent.

The song lets you hum along to your own apathy and feel slightly caught doing it, and that small sting is why it keeps coming back.
