A verse of **Restlessness** opens like a phone call home: "Dad, I've found love now." Then comes the news anyone would want to share: the move to the coast, the hair grown out and gone curly. Benjamin Haycock wrote the song to his late father, and the heartbreak is in how ordinary these updates are. He is giving a report to someone who can't answer, and the pride in it is real.

The line that catches is "And apparently, I'm looking more like you every day." The word "apparently" carries it. Someone else told him. He is hearing about his own resemblance second-hand and carrying it like a gift and a wound at once. Growing into a father's face is how the dead stay close, and it is also a daily reminder of who is missing. The song doesn't choose between those feelings. Out on the water, between waves, it lands on "Of the ocean's the place I can hear you talk, Dad." Haycock moved to the Devon coast after his father died, so the sea in the song is both the new home and the place where the conversation carries on.

The chorus pulls back from the private letter to a shared one. Its minds are "our" minds, its scars are ones time won't remove, and its fighters are dug into trenches against their own past. Haycock has said the song is about what people battle in their heads and keep inside. The chorus enacts that. Its last line never finishes: twice it trails into a hum, and the final time it stops on a hanging "our-". The restlessness never finishes its sentence.

Between those passages Haycock raps. One verse piles up words like "sediments," "embezzling" and "benevolence" into a dense run where momentum outpaces meaning, which is its own way of saying the head won't quiet down. Bandcamp files the track under acoustic rap and alternative folk with percussive guitar, and the song sits in that UK strand of confessional folk with bars folded in. The Music Man called it pop, rap and grit with a stripped-back, Bob Dylan-style authenticity. You hear rapid speech and a low, slightly rough, breathy singing voice that holds its notes with a little vibrato. A clean guitar keeps a slow, relaxed pace. Voice and guitar each get their own space in a natural-sounding room. It's the song Haycock brought to his blind audition on The Voice UK, where all four coaches turned.

What sticks is the casual "apparently," the sound of someone finding out from other people's faces that a parent is still alive in theirs.
