**It Gets Better** moves like someone who has finally stopped running. The drums are played with brushes and keep a laid-back beat, the bass walks instead of pushes, and a fingerpicked acoustic guitar turns its pattern over and over at a slow, relaxed groove. Travis Meadows sings over it in a clear, mid-range voice, smooth and steady. It's a calm song. Once you know where it came from, that calm becomes the whole story.

Meadows wrote *Killin' Uncle Buzzy* in 2010, during his fourth stay in rehab. A counselor suggested he keep a journal, and he says he basically made the record out of it. In his note for the album he describes almost seven months of climbing out of a hole he dug for himself, and calls the record "part exorcism." He told American Songwriter it was the only record he never meant anyone to hear.

That changes what a title like "It Gets Better" is doing. It isn't advice handed down from the safe side of recovery. It's a promise made from inside the process, by someone in his fourth round of rehab. A journal entry can hold hope and doubt on the same page without settling which one wins, and this track carries that kind of hope: steady, quiet, and not yet proven. The playing doesn't push to sell the idea. It just keeps time, which is its own kind of faith.

For a record built from private pages, it doesn't sound like a bedroom demo. Meadows produced it with Reid Scelza, who recorded, mixed and mastered it, along with Barry Dean, who also sings background vocals on this track as a guest. The sound is clean and polished, with a natural sense of room around the players. If anything, the polish makes the song gentler than its backstory, and some listeners may want more grit. The gentleness seems deliberate, though. You don't shout a thing you're still trying to believe.

The song sits in the Nashville confessional songwriter tradition. Meadows, born in 1965, had a Universal Music Publishing deal that his addictions kept him from making use of. *Killin' Uncle Buzzy* circulated among Nashville artists as an underground recovery record, and his songs went on to be cut by Dierks Bentley, Eric Church and Jake Owen. In 2021 Earache Records reissued it for its tenth anniversary, clear vinyl included, which is a long way to travel for a record meant for no one.

The song sticks because you're listening to a man say a hope out loud before he's earned it, softly enough that you end up holding it with him.
