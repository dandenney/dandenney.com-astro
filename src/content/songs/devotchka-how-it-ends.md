**How It Ends** opens with an object and an instruction: "Hold your grandmother's Bible to your breast." It's a strange way to begin a song about fate. It isn't a prayer, and it isn't a doubt. It's a test, and the person holding the book wants it to pass. Nick Urata sings in the second person, so the burden lands on whoever's listening. Somebody has to be strong here. Everyone depends on you, the lyric says, and you already know what you have to do.

The second verse is where the hurt shows through. The song says there's no escape from the slave-catcher's songs, which makes the past something you can't outrun. Then comes the confession buried in a compliment: they poked a million holes in your soul, "But you never let 'em show." That's the feeling at the center of the song. It's someone holding steady for others while quietly coming apart, and taking a kind of grim pride in keeping it hidden. The song never says whether that's admirable or sad. It lets both sit there.

The third verse turns toward reward: a place in the sun, children provided for, wishes granted beyond your wildest dreams. It could be consolation or it could be a sales pitch. One line gives the game away: "You always wanted to believe." The wanting is the true part. The refrain keeps coming back, insisting the ending is already known, and the lyric closes on it until it sounds less like a threat than a calm acceptance. Urata told the Christian Science Monitor the title reflects an optimistic fatalism: everyone knows how it ends, so enjoy the present. He has also said the song "wrote itself," that it was borrowed rather than authored.

The sound carries that steadiness. Acoustic guitar strums under a walking bass line, and the drums keep a plain, driving beat at a moderate pace. Urata's voice is clear and sustained, sitting in the middle of its range, never reaching for drama. Lindsay Emmet added violin on this track. The production is clean, with a moderate wash of reverb that suggests a medium-sized room.

DeVotchKa recorded the album with Craig Schumacher at Wavelab in Tucson over several road trips, and it sits in the Tucson orbit of Calexico and Giant Sand. The band's mix of Eastern European folk, Southwestern touches and indie rock, with a Western-soundtrack feel, fits that company. Urata said the band might have folded if the record failed. Instead this song found its way into the trailer for Everything Is Illuminated, and the album led to the Little Miss Sunshine score.

What sticks is the permission it gives: you can know the worst is coming and still keep your shoulders square for the people leaning on you.
