**Welcome to Hard Times** opens with a pitch. Crockett plays the barker at the door of a rigged house, telling everyone (boys and girls, women, children, you and him) that life is a casino and the dice are loaded. Even a hobo, he adds, could tell you that much. It's a dry joke, and the chorus leans into it with the cheerful menace of a host who knows how your night will end: "Do you like sinnin'? No? / Well, you will before you go." The welcome is warm. The room is crooked. That gap is where the song lives.

The band plays it the way that host would want, relaxed and unbothered. An acoustic guitar keeps a steady rhythm, a walking bass strolls underneath, and the drums hold a simple beat with brushes. The swing is slight, the tempo easy. A clean electric guitar plays a melodic line, and nobody harmonizes with Crockett. The voice stands alone, clear with a little rasp, delivered like conversation across a bar. The room sounds dry. Nothing here pleads for attention, which makes the bad news land harder.

This is the sound Crockett calls Gulf and Western: 1960s country-western blended with New Orleans soul and R&B, blues, and Texas troubadour roots. He has called the album a hardcore gothic country record. The title track bends that tradition toward something darker. The groove invites you to sway while the words describe a fixed game.

Then the middle of the song drops the act. Crockett asks you to look in his eyes and see past the bright lights stretched out in front of him, and the barker turns into someone wondering whether anyone would notice him at all: "If I told you my life just isn't fair?" The question is half self-pity and half a dare, and the song won't say which. It doesn't wait for an answer. The chorus comes right back, mask on, still selling sin and lies. The pride and the hurt sit side by side and never settle. The final line pushes the welcome one step further, into a hope that you're feeling it too.

Crockett wrote it in November 2019 and cut the record in February 2020 at producer Mark Neill's studio, a month before the pandemic upended the country. The album came after his open-heart surgery. He has said he was writing about what he saw, not about the pandemic. It was the album's first single and drew the strongest response of any he'd released, including from people who had given up on country music.

The song gets you smiling along with the man running the crooked table, and then it makes you wonder who is going to look him in the eye.
