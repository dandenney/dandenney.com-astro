The first voice on **Super Freaky Girl** isn't Nicki Minaj's. It's Rick James, a cappella, singing that she's alright, that girl's alright with him. Then the record takes the microphone away from him. James's 1981 "Super Freak," co-written with Alonzo Miller, gets reworked here from a woman's point of view. She does the describing now, and she isn't waiting for anyone's approval.

Minaj has been circling this song a long time. She sampled "Super Freak" on "Dilly Dally" back on her 2007 mixtape *Playtime Is Over*. This time the spark came from producer Malibu Babie, who said she and two collaborators landed on the sample and spent several hours fine-tuning the beat. It reached LunchMoney Lewis, then Minaj, who wrote the whole song within about a day. Dr. Luke, Vaughn Oliver and Aaron Joseph are credited as producers alongside Malibu Babie. Even the alter ego she raps as, Nick James, tips a hat to the original.

The beat is bright and bass-heavy. A sustained low note holds the floor while a high, plucked synth pecks out a short staccato figure, and kick, snare and quick hi-hats keep a steady, driving pace. The mix is dry, with no reverb to hide in. That suits a voice this direct: rhythmic and conversational, slipping into sung phrases, never padded with harmonies. It's one voice, right up front.

The feeling here is pleasure, and the pleasure comes from being in charge. The chorus runs through everything she can do and lands on the man's side of it: "every time he leave me 'lone, he always tell me he miss it." His longing is the punchline, and then it becomes a chant, F-R-E-A-K spelled out like a cheer at a game she's already won. The verses move from the bedroom to the rivalry. "I got a princess face, a killer body, samurai mind" is a self-portrait in three quick strokes. The competition matters as much as the sex: an ex who went up against her and didn't survive, imitators who can't be Nicki. The best joke is a résumé gag, "On applications, I write 'pressure'," a boast and a wink in the same breath. Desire and competition sit side by side, and neither one apologizes for the other.

The song debuted at No. 1 on the Hot 100, Minaj's first solo chart-topper. It also made Malibu Babie the first woman this century to debut at No. 1 on Hot R&B/Hip-Hop Songs as a producer. That's a fitting result for a record about who gets to tell the story.

You come away knowing how it feels when the person being described grabs the mic, louder and funnier and completely in control.
