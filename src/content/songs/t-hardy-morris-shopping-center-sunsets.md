There's a minute every evening when the parking lot outside the e-cigarette counter and the car-title lender turns the color of a cocktail. **Shopping Center Sunsets** is about that light landing on all the wrong things. T. Hardy Morris builds the refrain as an inventory: electronic cigarettes, hair supply outlets, tribal tattoo concepts, dinette sets on layaway, discount aquatic pets. "Car title cash regrets" is the sharpest item on the shelf, four words that carry a whole bad month.

The Record Dept calls it satirical, and Glide Magazine hears a song about the rundown strip malls that turn up in towns of every size. Both are right, and neither is the whole story. The verses step out of the catalog and into the narrator's own errands: a liquor run because the bedside table has gone dry, a thought that the lottery deserves more attention. The one making the jokes is also a customer. Then the sky goes magenta and the feeling flips. "Under God's neon sign" mocks and means it at once. "Nature sees my need to believe" is as unguarded as the song gets, someone admitting they crave a sign. The song never decides whether the sunset is grace or just a pretty accident over a parking lot, and it's better for refusing. On the page, the last line is that need-to-believe line alone, without the "And sends me" that followed it the two times before, so the wish is left hanging.

The music keeps a straight face. A clean electric guitar rolls arpeggios through a tremolo, a second guitar holds long, high notes over it, the bass stays on the roots, and the drums sit at an easy mid-tempo with the snare on two and four. The groove is relaxed, more evening stroll than march. Morris sings in a clear, slightly nasal mid-range, unhurried, with no harmonies behind, and the dry, intimate production puts the voice right at your shoulder.

Morris wrote it with Adam Landry, who produced The Digital Age of Rome at Tweed Recording in a downtown Athens, Georgia, emptied out by the pandemic. Morris, once the guitarist and singer of the Southern hard rock band Dead Confederate, said he wanted the record to sound the way the second half of 2020 felt to him: uncomfortable, chaotic, dystopian but still beautiful. This song is that sentence set to a tremolo guitar. It belongs to Athens's Southern indie songwriting, and it moves him away from the classic-rock feel of his earlier records.

It sticks because it lets you laugh at the strip mall, then catches you standing in its parking lot, waiting for the sky to tell you something.
