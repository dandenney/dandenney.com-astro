**Slowly** starts with someone waking up and checking whether he's dead. "Born again out of REM," Skuff Micksun sings, then lists the morning chores of getting through: drag the body out of bed, tell himself he's okay, proceed with the day. The flatness is the point. It's a routine, not a recovery. A line that says only that he's mostly lonely hangs there on its own, the one thing the routine can't cover.

The song keeps sliding between "I" and "we." One line says we don't want to be dark anymore, the next says I don't, as if the narrator can't decide whether this sinking feeling is shared or his alone. That uncertainty is the most human thing in the song. In the second verse the words speed into a run of -less wordplay: senses, senseless, headless, relentless, endless, wreck-less. This is where his habit of mixing spoken word with melody shows. The wordplay is almost a joke, until it turns into admitting it's all his fault and then, right after, fearing everyone might be doomed. Blaming himself and fearing for everyone sit next to each other with no line between them. Then comes the sharpest moment, a narrator catching himself mid-spiral: "Am I doing it again." He knows the pattern of getting real, real dark in his head and trying to crawl out of his own skin. Knowing it doesn't stop it.

The chorus asks for very little: a light, a spark, a smile from someone that comes from a part of them that hasn't died. It's modest and generous at once. The narrator is looking for something alive in other people because he's short on it himself. Then the chorus lands on "But I'm barely alive," and the hope doesn't cancel the confession.

This acoustic take came out in January 2023, after the studio original arrived on Bandcamp the previous August. Fingerpicked and strummed acoustic guitar carries it, with a walking bass line and a kick, snare and hi-hat underneath. The tempo is medium and the groove is relaxed but pushing forward. The mix is clean and clear. Skuff Micksun's mid-range voice stays conversational, with no harmonies dressing it up, so it sounds like someone talking you through a bad week. He tagged the announcement #midwestemo, which suits a song that sets a plain, unguarded confession over guitars that keep moving. He said it was out "to keep you warm," which is a kind way to frame a song this dark.

Casey W. Skufca is credited as composer and lyricist, with Tyler Cratcha as composer. Skuff Micksun works out of Cleveland and has said the "Micksun" honors his late father, Mick. The chorus can feel generic, and the "we" sometimes blurs who's actually in trouble. But that blur is also how depression talks.

What sticks is the narrator's voice catching itself, asking if it's happening again, and you recognizing that question from the inside.
