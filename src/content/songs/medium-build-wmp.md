The most damning line in **WMP** is also the gentlest: "Babe, I'll be in bed in just a minute." It arrives after the admission that the narrator's face is glued to his phone, and it lands like every promise made half-turned away from the person waiting. Nick Carpenter sings it in a clear, slightly nasal voice, in a clean, dry mix. He sounds like he's telling you across a kitchen table, not from a stage.

The song is a ledger of advice and accusations. It opens on the life advice handed down to him, "Be a good hang, a decent guy," a creed so low-bar it reads as a joke and a trap at once. Then come the nights: blackouts, a "whole new me" who grabs the wheel and drives home drunk. The woman he's sleeping with says he's too much like her dad, too in love with his own head, a product of white male privilege. He doesn't argue. He reports it as flatly as he reports the drinking, then retreats into a chorus that shrugs: too late to work out where he went wrong, so he'll be alone. Self-pity and self-indictment share the same breath, and the song won't choose between them.

The arrangement keeps all of that plain. An acoustic guitar picks out a simple arpeggiated figure, bass holds root notes, drums keep a basic beat, and the groove has a steady, melancholy sway. A second voice shadows the lead, so the confession sounds less like a soliloquy and more like two people admitting the same thing. Carpenter wrote it with James Glaves, who also mixed and mastered roughboy. Glaves has said nearly every song on the record came from something one of them actually lived through, from lost love to addiction.

Then the song turns. Told to make plans, and afraid of messing them up again, he recites the script: buy a house, have kids, get a job, "Hang rope lights in the backyard," then a minivan, then a second house on the lake. Each item is more ordinary than the last. The promise that everything will be fine curdles into a repeated "oh my god" that stretches its last word out as far as it will go. It's hard to tell whether he's reaching for that life or choking on it. Probably both.

Carpenter studied music in Tennessee, dropped out and moved to Anchorage, and he's called the Medium Build name a safe place to hide and fail. As the opening track on roughboy, this song sets that terms sheet out plainly: the failures, lined up in order, with no excuses attached.

You end up holding both halves of him at once, the man who knows exactly what's wrong with himself and the one still promising he'll be right there.
