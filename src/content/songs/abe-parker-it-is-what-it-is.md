The title is a shrug, but nothing else in **it is what it is** shrugs. Abe Parker sets the most worn-out phrase in English, the one people use to end a conversation about something that hurts, against verses that refuse to let the matter drop. The narrator is talking to a wall for the echo, running out of alibis, pounding on heaven's gates and hearing nothing back. "It is what it is" is what you say when you've stopped expecting an answer and can't stop asking anyway.

The writing borrows the hymnal's vocabulary and turns it toward doubt. There's a cross carried until the spirit gives out, a soul sent up toward heaven, a plea to a Father. The sharpest line inverts a miracle: "You're walking on the water that I'm drowning in." Someone else stands on the surface of the thing that's pulling the narrator under, and the line holds both awe and resentment without picking one.

What makes it ache is the self-accounting that won't settle. "I lay with someone else's sins" sounds like a complaint about being handed another person's damage. Elsewhere the narrator wonders whether the blame is theirs after all, casts themselves as the wolf among the sheep, and cries "Father, save me from myself," even while insisting the hell was never chosen. Guilt and innocence sit side by side, and Parker lets them. The closing lines turn to a "you" who alone keeps the narrator alive, and that is as close to relief as the song gets.

Parker wrote it alone and also produced, engineered and mixed it. The production is clean and polished, never crowded. A fingerpicked acoustic pattern carries the song, and it suits an artist who has said he's formally trained on guitar, who started out playing the street in Atlanta and worked his way into rooms like Eddie's Attic after leaving Blue Rock, Ohio. Kick and snare keep a slow, steady pulse over a plain root-note bass. Parker's voice is breathy and slightly strained, with stacked harmonies gathering around it. A synth pad swells, the drums find fills and cymbal crashes, and the arrangement thins to kick and snare at other moments. That push and pull fits a narrator who says they feel everything and nothing at the same time.

It's an acoustic-leaning, confessional folk-pop song, and its shrug of a title is the sound of someone holding in a scream until it comes out as a hum you can't stop carrying.
