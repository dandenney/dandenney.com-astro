The cruelest line in **I'd Rather Overdose** is also the plainest one: "If only you loved me, like you love gettin' high." It's a sentence anyone who has loved an addict has thought and maybe never said, because saying it means admitting you're in a contest you will lose. honestav and Z set it in a song that keeps switching sides. One moment the narrator is the one left crying while someone else is "fucked up on them pills." The next, the narrator is the one too high to be looked at, watching faith drain away "with every pill I take." The song never settles which voice is the sick one. That refusal is the point. In a family where addiction runs deep, everyone ends up both people.

The hook is the darkest twist. "I'd rather overdose" turns the pleading into a threat aimed back at the self, a person so tied to someone's using that the only exit they can picture is the same drug. It's ugly logic, and the song lets it stand without comment.

Then there's the verse where the story stops being about anyone else. "My dad's dead, my mom's proud" puts grief and pride in one line with no transition, the way a person in shock lists facts. The verse moves on to a brother stuck in their hometown, an empty bank account, being "'posed to be the hero" and still messing up. A few lines later comes the apology that doesn't ask for forgiveness: "I know that you hate me, and I hate me too." Self-loathing and longing sit side by side, and the narrator wants to be missed by someone they can't stop hurting.

honestav, who is Avrey Freeman from Pierce City, Missouri, wrote the song with Isiah Fortman. He has said he wrote it from a very hard place, soon after his father died, drawing on addiction he'd watched all his life, his own included. Heydium produced it.

The music doesn't match the wreckage, and that's a large part of why the song works. A clean electric guitar picks out an arpeggio over a simple bass line and a drum machine holding a straight beat, snare on two and four. A synth pad hums underneath. The mix is dry and polished. The vocal is clear and a little nasal, sung melodically, with no harmonies to soften it. One profile describes honestav's sound as folk meeting hip-hop, scratchy verses over tender acoustic guitar. Here the sound is cooler and steadier than that description suggests, closer to what Apple Music files under alternative.

The song caught fire on social media, reached the Billboard charts, and later landed on his 2024 project *hara-kiri*. It ends right where it began, with the same chorus. Nothing resolves. The steady groove keeps you nodding along to a sentence you'd be scared to hear from someone you love.
