The compliments in **Why Me?** arrive secondhand. In the hook, Mai Mai reports what he likes about her (the way she moves, how she keeps her distance from other men) in the third person, as if reading a review of somebody else. Then in the verses she turns to him directly and starts talking him out of it. That split between the person being praised and the person doing the refusing is the whole song.

Mai Mai, the Vallejo-born singer, rapper and songwriter Mailea Santos, has said the track is about someone who can't accept love because of their own insecurities, someone questioning whether they're worth it. The lyrics never slide into self-pity. The title question is half rhetorical and half a dare. "'Cause I know I'm never good for nobody," she sings, and the "but" hanging off the end of that line is where the hope sneaks in. She won't finish the thought. The chorus finishes it for her, with his reasons.

What makes it more than a breakup in advance is the pride tangled up in the doubt. "I ain't ready to be anybody's trophy" refuses being owned as much as it confesses being unready. She warns him she'll come with attitude, in and out of moods, and it sounds less like an apology than a fair-warning notice. Then the guard drops: "My life a mess." She frames her retreat as protecting him, which is kind and also a way to leave before she gets left. And she asks him to get her off her own mind, a strange thing to ask of someone you're pushing away.

MilanMadeIt's production keeps everything close and dry. A sparse, mid-tempo hip-hop beat sits under a low synth line that repeats like a thought you can't shake, with a higher synth part above it. There's almost no reverb, so the voice sits right at your ear: clear, slightly nasal, rhythmic with melodic lifts, riding the line between rapping and singing. The label talks up warm basslines and a laid-back groove, and the ease is real, but the dryness keeps it from going soft-focus. It's soul-leaning melodic rap from a Bay Area lineage, and critics have set her voice beside Macy Gray, Angie Stone and Billie Holiday.

Santos started writing as a quiet kid who didn't want the spotlight, treating songs as a journal. This one, her Warner debut, co-written with Milan Szelcsanyi, Noel Ameha and Brandon Acosta, still reads like a page torn out. At about two minutes, it says its piece and goes, the way its narrator keeps trying to.

You end up rooting for him and for her refusal at once, which is exactly the bind of being loved before you've decided you deserve it.
