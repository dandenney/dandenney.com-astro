A synth bass circles the same simple pattern while a pad holds long, unhurried chords underneath, and the whole thing sits so dry and close it feels like the room has no walls to echo off. That's the first thing **Cloud 9 Freestyle** gives you: a floor to stand on. Kick and snare keep a steady, laid-back pulse at a moderate tempo, and the groove doesn't push or ask for anything. It just holds.

Lizzy Jeff, the Los Angeles rapper, activist and self-described "Medicine Woman" who runs Zen & Kush Records, knows exactly what she built here. She pitches the track as an elevated vibe for the morning, something to activate your day, and calls it perfect right before meditation, a workout or other daily rituals. That framing matters, because it tells you whose feeling this is meant to become. It's less a confession than a preparation. The emotion on offer is the quiet resolve of someone getting their head right before the day starts pulling at them.

The vocal carries that. One voice, clear and direct, delivers the rap in a near-spoken style, with no harmonies stacked behind it. In a mix this clean and close, the effect is intimate in a practical way, like a friend talking you through the first stretch of a routine rather than performing at you. Mike Wallz is credited as the featured artist, and Lightfoot produced. That partnership clearly stuck: Lightfoot turns up again on Lizzy Jeff's Zen & Kush (Vol. 1) release, five years on.

The song sits in the independent West Coast underground, in the strand of conscious rap that treats verses as something closer to practice than boast. Lizzy Jeff's own site describes her music as the place where sensuality meets sacred activism and hip-hop becomes a healing modality, and she's taken that literally, hosting an Oakland session built around an herbal tea ceremony and guided meditation. Her Bandcamp bio says she delivers enlightened bars over hard beats, but this one trades the hard for the steady, which fits the ritual it's meant to serve.

The honest catch is that a bass line this simple and pads this sustained can read as static if you come to it wanting motion. Nothing in the groove argues with itself. That's the design, though, not an oversight.

What sticks is the permission it gives: a beat that refuses to rush you, and a voice that sounds already settled, so you borrow its calm before you've earned your own.
