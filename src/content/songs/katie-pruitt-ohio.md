The line that does the work in **Ohio** isn't the one everybody knows. It's the question in the middle: "What if you knew her and / Found her dead on the ground?" Neil Young's song was an angry response to the Kent State shootings in May 1970, a dare to anyone keeping their distance. In Katie Pruitt's hands it becomes a test of empathy. The song refuses to let the dead stay abstract. It asks you to put a face you love on the ground and then tells you what you already know, that running isn't an option once you've pictured it.

Pruitt released the cover five days before the November 2020 election, and she has said the song carried a double meaning that year. It worked as an anthem against police brutality, and it marked the roughly 230,000 Americans who had died of coronavirus by then. That reading changes what "Four dead in Ohio" means. The number stays four, but the grief behind it gets much bigger. The original was a newspaper headline turned into a song. Pruitt sings it as a stand-in for every count that kept rising that year.

The arrangement is clean and unhurried. An acoustic guitar picks out arpeggiated chords. An electric guitar holds a sustained, clear melody line over the top. Calvin Knowles walks the bass underneath, and Ross McReynolds keeps a steady, driving beat with the snare out front. The production is polished, with space around the players. Pruitt's voice is clear with a slight rasp, and the delivery is direct. There's no snarl in it. The anger comes through as steadiness, the sound of someone who has decided to say this plainly and keep saying it.

That steadiness fits the lyric's structure. The wordless "na, na-na-na" stretch is a crowd's chant, the part anyone can join in on. The ending repeats "Four dead in Ohio" seven times, and it lands less like a hook than a roll call that won't stop.

Pruitt grew up in suburban Atlanta loving rock and punk before turning to Joni Mitchell and Bonnie Raitt, and you can hear both pulls here: rock-band drive under a plain, clear voice that trusts the words to carry. She recorded it shortly after returning home, on the same day as her own protest song "Look The Other Way" and a cover of Young's "After the Gold Rush." Heard that way, it sits as part of one long argument.

The song gets under your skin by making you imagine one specific person on the ground, and then counting, over and over, until the number feels like a name.
