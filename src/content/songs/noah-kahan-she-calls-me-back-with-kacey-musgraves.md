"This town's the same as you left it, your page was blank, but I read it." That line holds the whole problem of **She Calls Me Back (with Kacey Musgraves)**. One person left. One stayed. The one who stayed is still reading meaning into silence, still dialing the number, still treating a callback as the only proof that everything's alright. Noah Kahan wrote the song alone, and it sounds like a private ledger. The repeated chorus sets heaven in someone's eyes against a narrator who "was not baptized," someone close to grace but never let in.

What makes the song sting is how clearly the narrator sees the obsession and can't stop it. "Why am I so obsessive?" isn't rhetorical. It sits in a rush of questions, one sentence tumbling into the next, the way thoughts go at 2 a.m. The verses swing between self-sacrifice and self-pity: he could be your sacrifice, an old tattoo, someone who lives "to die while saving you." It's grand and a little ridiculous, and the song seems to know it. The pain is real, and so is the self-justification. Neither cancels the other.

Then the confession that reframes everything: "I was too afraid of livin' life in your footsteps." Staying wasn't only loyalty. It was fear. That lands harder coming from Kahan, a Vermont native, on **Stick Season**, the 2022 album that became his commercial breakthrough after earlier cult-favorite records. The album was recorded at Guilford Sound in Vermont and The Study in Nashville.

The duet changes the angle. Kahan asked Kacey Musgraves to join him, and she picked this song herself. She sings a new second verse from the partner's perspective, recorded while Kahan was on tour. Kahan said her verse lets the song move toward resolve instead of the bitter tension of the original. Both voices take the last chorus together, and the one-sided call becomes a two-way line.

The sound stays plain and driving. A strummed acoustic guitar pushes it at a moderate clip, over a simple bass line and steady drums with a prominent snare. Gabe Simon, who co-produced with Kahan, played nearly all the instruments, and the room feels natural and clean. Kahan's voice is raw and earnest, and Musgraves' harmony sits close to the lead, in unison or a near interval. It's folk-pop with a faint heartland-rock spine, built to be shouted in a car.

The song lets you feel both ends of a stalled phone line at once: the shame of still dialing, and the quiet relief of a call coming back.
