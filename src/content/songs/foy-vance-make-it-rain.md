"Even so, Lord just the same." That's the line that holds **Make It Rain** together, and it's a strange thing to pray. Foy Vance has just admitted the fire will hurt. He knows the tears might be wasted. He asks for it anyway, eight times over in the early choruses, as if saying it enough could turn dread into consent.

The song opens with inherited weight: "When the sins of my father weigh down in my soul," followed at once by a mother's pain that "will not let me go." Nothing gets confessed in detail. The narrator doesn't say what was done or by whom. What's left is the feeling of carrying something you didn't choose and can't put down. The fire from the sky is meant to "refine the purest of kings," so the suffering is supposed to purify. But the second verse loses that confidence. The ground keeps getting harder, the hunger more profound, and the tears "may as well be in vain." Surrender and something close to despair sit right next to each other, and the song never settles which one wins.

Then the third verse turns outward. The sea stops at the shore, "just like the riches of grandeur never reach the poor." A private plea for cleansing becomes a demand for justice, with lightning as veins and thunder as applause, "when it's time to make a change." The refrain that follows runs twice as long as before. The prayer widens because now it's for everybody.

The playing gives that prayer room to breathe. It's a slow, bluesy shuffle. An acoustic guitar strums the chords, the bass walks underneath, and the drums keep a simple beat. The room is warm and medium-sized, with natural reverb and a slightly raw edge. Vance sings in a raspy, soulful voice and holds the notes long, so each "make it rain" lands with weight instead of urgency.

That rawness fits the record it closes. Vance cut *From Muscle Shoals* at FAME Studios with co-producer Ben Tanner. It was recorded live with all the players in the room, and Swampers Spooner Oldham and David Hood are on the album. The song itself is older. It's one of two tracks reworked for the record, and Ed Sheeran covered it for *Sons of Anarchy* in 2014. He asked to because it opens with "the sins of my father," a line the show's creator had requested. Here it lands in the southern soul tradition the album pays homage to.

It sticks because it lets you ask for the hard thing out loud, knowing it will hurt and wanting it anyway.
