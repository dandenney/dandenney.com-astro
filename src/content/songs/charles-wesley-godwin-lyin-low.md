"Lying low" usually means hiding out. In **Lyin' Low**, Charles Wesley Godwin gives the phrase a second floor. The narrator lies low by choice, up the holler on the north side of the mountain. His father lay low because the job demanded it: "In a better way than my daddy minin' thirty inch coal." The chorus sets a man resting on his own land beside a man in the mine, and it never says which feeling should win. There's pride in that line, and gratitude, and maybe a little guilt about having the easier version of a hard inheritance. Godwin's own father was a coal miner, which makes the comparison feel less like a device and more like a debt being acknowledged.

Godwin has called it a song about being content: stepping back, staying out of the rat race, quitting the endless chase for more. He wrote it while sorting out his own priorities, with family and home first, and he talked about a point of diminishing returns. The lyric earns that peace honestly, because it keeps the fear in view. The narrator is "like a bull to the slaughter, a fish out of water" whenever he crosses the wood line. Running with the masses leads to "an early casket." Contentment here isn't serene. It's a decision a man has to keep making, partly because he knows what pulls at him. "I'm doin' just fine" sounds like he's still convincing himself.

The sound matches that steadiness. A fingerpicked acoustic arpeggio rolls under a walking bass line. Simple drums hold a moderate, relaxed country groove with a slight swing, and a steel guitar lays sustained, melodic lines over it. Godwin sings alone, with no harmonies, in a clear baritone with a little rasp, and he delivers it plainly. The album was produced by Al Torrence and made at his Music Garden Studios in New Brighton, Pennsylvania. This track sounds polished but not slick, with a natural room around the players.

This was the first song released from *How the Mighty Fall*, and it fits the West Virginia tradition of holler-centered, character-driven country-folk that Godwin keeps working in. The best small touch comes in the last chorus. "Reapin' what I sow" turns into "where my ginseng grows." The farmer's proverb gives way to something quieter and more private, a crop tucked away on the north side of the mountain.

The song sticks because it makes you feel what it costs to choose enough, carrying your father's thirty inches of coal in your chest while you sit still on purpose.
