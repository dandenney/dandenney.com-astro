The trick of **Never Been Over** is that the whole song is built from denials. The chorus runs through every breakup scene a country song could reach for: friends divided, records divided, walls going up, letters going into the fire, taillights shrinking down the road. This couple, the narrator insists, has never been any of it. Then the title phrase lands, "But we've never been over," and it sounds like a vow and a wish at the same time.

Who's singing it changes everything. Darius Rucker wrote the song with Lee Thomas Miller and John Osborne of Brothers Osborne, starting early in the pandemic, and it was the first thing he wrote about his divorce after 20 years of marriage. His co-writers heard a love song. He heard a breakup song. Both readings live in the lyric, and the song never makes you pick one. The verses list what this pair has come through: sunny days and straight-line wind, blue lights on back roads, dead quiet and rockstar loud. The pride in that list is real. So is the dread under it. A later verse slips in a line the rest of the song can't take back: "Knowing that day will come." Then the bridge admits why they're still standing: "That we don't know how to run." That isn't quite devotion. It's love that has outlasted the knowledge of how to leave, and Rucker lets the tenderness and the trap sit side by side.

The arrangement stays easy so the words can do the hurting. Brushed drums keep a steady beat, a walking bass line moves underneath, an acoustic guitar strums, and steel guitar holds long, sustained notes. The groove is relaxed. Ross Copperman's production is clean, with a natural room sound, the instruments balanced and the voice clear up front. Rucker sings conversationally, slightly raspy in the middle of his range, with a second voice blended in close harmony. It's contemporary Nashville through and through, polished and unhurried, and that plainness suits an artist who rose with Hootie & the Blowfish before building a country solo career. He doesn't oversell a single line.

Rucker called the writing "almost like therapy for me" and said the song was tough to sing. The structure shows the strain. The chorus comes around three times, each pass a little harder to believe, and at the end the setup line repeats and stalls before the payoff finally arrives, as if the singer needs one more breath before saying it.

It's a love song sung by someone who already knows how the story ends, and the gap between what the words swear and what the singer knows is the thing that keeps pulling you back.
