A chalk outline is what's left after the worst has happened: the shape of a body with nobody in it. **Chalk Outlines** takes that crime-scene image and makes it something you wear. The first verse opens in a bed the narrator crawled into, then reaches for the saddest kind of hope: "I hope that I'm someone else in the morning." Not better. Someone else. By the end of the verse a pill is being handed over, and the narrator is walking around inside a floating outline. Alive and accounted for, and not quite there.

The second verse names sertraline outright. Then comes the line that holds the song's bitter joke: "Don't cry 'cause there's a pill for everything." It sounds like something people say to you, kind and dismissive at once, and now the narrator is saying it to themselves. The chorus answers with a plan that isn't really a plan: push the feeling back down with a new habit, little by little, and bury it under a great big smile. Then "I" becomes "we," and the outlines turn into something people trace and erase together. The second time through, the last line loses its tail and ends on "Erase ourselves," as if the rest of it got swallowed.

The bridge says the fear plainly: "I'm scared of being okay, 'cause all things change." Feeling fine is its own threat, because fine never lasts. The medicated calm sits right beside a dread of losing it, and the song doesn't try to untie that knot. It calls the perfect day a beautiful shame and leaves it there.

The music keeps a steady hand, and that's the trick. Acoustic guitar strums over a simple, sustained bass line, and the drums hold a plain snare backbeat at a moderate, relaxed pace with a slight swing. The lead vocal is clear with a little rasp, conversational more than dramatic. The chorus fills out with layered voices, and the production stays clean and polished. A song about numbness that's this easy to sing along with hands you the great big smile and lets the words underneath do the crying.

Ren and Chinchilla wrote it together, continuing a partnership that goes back to "How to Be Me" in 2019. Ren co-produced with Russell Church and played guitar, keys and bass. Noah Haines is on drums, Georgia Harrington on cello, and Aled Way handled engineering and keys. The song belongs to Ren's run of confessional mental-health writing, but the duet changes its weight: with two voices, the "we" in the chorus stops sounding like a figure of speech.

It sticks because it gets you humming along to the exact feeling it describes: a calm that's afraid of itself.
