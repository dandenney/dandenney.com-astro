**The Roses** sounds closer to a confession than a performance. Joe Keogh sings in a mid-range that's a little raw at the edges, half-speaking the lines, the way you'd explain something across a kitchen table rather than from a stage. Underneath, a clean electric guitar picks out a simple arpeggio. The beat holds a steady, mid-tempo pace with a relaxed feel. The room is dry and the vocal is close-miked, so the voice has nowhere to hide. The song also pushes outward: a second, higher voice joins, the groove drives harder, and the arrangement fills out without losing that cramped, intimate feel.

The pull between holding back and pushing out matters, because this is the darkest song on Sunflower. Ahead of the release, Amber Run said the EP's songs are about family in all its forms: the one you're born into, the one you choose, and the people you love and lose. The title track was written as a love letter to Keogh's now-wife. "The Roses" comes from the same garden but shows the thorns. Melomaniacs called it the darkest point on the EP, a song driven by obsession and imbalance, and the sound fits that reading. A lopsided love rarely announces itself with a big chorus. It mutters and circles, in a level voice that keeps threatening to give.

Amber Run come out of the British indie tradition of emotive, anthemic, heart-on-sleeve songwriting, on the same shelf as Kodaline. The Nottingham trio formed in 2012: Keogh on vocals and guitar, Tom Sperring on bass, Henry Wyeth on keys. Their 2014 single "I Found" has passed half a billion streams, helped along by TikTok. "The Roses" bends that tradition. It keeps the emotional directness but runs tighter and more uneasy. When the sound swells, it plays less like catharsis than pressure building in a small room.

The band wrote it with Josh Noble and Karl Zeigler. Kev Jones produced it, and it came out on Tripel, the label behind 2023's How to Be Human. Keeping it dry and close was the right call. Reverb would have given the feeling somewhere to go, and the point is that it doesn't have anywhere.

It sticks because it lets you hear how calm someone can sound while the feeling underneath is too big for the room.
