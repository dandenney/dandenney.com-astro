The hook on **Lottery** is built out of conditions. "If I let you put your hands all over me" opens it, and three ifs stack up before the payoff, "You won the lottery," arrives. Even then it sounds like a promise she's still deciding whether to keep. That's the power move of the whole song: the prize is described in loving detail and never quite handed over. The man is holding a ticket. She runs the draw.

Lu Kala, the Congolese-Canadian singer, started writing that hook in March 2022, and Latto added her verses later. The hook teases and promises. Latto's verses lay out the terms. She opens with a laugh and a self-crowning, then stacks lottery puns: scratch-offs, jackpots, Mega Millions, a body turned into the winning number. Uproxx heard it as a set of rules for persistent men, and that's the right frame.

What lifts it above a pun parade is that the rules contradict each other, and Latto lets them. In one verse she wants a boss who doesn't care what the bill costs. Later: "You gotta work for this, can't purchase this." Both are true to her. She likes being spent on and refuses to be bought, and her pride lives in the gap between those positions. It's the voice of someone who knows her worth and won't let a wallet settle the question. The swagger works as a fence as much as a flirt.

Dr. Luke's production reunites him with Latto after her 2022 album 777, in the vein of the bright dancefloor pop he made with Doja Cat on "Say So." Latto has said exploring pop was fun, and that the bigger, more commercial beats had her second-guessing and re-cutting. She said the visuals flowed with the beat, matching its 70s disco flavor, and Chandler Lass's video, with its disco and casino sets and limo ride through Las Vegas, follows it there. The drums themselves are trap: kick, snare and hi-hats, a low sustained synth bass holding the roots, a higher synth repeating a short phrase. The groove is relaxed at a moderate tempo, with a slight swing. The mix is dry, clean and upfront, and Latto raps right on top of it, precise and confident, with no harmonies to soften her.

The song gave Lu Kala her first Billboard Hot 100 entry. It closes in the conditional where it began, with "If I let you hit this" and then one more line: "You know you hit the lotto, right?"

That last "right?" is the hook: it puts you in the seat of the person who decides, and it makes withholding sound like the best feeling in the room.
