Two people sing **You Don't Know Me** at each other, and the only thing they agree on is the chorus. Ben Folds and Regina Spektor trade the title line back and forth, often landing in unison or parallel thirds. So the harmony is doing something sly: two voices, perfectly matched, each accusing the other of never having listened. It's the most together this couple gets.

Folds wrote it, and the verse is his slow-dawning question. How can two people share a life "for so long" and never know what's going on in each other's heads? The images get sharper as the resentment builds. He pictures himself propped up like a mannequin or a cardboard stand-up, painted with whatever face the other person wanted. Then comes the line where the song stops being a gripe and turns on both of them: "It was we who were the cliché." He doesn't let himself off. They saw the couple in the coma and recognized themselves, and they "carried on anyway."

Later the feeling cracks open. Folds runs through the version of himself he thinks his partner holds, a clueless chump, an errant dog who needs a shorter leash, and then blurts the question underneath all of it: "Why the fuck would you want me back?" It's anger, but it's also hurt and a little pleading. The answer he offers himself, that maybe it's *because* they don't know him, is the bleakest joke in the song. Then the confession stalls. He can't get it out the way he means it, "'Cause I know you'll only change it," and the lyric gives up on words entirely, dissolving into a run of wordless ahs.

The sound keeps all this moving instead of sinking. A driving, arpeggiated piano figure with a strong bass line and a slight swing carries it at a moderate clip. Folds sings clear and a little nasal, like he's arguing across a kitchen table. Spektor's voice is breathier and sits higher, and the second voice keeps leaning in beside his, like someone who won't let a speech go unanswered. Folds said he called Spektor up with a part in mind, and Spektor added a lot he hadn't planned, which he liked. You can hear that give-and-take in how the two voices crowd each other.

This is classic Folds, the piano-pop of his Ben Folds Five years, smart-mouthed and melodic, paired with a guest from the anti-folk scene. He wrote part of the album, produced by Dennis Herring, as he finalized a two-year divorce, though he said the songs weren't topical and that the record was about being free. Fair enough. This one doesn't sound free yet. It sounds like the moment right before.

It sticks because the bounce lets you sing along to an argument nobody wins, and you're never quite sure which side you're on.
