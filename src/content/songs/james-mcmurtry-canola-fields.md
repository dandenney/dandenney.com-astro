A field of canola in Southern Alberta turns nearly the same chartreuse as a '69 Bug, and suddenly James McMurtry's narrator is back in San Jose, years younger, remembering someone he loved driving it around town. That's how **Canola Fields** opens, and it's a sharp picture of how memory works: not a decision to look back, but a color that ambushes you from the highway. McMurtry has said the song grew slowly out of those Alberta fields and a roadside sign advertising canola processing, phone number and all. It's the kind of mundane detail a good storyteller hoards until it earns a place.

What follows is a whole friendship told in road trips. There's an old white Lincoln with suicide doors and always room for more, then a white-knuckle ride back from Santa Cruz with a surfer who had her wrapped up. The narrator admits more than he means to: "And I coulda cut his throat." He blames the whiskey, insists they were never really a couple, then shuts the door twice: he doesn't want to think about that. The jealousy, the shame, and the self-defense all stay in the room together, unresolved, which is how old regret feels.

Later the song finds them in Brooklyn, after she's moved in with her sister, carrying her keys in her fist. That small, guarded detail does a lot of work. Everyone's lives, he says, were busting apart at the seams. On a cross-town bus they finally cash in a crush thirty years old, and McMurtry lands the line of the song: "You can't be young and do that." It's half rueful, half grateful. Youth would have wrecked it. The repetitions pile up as the song goes on, and the line starts to sound like something he's still convincing himself of. The chorus is a plain, scared plea to Marie: "Take a death grip on some part of me," to keep him from drifting far out to sea.

The band, tracked in June 2019 with Ross Hogarth producing, keeps out of the way. Fingerpicked acoustic guitar, a bass sticking to simple root notes, drums holding a steady, slightly swung mid-tempo beat. The room is dry and close. McMurtry's voice is gravelly and conversational, no harmonies, like a man telling you this across a kitchen table. It sits squarely in the Austin story-song tradition, literate and unhurried, and it opened his first album in about seven years. McMurtry named it one of his two favorites on the record, and it earned an Americana Song of the Year nomination.

What sticks is how the song lets loneliness and gratitude share one breath: you hear a man admit he's adrift, and still treasure the late, unlikely moment someone held on.
