"Let's pretend we don't exist / Let's pretend we're in Antarctica." It's a strange thing to offer someone you love: let's go somewhere empty and cold and vanish together. Kevin Barnes sings it like the best idea anyone ever had. On **Wraith Pinned to the Mist and Other Games**, disappearing isn't defeat. It's the honeymoon.

The verses do the dressing up. Barnes has said he packed them with literary references because the chorus was so simple, and you can hear the logic. A Satyr, a bride stripped bare, Tristan and Izolde, a title that echoes Longfellow's "A Wraith in the Mist." These are roles for two people to try on, games in the title's sense. The best line hides in the costume box: "make sure I see white sails." Even while playing the most doomed lovers he can think of, the narrator wants the version where the ship comes home happy.

That fear sits right beside the joy and never gets settled. The bridge imagines growing younger together forever, then admits it seems too lovely to be true, and answers that the best things always are. He flips the old warning into a promise, but the warning is still audible underneath. After that the chorus takes over and repeats again and again, as if saying it enough could make the hiding place real. The album's title comes from a dream Barnes's wife Nina had, in which the two of them were the only people on a planet called Sunlandia. This song is that planet with a backbeat.

The sound is clean and dry, close to the ear. A clean electric guitar picks out a repeating arpeggio, the bass walks underneath, and the drums push a steady, medium-fast beat on kick, snare and closed hi-hat. Barnes built the track with the drum software from Reason, recording most of *The Sunlandic Twins* on his own during 2004 in Athens, Georgia and Oslo, on a laptop while the band toured Europe, one instrument at a time. His voice is clear and medium-high, smooth and melodic. The song comes out of the Elephant Six orbit in Athens, but it sits at the point where Barnes turned toward bedroom-recorded, dance-leaning pop. Song Exploder's host calls it the band's breakout song.

The world found it. It turned up in *Cloverfield* and *Weeds*, and with its words changed it sold steaks for Outback. That's a funny fate for a song that asks to stop existing.

It gives you the giddy, slightly scared feeling of loving someone so much you'd happily vanish with them, and it keeps singing until you almost believe you could.
