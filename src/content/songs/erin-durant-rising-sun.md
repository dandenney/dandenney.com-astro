**Rising Sun** opens on a narrator greeting the weather like an old friend: "Hello wind, haven't seen you in years." It's a sweet, odd first line, and it sets the terms. This is someone who has been away from wide-open things, staying put and learning rituals, including how to truly love another. The early verses carry a hopeful, slightly sheepish pride: mistakes admitted, a vow to go far, go wide, head back to the sunny side. It sounds like someone talking themselves into it.

Then there's the house. Durant was born and raised in New Orleans, and the title, together with a house in New Orleans, calls up the old folk song "House of the Rising Sun." Durant bends it. This house is a green California bungalow on Moss Street, one the narrator has never seen, and it promises to take them somewhere they've never been. It becomes an imagined refuge. When the song turns to the river and the sea, the land of the rising sun hands out absolution instead of a warning: "Don't matter what you have done."

The absolution doesn't hold. RNZ's Song Crush heard the song start optimistic and slowly fall apart over almost six minutes, and the lyrics show how. The future verse comes around twice, and both times the narrator calls out to the future and hears "no way." The last thing asked for is small and heavy: "Just want you to hold me till my time is up." Tired, a little drunk, the narrator lets the big talk of going wide shrink down to one person's arms. The hope and the defeat are both still in the song, side by side, and neither wins.

The sound keeps all of this close. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through a clean, bright arpeggio at a slow, gentle pace. Durant's voice is clear and a little breathy, with no harmony to soften it. The delivery is conversational, which makes the confessions land harder. It feels like a small room. One blog that called the song the album's raison d'être also heard muted toms and strung-out trumpets around that strolling guitar. That fits what *Islands* does more broadly: it takes the minimal piano folk of Durant's 2016 debut, *Blueberry Mountain*, into warmer, fuller arrangements under producer Kyp Malone of TV on the Radio.

Durant has said New Orleans music is part of her and that she wanted *Islands* to feel a little like sand, wood and water. Set against that upbringing, a house in New Orleans the narrator has never seen is a strange, pointed detail, a hometown held at arm's length.

It sticks because it lets you watch someone reach for a whole new life and then settle, honestly and without shame, for being held.
