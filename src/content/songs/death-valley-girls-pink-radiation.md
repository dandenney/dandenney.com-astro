**Pink Radiation** is built on a held chord. A distorted electric guitar sustains it while a second guitar picks out a melodic line on top, the bass stays on root notes, and the drums push a medium-fast beat with the snare sitting right at the front. Over that, one voice sings high and sustained, raw at the edges, with no harmony to soften it. The reverb on the voice makes the room feel large, but the grit stays. The parts lock into a pattern and hold it, and that steadiness is where the song gets its pull.

There's a reason it sounds like a summons. Death Valley Girls have said they made these songs to introduce rock & roll to the dead, then decided to introduce it to the living instead, and that **Glow in the Dark** was recorded as a call to arms. That's a joke and a mission statement at once, and this track takes the mission part seriously. The single unharmonized voice, stretched over a beat that won't let up, sounds less like a performance and more like calling out across a long distance and expecting to be heard.

The circumstances help. Bonnie Bloomgarden, credited as the song's writer, said the album was cut over two days with engineer Mark Rains at Station House Studio in Echo Park, and described the experience as kind of cosmic. You can hear that speed in how little the track is fussed over. Nothing is polished down, and the thick, gritty texture keeps the edges showing. Glow in the Dark was the band's second full-length and documented a changing lineup, with Alana Amram arriving on bass and Jessie Jones joining to share vocals with Bloomgarden. The credits also list Larry Schemel and Laura Kelsey.

The record first came out on Burger Records, and the band sits in the Los Angeles garage and punk underground. One interviewer called them a proto-punk, rock 'n' roll gang. That fits. The song leans on repetition and conviction more than on a clever arrangement, and it trusts that a steady pulse, held with total commitment, can carry more feeling than a lot of moving parts. At five minutes, the repetition has room to settle into a kind of trance instead of a quick blast.

What makes it stick is the devotion: one voice and a guitar line that won't quit, believing hard enough that you end up believing with them.
