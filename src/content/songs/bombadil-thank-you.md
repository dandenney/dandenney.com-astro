**Thank You** is a list of advice that keeps interrupting itself. It starts like a commencement speech: forget the ones who let you down, stay gracious toward the ones who stuck around, believe in yourself even if your own mother starts to doubt you. Then it tells you to write down everyone you love and immediately thinks better of it: "You might not want to tell all of them because that could weird some people out." That hedge is the whole song in small. It wants to say the big, open-hearted thing, and it can't stop noticing how awkward the big, open-hearted thing sounds out loud.

The humor never cancels the feeling. It's how the feeling gets through. When the song tells you to apologize to the one you love, it allows that he might not forgive you: "And if he doesn't, well, fuck him, you've done all you can." It's funny because it's honest about the limit of trying. The prayer verse works the same way. Pray every night, it says, though not necessarily to God, because it helps sort your thoughts, and then it adds that you never know. Then comes the line that drops the joking: "So, if I'm remembered, I'm remembered realistically." Asking whether you'd say nice things if the singer died tomorrow, or tell the truth, is a small, real fear: being loved for a version of yourself that isn't quite you. The song would rather be seen accurately than flattered.

Against all that hedging, the chorus is plain gratitude: thank you for staying by my side, what a wonderful feeling to know everything is fine. In the final run of the chorus the "wonderful feeling" lines pile up, as if the singer finally stopped qualifying and just let it repeat.

The sound is gentle. Fingerpicked acoustic guitar and a clean electric guitar roll through arpeggios over plucked bass and drums kept light with a brushed feel. The tempo is slow and relaxed. The lead voice is clear, with a light vibrato, and two-part harmony sits alongside it. The mix is warm, with a natural reverb. It's polished, but not fussy.

Bombadil made *Metrics of Affection* themselves over two months in the summer of 2012, in a big house in Durham, North Carolina. James Phillips engineered it at home, and he described the songs as being about approaching 30, relationships beginning and ending, and finding your place. The record followed Daniel Michalak's long nerve injury, which limited the use of his hands and left the band's future in doubt. The Salisbury Post singled this closer out as Stuart Robinson's song. Charlotte's Creative Loafing called it a summation of the band's most powerful parts. That fits the Durham folk-pop strain Bombadil works in: literate, whimsical, willing to be goofy right up to the point where it means it.

By the end, the song has talked itself out of its own embarrassment, and it leaves you wanting to thank somebody out loud, weird or not.
