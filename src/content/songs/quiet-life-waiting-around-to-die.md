The cruelest line in **Waiting Around to Die** isn't about death. It sits in the second verse, tossed off in a list of relatives: "He beat her with a belt once 'cause she cried." The mother heads to Tennessee, asks the father to take care of the kid, and the song moves on without a pause. That's the method of this Townes Van Zandt song. Terrible things get one line each, and the narrator keeps walking.

Quiet Life, the Americana band born in New London, Connecticut and settled in Portland, Oregon, close their Housebroken Man EP with it. Adam Selzer recorded it at Type Foundry in Portland, and the band doesn't try to roughen it up. An acoustic guitar fingerpicks a steady arpeggio. The drums keep a slow, simple beat with brushes on the snare. The walking bass line comes from Jim James of My Morning Jacket. The recording is dry and clean, the voice high and a little breathy, the groove slow and relaxed. Nothing in the band flinches at what the words are saying, and that calm is what lets the words land.

The refrain carries the song. "It's easier than just a-waitin' 'round to die" is the narrator's excuse for everything: the gambling and the booze, the girl in a Tuscaloosa bar who cleaned him out, the wine and the train, the robbery that ends with a posse dragging him back to Muskogee. Each verse is another escape, defended the same way. There's a drifter's pride in that line, a shrug, and under it someone who has never had a reason to stay anywhere. By the prison verse the refrain stops being a choice. The waiting is just two long years of it.

Then the last verse turns the whole song. Out of prison, he finally has a friend who doesn't steal or cheat or drink or lie. "His name's Codeine, he's the nicest thing I've seen." It plays like a joke, and it's the saddest thing in the song, because his idea of kindness is built from everyone who failed him. He's done running. The plan now is to wait.

Frontman Sean W. Spellman has said the songs on the EP were written when a lot was in flux, at rough times in certain relationships. This one is borrowed, and Quiet Life play it gently, which is the right call. The softness keeps you sitting next to the man instead of looking down at him.

It sticks because a man describes his whole ruined life in the same gentle, unhurried voice he'd use to introduce a friend, and by the end you realize that's exactly what he's doing.
