**Sun Bear** opens with a grievance that sounds like it has been rehearsed in the dark: "Nobody told me how I'd feel at 25." Then Eleni Drake goes down the list of who failed to warn her: mother, father, sister. The verdict is blunt and a little unfair, the way it is when you're tired and nobody's listening. It's the first line of the first track on her debut album, *Can't Stop The Dawn*, and it sets the terms. Drake has said the record was written mainly for herself rather than about other people, unlike her earlier EPs. You can hear that here. The resentment points outward, at friends, lovers, and an undiscovered god, but the fear underneath points straight back in: she's terrified she isn't living the way she'd like.

The music doesn't match that fear with drama. An acoustic guitar picks out arpeggiated chords with a clean, bright tone. A bass holds simple, sustained notes, and brushed drums keep a slow, gentle pulse. Drake sings alone, low and breathy, with no harmonies to soften anything. The room sounds natural and warm, polished but still a bit raw. She wrote, composed, and produced it herself, which fits the contemporary UK indie and bedroom-pop lane she works in: guitar-led, and built so it feels like one person talking to themselves.

What makes the song sting is how its feelings refuse to settle. She retreats to a garden to tell herself it's alright, then admits she hates it when she cries, comparing the tears to rain in mid-July. They're sudden and unwelcome, and still a kind of relief. Later she blames herself, shrugs off resolutions she says she won't see out, and claims she doesn't care at all. It's the kind of shrug people make when they care too much. Then comes the image that names the song: "Like a sun bear I just wanna dance alone." It's a strange, tender picture, an animal doing something joyful with no one watching. In the same breath she wants to resolve all her flaws, and she still cries anyway. Self-acceptance and self-correction sit side by side, and neither one wins.

Drake has described this album as sadder than its follow-up, *Surf The Sun*, and that's easy to believe. But the lyric closes on a repeated mantra, "There's a life to be lived," paired with a life to be loved. It sounds less like a conclusion than like something you say out loud until you half believe it.

It sticks because it captures that exact moment when you're crying about your life and coaching yourself through it at the same time, and it lets both be true.
