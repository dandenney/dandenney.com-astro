The detail that sets everything up comes early: when Dad gets home from an eleven-hour shift, the kid loses the TV. Nobody is cruel in **"In My Room."** Mom is stressed trying to make sure everyone eats, big brother is numbing out on the PlayStation, and the house is too full of tired people for a quiet kid to take up space. So he goes to his room.

Nick Carpenter, who records as Medium Build, told the Tennessean the song grew out of a writing prompt about his childhood bedroom. He described himself back then as a passive kid whose parents were chaotic church people. The song doesn't argue with that house. It just lists what happens behind the door, and the list is funny, embarrassing and honest at once: a Simpsons poster, a Weird Al tape, learning guitar, "Makin' action figures kiss." That line is a child rehearsing tenderness with the only company that won't laugh at him. The second verse turns the room outward. There's a friend, a bus ride the moms arranged, melted cheese on chips, a Matrix quote deployed to seem interesting. Then comes the nervous request to sleep over and talk about life all night, and you can hear how much rides on a yes.

The sound keeps all of this close. Fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls in arpeggios over a bass that mostly holds the root. The drums play kick, snare and hi-hat, with a ride cymbal in the mix. The tempo is medium and the groove relaxed with a slight swing. The mix is clean, with a little reverb on a single voice and no harmonies. Carpenter sings smooth and gentle, never pleading, which suits a kid who learned not to make noise. It sits in the indie-folk singer-songwriter lane he built between Anchorage's DIY scene and Nashville. He wrote it with Carrie K and Jake LiBassi, who is also credited as Laiko. Carrie K and Laiko produced it, and it became track two on the album Country, recorded on Music Row.

Then the last verse does something quietly crushing. After "I am sacred, I am safe, I am free," and a line about dreaming up who he wants to be, the song stops saying "I." It describes "a kid" from the outside: he tried on all his brother's clothes, hates how he looks, is bad at saying no, works hard so none of it shows. The narrator can only look at that boy from a distance. The first verse wondered if he could ever get rich enough to never feel alone at home. The answer is softer and sadder: "And sometimes he's alone when he's at home."

You come away remembering whatever room you hid in, and finally hearing what the kid inside it couldn't say.
