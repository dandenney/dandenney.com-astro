In **Seneca Creek**, the water comes up. It's 1985, and the narrator names what the flood carried off: Gandee, North Fork, South Branch. Then comes the plainest line in the song: "But it only took the other side." The house he built is still standing. There's relief in that, and pride, and something quieter underneath, the knowledge that luck sorted his family from the neighbors'. The song doesn't stop to weigh it. It moves on.

Charles Wesley Godwin wrote it about his grandparents, in his grandfather's voice, and calls it the song nearest and dearest to him on Seneca, an album he wrote alone. It opens in the spring of 1949 with a girl named Ruth washing her feet in the creek and a young man promising half a karat. She answers that no stone can match the creek's shine, and that's the marriage in miniature: he wants to provide, she already has what she wants. Korea takes four lines, mud and retreats and every boy from his hometown dead, and then he's coming home with money in his pocket. The grief gets said fast and set down. The plan is what he'd rather talk about.

The plan is the chorus. The first time, it's an invitation, all let's and we'll. Near the end the same words return in the past tense, sung toward a grave on the hill above the water. Only the grammar changes, and that's where it hurts. Between those choruses sits a shrug that admits hard years without blaming anyone, "You loved yours, I did the same," and then the illness, when the two of them invent trips for the hell of it and he offers, "I'll take you back to any year." The pride and the grief never separate. A promise kept is still a promise that ends.

The sound stays out of the story's way. Fingerpicked acoustic guitar carries it, with a second guitar strumming, a simple bass line and a kit keeping an easy beat at a slow tempo. The voice is clear and calm, with no harmonies around it. Eric Heywood is credited on pedal steel; Godwin chose him after hearing him on a live take of Ray LaMontagne's "Empty," reaching him through Jeffrey Foucault. Al Torrence produced the record in an 1890s Victorian house turned studio in New Brighton, Pennsylvania, and it has that clean, warm, natural-room feel. A Morgantown native writing Appalachian songs from the Midwest, Godwin keeps the place names specific, and that specificity is the song's version of devotion.

It's a love song told as a ledger of what held, and when the chorus comes back in the past tense you feel a whole life fold into one changed verb.
