A reprise is a second look at something you thought you'd finished. **Alone (Reprise)** sits at the center of Ryan Harris's seven-song *Same Sun* EP, track four, right after "Alone." That placement matters. Harris circles back almost immediately, with the dust barely settled. The song runs under two minutes, which makes it feel less like a second song and more like the thought you keep having after you've said what you meant to say.

Harris has been plain about what drives the EP. He calls it personal, and describes spending his early life looking for outside fixes to inside problems, then coming to see that those problems travel with you wherever you go. That idea fits a reprise. You leave the subject, and it's waiting at the next track. The feeling that idea suggests isn't panic or heartbreak. It's quieter: recognizing that the weight you hoped to set down is still in your hands.

The sound carries that steadiness. An acoustic guitar runs a fingerpicked, arpeggiated pattern, rolling and unhurried. A bass holds simple, sustained notes underneath, more anchor than melody. The drums play light, with a slight swing, kick, snare and hi-hat keeping a moderate pulse. Harris sings in a clear mid-range, smooth and melodic, with no harmonies stacked behind. One voice, alone, which suits the title without overplaying it. The song doesn't dramatize being alone. It stays in the room with it.

That room is open and clean, with a little reverb on the voice and the instruments. Harris makes his music in a bedroom or home studio in Whistler, BC, and Tyler Neil Johnson is credited with production and engineering. The polish is the gentle kind. Harris's own pages put him beside Ben Howard, Jack Johnson, Ocie Elliott and Gregory Alan Isakov, and you can hear the bedroom-folk lineage in that easy, swung drumming and the uncluttered guitar. What he borrows is the calm. What he does with it is aim that calm at an idea that's hard to face.

Harris started writing folk songs in 2021 for his own films. This one plays like a held shot, the camera staying on someone after everyone else has left the frame.

It sticks because it sounds like making peace with a problem you can't outrun, and the steadiness lets you admit you're carrying one too.
