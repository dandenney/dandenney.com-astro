There's a runner in the chorus of **Strong**. Charles Wesley Godwin names Prefontaine, puts him in the outside lane coming around the bend, and asks you to picture yourself there: behind, worn out, kicking anyway. It's an unusual figure for a West Virginia country-folk song to lean on, and it works because a race is something you finish with your body. Nobody talks their way down the last straightaway.

The song is sung to "you," and Godwin has said who that is. He called it a call to himself and to listeners, to stand strong when life throws bombs and to fight for who you want to be. That doubling matters. The verses read like a pep talk about breaking off the old tracks and cutting a new path, but the chorus slips in the line that makes it tender: "I know your nights been long." That isn't a coach yelling from the infield. It's someone who has been awake at the same hours.

The second verse is where the writing earns its keep. Godwin takes failure and reprices it, so every miss becomes a payment on dues everyone owes. Then he goes after the shame of being broke: "Your empty coffers aren't a sign / That you haven't saved for a whole lifetime." What he means by saving isn't money. It's a defense of a life measured in effort, and he doesn't pretend the account ends well. The verse walks straight to a cold dark grave, and the best it offers is that nobody can say you didn't live brave. Pride and grief share the same lines, and neither one cancels the other. Born to pain and strain, leaving with grit fixed to your name: that's the whole inheritance, and the song holds it up proudly anyway.

The band plays it steady rather than triumphant. An acoustic guitar strums the floor, a clean electric guitar picks an arpeggiated line over it, and the bass walks under a snare backbeat at a moderate, relaxed shuffle. Godwin sings in a clear baritone with a little rasp, earnest and unharmonized, one voice doing the talking. Al Torrence produced and mixed the album it sits on, and the finish is polished and clean while still leaving room for that grain.

It came out of an upended stretch. Godwin made How the Mighty Fall after the pandemic stopped his touring, during a time that also brought his son's birth and a move back to West Virginia. The album trades the autobiography of his debut, Seneca, for characters facing mortality, hope and regret, and "Strong" is where that turns toward encouragement.

It sticks because it never promises you'll win, only that somebody sees you still running, and on a long night that's the thing you needed to hear.
