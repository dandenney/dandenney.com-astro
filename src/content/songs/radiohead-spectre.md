The first thing Thom Yorke sings on **Spectre** is a confession, not a threat: "I'm lost, I'm a ghost." There's no swagger in it. It comes in falsetto, high and breathy, each note held long while the melody barely moves, circling a few pitches like someone pacing a small room. Underneath, minor piano chords roll out in slow arpeggios. Strings swell and pull back around them, breathing in and out. There are no drums. Nothing pushes the song forward. It just hangs in the air.

It was written to be a Bond theme, and it never became one. Radiohead made it for the 2015 film after an earlier try, "Man of War," was turned down, and this one didn't make it either. Jonny Greenwood said it was rejected for being "too dark," and Sam Smith's "Writing's on the Wall" took the slot. Nigel Godrich, who produced it, later called the whole episode a "real waste of energy" that cut into work on A Moon Shaped Pool. The band gave the song away on SoundCloud on Christmas Day, their first release since 2011, and Pitchfork called it one of their finest songs in years.

The darkness Greenwood mentioned lives less in the arrangement than in where the song stands. This one sings from inside the danger. The narrator is emptied out and occupied by something else, with a hunger that burns through like a bullet. Rumour, suspicion, anger and fear pile up like symptoms. Against all of that there's exactly one solid thing, a kiss: "Is when you put your lips to me." It's the tenderest line here and the shakiest one, because a few lines later fear has them second-guessing even love.

Watch what happens when the lines come back. They return almost word for word, but "could see" turns into "can see." Where the lover's lips used to be, the answer is now "Spectre has come for me." The one truth has changed hands. Love was what the narrator held onto. By the last line, the only sure thing left is the thing that's hunting them. Nothing in the delivery sounds alarmed. The vocal is breathy and doubled, and the strings just rise and fall, as if the narrator had known all along this was coming.

That calm is where the grief sits. A rejected spy theme turns out to be a slow song about losing your grip on the one person who made sense, and it holds you there with the narrator, unafraid and undone at once, as the dark finally arrives.
