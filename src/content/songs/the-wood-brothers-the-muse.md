Three rooms, three times the same seat. **The Muse** opens with a narrator perched on "this never-made bed," moves him to the end of a dirty old bar, and finally sets him down in a hospital room. Each verse closes on the same hedged prayer, "Thank God, whoever you are," a thank-you sent to no fixed address. That phrase carries the whole song. The gratitude is real, but it comes out sideways. It's tucked inside a conditional (*if I was thinking, I'd be thinking*), and the refrain explains why: thinking is the last thing you want to do. Some moments are too good to look at directly. Others are too bad.

The middle verse is where the song earns its sweetness. The muse who stood in the doorway brushing her hair is now "all alone on the other side of town," and what he thanks God for is the whiskey. The refrain turns from sweet to sad but keeps calling it true, and he doesn't apologize or explain. Oliver Wood told Billboard that a muse can be positive or very dark, and he declined to give specifics about the inspiration. The song doesn't give them either. It just lets the bar sit between the bedroom and the delivery room, so the joy at the end feels like something that was in doubt.

Then the third verse lands. He's "shedding a tear for the bride and groom," grieving the couple they were even as the baby starts to cry. And then: "It's my finest work yet, if today I should die." It's a songwriter's boast and a new parent's terror in one breath, pride and mortality holding hands. The label called it Oliver Wood's most tender and autobiographical writing yet, and you can hear him reach for a craftsman's word, *work*, because the plain feeling is too big.

The band said the song had to be spare, with no drums and no beat, so the music could follow the words. That's what you get. A fingerpicked acoustic pattern rolls in slow arpeggios over a steady bass, and the groove sways rather than pushes. Wood's voice is clear and slightly raspy, relaxed enough to sound like he's singing it to the room. The sound around it is warm and close, a small room with nothing between you and the strings. Buddy Miller produced the album at Southern Ground in Nashville, with the trio circled around a tree of microphones and the lead vocals cut on the spot. The Wood Brothers sit in the American roots, folk and blues tradition, and here the folk side comes forward, at its plainest.

It sticks because it gives you permission to feel grateful without understanding, to thank whoever's listening for the good years and the bad nights alike.
