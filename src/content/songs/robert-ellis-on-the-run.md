Robert Ellis sings about running with almost nothing moving. **On The Run** opens in the stretches of West Texas too empty to have a name, where the narrator gases up every time a chance comes. Then it sets that flight against a slow, fingerpicked guitar figure, bright and clear. The voice is close and dry, smooth and unadorned, as if the whole song were happening in a small room with the door shut. That gap between the subject and the sound is where the feeling lives.

The early images are heat and pressure: summer vapor rising from the road, and the narrator "Strapped into a fuselage and waiting to explode." That line holds the machine, the body and the nerves at once. Later the enemy has no name either, the money is running out, and the real fear surfaces: that this never ends, and that accepting anything "preordained" would feel like giving in. The song is slow to say what's being fled. It doesn't rush.

What makes it ache is that the narrator half-knows the running is pointless and keeps going anyway. The repeated couplet admits that distance changes nothing. You still land in the one place you really know. Then comes the defiance: "If heaven feels like standing still I'd rather go to hell." There's pride in that line, and a kind of terror. The narrator fears the silence at the bottom of the well and fears becoming one more book on a shelf. The plainest confession comes after: "We are running from each other, we are running from ourselves." The "we" matters. Somebody else is along, and the flight is shared, which makes it lonelier, not less.

This is Ellis's sixth solo record and a sharp swerve from the piano-rock of 2019's *Texas Piano Man*. *Yesterday's News* was cut live to tape in two days and produced at Niles City Sound, the Fort Worth studio where he's a partner, with nylon-string guitar, Aden Bubek's upright bass and little else. Critics reached for Nick Drake, Mark Kozelek and Springsteen's *Nebraska*, and the Townes Van Zandt line of hushed Texas songwriting fits too. Ellis has said he spent his career making records he thought others would find "exciting," while the albums he loves are small, gentle and soft, and that stillness was these songs' strength. Here the stillness carries the argument: a restless heart, set down in the quietest room available.

The song closes on its title three times over, and the calm in the voice tells you the narrator has already understood what the words won't concede: you can't outdrive yourself.
