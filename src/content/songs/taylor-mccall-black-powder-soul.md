The detail that sells **Black Powder Soul** is the oddly specific one: "No I don't play poker since that stint in Spokane." It's a joke with a scar under it. Taylor McCall's narrator gives you the cause, bootleg moonshine and bathtub gin, but not the story, and the habit he changed tells you it went badly. The verse closes on a line that cuts two ways: "That's why my over under is my only friend." An over-under is a stacked double-barrel shotgun. It's also a betting line. Either way, the only thing this man trusts is something that can go off.

That's the black powder of the title. It's old, unstable, and dangerous to carry. The narrator wears it like an identity, lawless and proud of it, but the chorus tells on him. "My heart is burning / My blood runs cold" is swagger and dread in the same breath. He's hot-tempered and scared, and he doesn't get to pick one. The dark clouds above and the fire below put him between heaven and hell with no room to stand. The second verse makes the trap literal. You can run the tables, but you can't outrun what's waiting, and when "there ain't no running east or west," blood money is what's left to eat on.

The song never resolves that, and McCall seems to know it shouldn't. He has called the album a circle of life and said everyone has a black powder soul, something that can explode but also holds redemption. He sequenced the tracks as a Biblical chronology, and he bookended them with an old recording of his grandfather singing the hymn "Old Ship of Zion." Inside that frame, this song reads as the man before any rescue arrives. It's a Saturday-night soul inside a Sunday-morning structure.

McCall co-wrote it with Dave Pahanish and said it was the hardest song he ever wrote, three days of work. The economy shows. The narrator's whole history comes through in hints: a city, a drink, a gun, a card game gone wrong. The album was cut at Silent Desert Studio in Nolensville, Tennessee, with Sean McConnell producing. The South Carolina native, who studied fisheries and wildlife management in Montana before he turned to music, lands in the darker corner of Americana, where God and the devil share a bar stool. Holler heard Steve Earle in that territory, and the outlaw-with-a-conscience fits.

The song lets you feel what it's like to be proud of the very thing that's going to blow you apart.
