The only friend the narrator of **Black Powder Soul** admits to is a gun. "That's why my over under is my only friend," Taylor McCall sings, and the phrase does double work. An over-under is a shotgun. It's also a betting term, which fits a man who hasn't played poker since a stint in Spokane, one that started over bootleg moonshine and bathtub gin. The first verse sets him up like a figure from an old ballad: a soldier with no war to fight, a man outside the law. It plays as bragging until that last line. All the swagger lands on loneliness.

The chorus is where the pride splits open. "My heart is burning / My blood runs cold" isn't a contradiction the song tries to settle. He's hot and cold at once, dark clouds overhead and fire below, and the title names what that makes him: black powder, the old unstable stuff that sits quiet until a spark finds it. McCall has described the album as a circle in which everyone carries a soul like that, one that can explode but can also be redeemed. This song keeps the first half of that promise and holds back the second. By the second verse there "ain't no running east or west," and the hustlers and blood money close in like weather.

McCall has said it was the hardest song he ever wrote, three days of work. He had the verses first. When he described the song to co-writer Dave Pahanish, Pahanish answered with the phrase "Black Powder Soul." A man this boxed in by his own story needed somebody else to name it.

Sean McConnell produced, and the album was made over two weeks in his home studio. The band keeps things patient: a clean electric guitar picking out arpeggios, bass holding the root, a slow, relaxed groove with the snare cracking on the backbeat, and a second guitar laying a sustained, distorted line underneath like smoke. The voice is mid-range with a rasp at the edges, recorded dry and close, so the lines land like a man talking across a table.

Holler compares McCall to Steve Earle and places the record among the edgier, good-versus-evil strains of country, and the inheritance is plain: an outlaw self-portrait that's half confession. The album opens with McCall's late grandfather and his teenage mother, who sang in a traveling gospel group, performing "Old Ship of Zion," and this song answers that hymn from the sinner's side.

It sticks because it puts you inside a man who is burning and freezing at once, proud of the danger in himself and afraid of it, and it never tells you where the spark will land.
