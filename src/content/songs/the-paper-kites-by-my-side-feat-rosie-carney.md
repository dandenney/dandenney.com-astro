"And if I ran then I would run to you." That line in **By My Side** does a lot of quiet work. It admits that running has crossed the narrator's mind, then bends the escape back into the promise. Even the getaway has a destination. The whole song works the same way: love declared through a pile of wants, stacked one on top of another until they start to sound like a vow.

Count how often the lyric says "I want." To take you in my arms, to talk with you, to vanish in the night with you. Then comes the little tag "I do," tacked onto the ends of lines like a reassurance, as if the singer is answering a doubt nobody voiced out loud, maybe their own. Nothing here is clever, and that's the point. Dressing it up would be a way to hide.

The best line is the most modest one: "I'm gonna make some kind of life for you / Any way I can." "Some kind" is an honest phrase. There's no plan and no picture of the house, just willingness, plus a quiet admission that the means might be thin. Pride and uncertainty share the same breath. The chorus pares things down further, the title phrase answered by short fragments, as if full sentences are more than the feeling needs.

The music matches that restraint. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar turns over an arpeggio while piano holds long, sustained chords underneath. The tempo is slow, the groove gentle and flowing. The lead voice is soft and breathy, close to the ear, with a slight melancholy that keeps the devotion from tipping into sweetness. Everything is clean and polished. Sam Bentley and Tom Iansek produced *Roses*, and Iansek recorded the track. Critic George Graham heard it as the album's closing track, a melancholy, piano-based love song, with a little steel guitar that sets it apart from the rest of the record.

*Roses* is, in Bentley's words, "an album of duets," and he has said he wrote its songs after listening closely to the guests' records, aiming for material they could sing well. That suggests this one was written with Rosie Carney, the Irish singer-songwriter, in mind. She recorded her part on the floor of her London flat during a hard lockdown, worried because she wasn't used to working without an engineer, then sent the vocals to the band. That gives the song a strange, tender backstory: a duet about wanting someone by your side, finished by people who couldn't share a room. It's the Melbourne indie-folk band's understated style turned toward distance, longing carried by two parties apart.

The song sticks because it never claims to know how love will work out, only that the wanting is real, and it makes "any way I can" sound like the most generous promise you could hear.
