**I Don't Recall** opens with a count, one two three, as if the band were about to start a happy song. Then the first claim lands: "I can't remember anything at all." It's a strange thing to sing over a tune built to stay in your head. The narrator says the past has been wiped clean, yet every line that follows is memory: the life they knew, the love they shared, the person who is gone. The amnesia is a defense, and the song shows that it doesn't work.

Look at how the second verse turns. After insisting on forgetting, the narrator admits "And all the love we shared is true." That isn't someone who can't recall. It's someone who recalls too well and would rather not. The verse also makes room for the other person, for what they lost inside themselves, so the grief isn't only the narrator's. Both people lost something, and the song keeps that in view. Then comes the line that sums it up: "But it's hard to go." The life is over, and leaving it is still hard. Nothing gets resolved. The last line sets loss and longing side by side one more time, with the life they shared gone and things still hard without the other person.

The arrangement is plain. Arpeggiated acoustic guitar rings clean and bright. Brushes on the snare keep a soft, steady beat. The voice is clear and high, held in long, smooth lines, and a second voice sings harmony with it. Everything sits in a modest room with natural reverb, polished but open. The calm is the trick. A song this gentle about a loss this complete sounds like someone keeping their composure on purpose.

It's the third track on *Incorruptible Heart*, Lavender Diamond's second album, which followed *Imagine Our Love* on Matador in 2007. Becky Stark had piled up a huge number of songs. Her childhood friend Damian Kulash of OK Go, who also went to Brown, suggested she treat them as one batch and offered to produce. He also played on the record, and he shares the writing credit here with Stark, Steve Gregoropoulos, Ronald Rege Jr. and Jeff Rosenberg. Dave Fridmann of the Flaming Lips mixed it. The album draws on 1950s pop, disco and electronic sounds, and this song uses the plainest part of that range: brushed drums, close harmony, a melody you could hum after one listen.

The band later released a video directed by Jena Malone, inspired by Andrew Wyeth's *Christina's World*. They described it as taking a lighthearted, humorous tone toward the therapeutic side of dealing with a broken heart. That fits. The song never wallows. It tries to get better and gets caught telling on itself.

It sticks because you hear someone say they've forgotten and then spend the rest of the song proving they haven't, and the sweet tune makes the lie easy to forgive.
