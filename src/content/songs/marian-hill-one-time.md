The whole song hangs on one odd, perfect metaphor. **One Time** doesn't use the usual figures for being loved and left, the toy or the game or the notch on a bedpost. It picks an instrument instead: "But I'm not the kind of drum you play one time." A drum is built to be hit, and the narrator doesn't pretend otherwise. She isn't refusing the attention. She's refusing the brevity. The line is half warning and half invitation, and it lands as both every time it comes back around, which is often.

The verses are built as a mirror. The first one sizes him up: the charm that "makes the girls all swing," the taste for the hit and run, the knowing. The second flips every pronoun. Now it's "I know," her sway, her read on "the way that men can be." Then comes the line that gives the game away: "I like it smooth and slow, give it some time to grow." Under the poise is plain want. She wants something that lasts, and she says so.

That's what keeps the song from being a simple kiss-off. The refrain opens with "Boy, if you wanna go I would not mind," which is a shrug, and a shrug repeated over and over stops looking like indifference. Pride and longing sit in the same breath and never settle. She'll let him leave. She just needs him to know what he's leaving. The song understands that you can mean both halves at once.

Jeremy Lloyd, the duo's producer, keeps the record almost bare. A simple, staccato synth bass and a drum machine set up a steady hip-hop groove, and the mix is clean and dry, close enough to feel like a room with the door shut. Samantha Gongol's voice is clear and breathy. She half-raps the verses with a melodic lilt, then layers the vocal on the hook so "one time" comes back doubled. Sway was mixed entirely in Propellerhead Reason, and the restraint shows. Nothing is there that the vocal doesn't need.

The lyric's back half is where that restraint pays off. The words splinter into stuttered "pl-pl-play" and long runs of "one, one, one," the phrase chopped until it turns into rhythm. Here the singer becomes the drum. Press tied the Philadelphia duo to a meeting of 1920s jazz and electronica. Rolling Stone heard this song as bluesy and speakeasy-like, and for its live session the duo brought in saxophonist Steve Davit. It was the first Marian Hill song to catch on, carried by a big Vine and by crowds who sang it back at early shows, and it later went Gold.

It sticks because it lets you be cool and hungry in the same line, and saying "one time" over and over turns into a quiet way of asking for more.
