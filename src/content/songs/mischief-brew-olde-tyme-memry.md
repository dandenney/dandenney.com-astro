The first line is a pun so old it creaks: **"Olde Tyme Mem'ry"** opens with "When Father bought the farm, we sold the farm," and the joke turns on you before you finish smiling. A man died, and the family cashed out. The next line names the shame without dressing it up: "Mistook his blood for rustic charm." The narrator doesn't blame the city first. The narrator starts with what *we* did.

That admission is what keeps the song from being a simple grumble about kids who can't hold a spade. Erik F. Petersen, who wrote it, sets much of it in a bar. The barman gets a real argument, telling the narrator that everyone's living in the past and asking why anyone should keep a dying craft alive instead of ending its misery. Petersen lets that stand. The reply isn't a rebuttal. It's a battered hand held out and a plea to be heard. Grief and stubbornness sit together here, and neither wins.

The satire is sharp and very funny. There's a gas stove in a second home made up to look like oak, even giving off smoke and glowing embers, and a Home Sweet Home quilt paired with wise words from Thoreau. Then the narrator admits to being called a throwback and to crying while remembering. That's the bruise under the jokes. By the later verses the narrator has become the antique, asking to be locked behind plastic glass, a eulogy that will get sold off as folk art. The chorus keeps landing on one image of a lost honesty: "Of a day when wood was wooden, silver-silver, gold was gold." The water in the whiskey and brass passed off as gold make it a drinking song about being cheated, and about doing some of the cheating yourself.

The sound fits the barroom. A bright, strummed acoustic guitar carries a rough, conversational voice. A walking bass line and plain, steady drums fall in behind it at a lively clip, and the room feels live and unvarnished. The chorus fills out with more voice behind it, like the regulars joining in. Petersen has said he played all the instruments on the early Mischief Brew recordings, drums included, so that full-band barroom was one person's doing.

The lineage is easy to hear. Petersen drew on Woody Guthrie and Crass, and the song sits in the Philadelphia DIY folk-punk tradition, where acoustic protest-song storytelling meets punk ethos. It first came out in 2003 on a split with Robert Blake. It was remastered from the original 2002 analog tapes for this 2016 collection, months before Petersen died that July. A song about craft being boxed up as memorabilia ended up as a recovered artifact itself.

It sticks because the narrator mourns a father and a whole way of living while admitting a share in selling both, so the chorus feels less like nostalgia than a confession you can raise a glass to.
