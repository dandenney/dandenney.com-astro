The song opens with a body check. **Julia** starts with "Straighten my sweater, fix my spine," a narrator pulling himself together in a food line, high, rattled, and about to order from his favourite waitress. It's a funny, small scene. He doesn't know what he asked for. She's smiling like he can't afford it. Mt. Joy plays the crush for laughs at first, and the laughs make what comes next land harder.

The song widens fast. The narrator is addicted to TED Talks, waiting for lift-off, lost "in the fluorescent light" of his own life. By the chorus he's no longer flirting. He's pleading. He asks Julia to fix him a blue sky and then asks how long until he's all gone. Matt Quinn has called this the closest thing to a love song on the band's 2018 debut. He says it's about whether a relationship can work while your life is changing drastically, and that it's hopeful. The hope is real, but it shares space with a fear of disappearing. Then comes the line that gives the song away: "Don't say you loved the old me." He's asking her not to hold onto someone he has already stopped being, and maybe he's afraid she will anyway.

Later the song drifts into imagery of Spanish moss, silver moonlight in the trees, and the devil in his bloodstream. Its refrain has him stumbling down Elysian Fields, a name that suggests paradise, for a walk that feels anything but settled. He admits he doesn't know where home is. The closing lines hold the hardest confession: "'Cause you deserve a man to lead you in." He holds up an old idea of what a partner should be, measures himself against it, and comes up short. Self-doubt and devotion arrive in the same breath, and the song never resolves them.

The sound stays humble against all that. A strummed acoustic guitar carries a slight country lean. Bass and drums come in with a simple line and a steady snare backbeat at an easy mid-tempo. The lead vocal is clear with a little rasp, and a second voice shadows it in harmony. The recording has a natural room feel, in keeping with what the band wanted when they made the album with producer Jon Gilbert in a converted garage studio, aiming for something close to playing live in a room. AllMusic heard the Lumineers and Vance Joy in the album, and that indie folk lineage fits. The difference is that "Julia" hides its unease inside a crush on a waitress.

It sticks because it catches the moment you realize the person you're nervous in front of might be watching you change, and you can't promise her who you'll be.
