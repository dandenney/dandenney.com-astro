"Still I'm gilded and groomed, gliding into the room." That line is the hinge of **There's Something Dark**, and it's what lifts the song above a plain confession. Dustin Kensrue isn't only saying he has a darkness. He's admitting he's good at hiding it. The narrator walks in polished, "saying such bold and beautiful things," while the title refrain returns again and again like a pulse he can't quiet. The feeling is shame mixed with skill: the knowledge that the performance works, and that it working makes things worse.

The arrangement mirrors that composure. An acoustic guitar picks a steady, clean arpeggio. The bass sits on root notes. The drums hold a mid-tempo beat with kick, snare and hi-hat, steady all the way through. Kensrue's voice is clear and mid-range with a little rasp at the edges, sung in long, melodic lines with no harmony to lean on. The production is dry and polished, every instrument in its own space. Nothing in the playing sounds frantic, which means the turmoil lives almost entirely in the words. That tension is the point. The song is as well-groomed as its narrator.

The lyric moves through moods that don't settle. The imagery of a "ghost in this machine," a jagged hole, old roots twisting through the soul, describes something that feels both intimate and alien, part of him and also against him. Then the writing turns hard: "I will see you in hell." It's the most unsettling line here, a flash of defiance aimed at the person who was "meant to find" his secrets. Hurt, threat and exposure sit right next to each other. Near the end the bravado drops for a plain plea: "Is there anyway you could save someone like me?" He calls out a name, and the one he calls seems far away. The song never says whether help arrives.

Kensrue is best known as the frontman of Thrice, and he self-produced *Carry the Fire*, his long-awaited follow-up to 2007's *Please Come Home*, with Brian Eichelberger mixing and Howie Weinberg mastering. His website describes the album as a study in contrasts, light against darkness, asking what it means to carry the fire when no warmth seems left. This fourth track is the darkness half of that argument stated plainly. One review called it a clichéd acoustic number, praising the lyrics and intent but not the hook. That's fair: the refrain is blunt. But the bluntness fits a narrator who has run out of clever ways to say it.

The song lets you sit inside someone who can charm a room while privately asking to be rescued, and it makes that split feel uncomfortably familiar.
