The coldest line in **Cliffy** comes early, and Matt Maeson doesn't dress it up. Cliffy is cutting close to a power line, his father is too high to notice, and the narrator sees all of it and keeps quiet. The reason lands flat as a receipt: "'Cause I'm running real low and he's my supply." It's a confession of the ugliest kind of math, where someone else's safety gets weighed against your own need and loses. Maeson doesn't apologize for it in the verse. He lets it sit there.

Then the chorus turns tender, and the tenderness is the hard part. "Are you still hurting?" someone asks, wanting to know where all those burdens came from. The lyric never says who's asking. It could be the narrator to Cliffy, voicing a care he couldn't manage when it counted. It could be God, who shows up in the second verse wrestling with the narrator, telling him to quit pulling his hair out, insisting he's still involved and won't be drowned out with alcohol. Or the narrator could be asking himself. Guilt and tenderness share the same melody, and the song doesn't choose between them.

Maeson grew up in Virginia Beach with parents who played in Christian heavy-metal bands. He travelled with their outreach ministry, performing in prisons and at biker rallies. He has talked about losing roughly three years to addiction, getting arrested again and again, leaving his family, and finding his way back to them before the music took off. Knowing that, the song's argument with God has the worn-in feel of someone raised around people who expected an answer. Fans say he tells the story behind it from the stage.

He wrote it with James Flannigan, who also produced, and they kept it plain. Acoustic guitar strums over a walking bass line, and the drums hold a moderate, driving beat. A second voice harmonizes on some phrases and thickens the line. The mix is dry and a little raw, with a room around it, full without crowding Maeson's slightly raspy voice. Nothing sands the edges off the confession.

Later comes a vow, repeated four times: "I bet my life on you." Aimed at God, it's faith. Aimed at Cliffy, the supply, it's the addiction talking. The song lets both readings stand, which is more honest than picking one.

It makes you stand where the narrator stands, close enough to see someone in danger and too needy to speak, and then it asks, gently, whether you're still hurting anyway.
