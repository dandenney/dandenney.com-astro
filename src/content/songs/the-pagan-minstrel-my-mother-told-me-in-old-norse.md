The line that does the most work in **My Mother Told Me** is the one sung twice: "Hǫggva mann ok annan." Strike down one man, then another. It lands at the end of a verse about standing tall in the prow and steering a costly ship into harbor, and the song gives it the same even weight as everything else. That flatness is the point. This sounds like a child's boast, and in a child's boast the killing and the beautiful oars sit side by side.

The whole song is that boast, told secondhand. "Þat mælti mín móðir," it opens: my mother said. She said someone should buy me a ship and "Fley ok fagrar árar," fine oars, so I could go off with the Vikings. The pride doesn't belong to the speaker alone. It is borrowed from a parent's promise, and that is why the song feels warmer than its violence suggests. Under the swagger is someone who wants to be seen and has been told, by the person who matters most, that they will be. The lyric never squares that tenderness with the blood, and it doesn't try.

The structure makes the wish stronger than the deed. The mother's verse comes around three times and the prow-and-axe verse only twice, so the song keeps circling back to the promise and finally rests there, on leaving with the Vikings, not on the killing. Every closing line is sung twice, the way you repeat something you want to be true.

The Pagan Minstrel, a self-taught, self-produced multi-instrumentalist and singer based in Valparaíso, Chile, keeps the setting spare. Plucked strings roll out a steady arpeggio, a bowed string holds long notes underneath, and a drum keeps a driving, even pulse at a moderate tempo. Her voice is clear and resonant, a single line with no harmonies, delivered strong and direct, more storyteller than balladeer. The room sounds natural and the mix is clean, closer to a recital than to a smoky fire circle.

This is an Old Norse cover of a Perly i Lotry song, and it opens the Viking Anthems EP, a set of covers that also takes on Einar Selvik's "Helvegen" and Eivør Pálsdóttir's "Trøllabundin." That puts it in the bardcore and Nordic neofolk world, in the vein of Selvik, Eivør and Heilung. What this version brings is restraint. It doesn't reach for a war chant. It trusts one voice, a few strings and a drum to carry an Old Norse lyric to listeners who mostly can't parse it, and the melody does the translating.

It sticks because you end up humming a child's promise of glory, handed down in a mother's words, before you notice what the glory was for.
