The whole song hangs on a bargain struck on a plane: if this thing goes down tonight, the narrator figures, there's nothing left to lose by telling the truth. **Pancakes for Dinner** uses imagined disaster as a permission slip. It's a confession that can only happen if the stakes get high enough, and Lizzy McAlpine circles it the whole way through.

The verses are all hedges. Don't want to be forward, don't want to cross a line, don't want to be weird. Each apology runs straight into the crash fantasy, and you can hear how badly the narrator needs that excuse. When the nerve fails, small talk takes over: "how was fall semester?" McAlpine wrote the album while studying abroad in Spain in fall 2019, and the line has the ache of distance in it, someone checking in on a life going on somewhere else.

Then the chorus gives the real thing away, and it's tiny. Breakfast at the wrong hour. Getting stuck in someone's head. A TV show watched together, then watched in bed when you're both under the weather. These are the wishes of someone who has already pictured the whole ordinary future. A quick wink about dressing up just to get undressed keeps it from going soft, a flash of want inside all that politeness. The lines that follow the chorus borrow the voice of a flight announcement or a nervous presenter, "save all your questions for the end," as if the feelings were a speech to get through without interruption.

The second verse holds the hardest feeling in the song. If the other person is still in love with someone else, the narrator will just leave it there, never say a word. Hope and self-protection sit side by side, and the song doesn't choose between them.

The arrangement stays as unassuming as the wishes. Fingerpicked acoustic guitar sets an arpeggiated pattern, a bass line sits underneath, and the drums use brushes on the snare with a steady kick, so the groove feels relaxed rather than urgent. The vocal is clear and slightly breathy, conversational and melodic, and there are no harmonies to hide behind. It's one voice working up to something. Philip Etherington, McAlpine's Berklee classmate, is the song's credited producer, and it sounds clean and open, in the indie folk and folk-pop register of the rest of *Give Me a Minute*. It had passed 20 million Spotify streams before "Ceilings" went viral, which makes sense. Plenty of people know this exact silence.

The last line gives up on bravery entirely and hands the job to the other person: "You'll say it instead." It's funny, a little cowardly, and true to how a crush actually feels.

The song sticks because it lets you live inside the moment just before you say it, when the wanting is specific and the courage keeps not showing up.
