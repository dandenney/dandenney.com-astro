One time through the refrain of **H.B.W**, the line breaks off on the first letter of "heartbreak," as if the singer got jolted awake before the word could land. It's a small cut, and it tells you what kind of song this is. Saya Gray has said the title, an acronym for "Heartbreak Wake," is about "the pain of nightmares," and the phrase works both ways: the morning after a bad dream, and a vigil for something dead.

The dead thing is a relationship, and the narrator keeps visiting it. "I lay a flower once a week for you and me" is the line that hurts most. The mourner reads as devoted rather than angry, still tending a grave dug for two. Earlier, the narrator describes being treated as disposable: "I'm an outfit you change into." That's someone who understands they were worn, then dropped. The song doesn't fight back. It shrugs at a life that keeps flying overhead and decides that resisting is pointless. Press around the single framed it as grief and loss of control, and that tracks. But the sharpest moment is less settled than either. The narrator asks whether he's lonelier than last year, then adds, "I don't know if anybody can love you like me." It's tender and a little proud, maybe even hoping he's suffering. The song lets those feelings sit together, and they never resolve.

The sound keeps all of this close. A clean electric guitar picks out an arpeggio through a slight delay. A bass holds simple, sustained notes underneath. A drum machine ticks at a swung mid-tempo with a prominent snare and hi-hat. Gray's voice is breathy and somewhat high, singing long melodic lines with no harmonies to cushion them. It's recorded dry and close, with a raw edge, so it feels less like a performance and more like someone talking themselves down at 4 a.m.

Gray wrote it, and SAYA was co-produced by Gray and her brother Lucian. The song arrived after the country-leaning "SHELL ( OF A MAN )" and sits eighth on her debut, a record she began after a troubled romance ended, when she booked a flight to Japan and took a solo cross-country road trip with an acoustic guitar. SAYA drew on Joni Mitchell. **H.B.W** carries that candor into trap-influenced avant-pop, and one review singled it out as the album's outlier. The lyric ends on the title phrase, repeated, with nothing left to add.

The song holds you inside someone who knows the love is over and still brings flowers, and it makes that loyalty feel like both the wound and the only comfort.
