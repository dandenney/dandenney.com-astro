The toothpaste is the detail that does it. Plenty of breakup songs inventory what a lover left behind, but **"Tangerine"** lands on the most ordinary object in the bathroom and turns it into a small act of self-harm: "No brush but you left your toothpaste / So I won't forget how you taste." The narrator isn't just stumbling over leftovers. They're keeping them on purpose, so the wound stays fresh.

That's the feeling at the center of Noah Richardson's song, and he doesn't pretend it's healthy. The chorus names hair still stuck in the bedsheets, a scent like the fruit in the title, and then a line that admits the whole thing outright: "Addicted to you and your misery." Right before it, the narrator claims to be doing what they can to come clean. Both stay true at once, the wanting-to-quit and the reaching for another hit, and the song never settles which one wins. The writing ties the senses together through small, intimate details. Smell, touch, taste: the ex is gone, but the body hasn't caught up.

The other stanzas carry a quieter tug-of-war. The narrator casts the other person as the heartbreaker who's too good at leaving, and casts themself as the storm chaser who keeps believing. It's a flattering self-portrait, loyalty dressed up as daring. Later the swagger cracks into plain hurt: the lover is a sorceress, the narrator is tortured, and they ask whether it was so hard to tell. The sharpest turn is a single word. The opening stanza ends with the two of them walking away. Later the line comes back, and now they won't. Then the hair-and-tangerine section returns twice at the end, the same images circling, like someone who can't stop rereading old texts.

The sound keeps all this in a relaxed sway rather than a wail. A clean electric guitar picks out an arpeggiated figure with a touch of delay, over a simple bass line and a steady, mid-tempo kit with a prominent snare. Richardson's voice is mid-range and a little nasal, unfussy, and a harmony a third above gives the hook a warmer glow. The production is clean and polished with a natural room around it. It's easy to see why the song caught fire on TikTok and reached Spotify's Viral 50 in the US, Canada and the UK. It sits in the TikTok-era, folk-leaning indie and alt-pop singer-songwriter scene, the kind that gets promoted alongside Hozier and Dominic Fike.

It wasn't built for that moment, though. Richardson, a Philadelphia songwriter who started out busking on the city's streets and used to record in the basement of his family's funeral home, has said "Tangerine" was among his first two dozen or so songs. It sat on SoundCloud in 2021 before he redid it because people liked it. He wrote, composed and produced it himself.

The song sticks because it catches the part of grief nobody admits to: you could throw the toothpaste away, and you don't.
