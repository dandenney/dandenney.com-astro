A melody arrives on a fast, clean attack, hangs in the air on a long release, then disappears and leaves the rest of the band holding the floor. A pad stretches sustained chords wide. A bass line sits low and patient. Kick and snare keep a steady, driving pulse at a slow walk. Then the melody comes back. **Planet TD1** has that small drama of leaving and returning in it, and it turns out to be the right shape for a song made by two people who had never been in the same room.

Trip the Witch is Dean DeLeo, the Stone Temple Pilots guitarist, and Tom Bukovac, a Nashville session guitarist. They wrote and recorded their self-titled debut entirely online during the pandemic, emailing parts back and forth without ever meeting in person. Bukovac has described the process as sending DeLeo a riff and getting one back, until they had a million riffs and started stitching them into songs. "Planet TD1" was one of the first. The title, he said, stands for "Tom Dean 1," and the track set the template for the album.

Knowing that changes how the song lands. The feeling here isn't loneliness, exactly. It's closer to the steadiness of a conversation carried on at a distance, where each answer takes a while to arrive and you trust that it will. The rhythm section never wavers. The lead line steps away and the ground holds. When it returns, it doesn't strain or show off. It just picks the thread back up.

The production is polished and smooth, with a long reverb tail that makes the room feel bigger than any one studio. That fits the space-rock and instrumental-rock lineage the record sits in, though this is the calm end of that tradition, closer to drifting than to launching. Both players are credited on electric guitar and keyboards, with Bukovac adding vibes, Ryan Williams on B3 organ, and Ian Fitchuk on drums. Williams also mixed the album. The album's first single brought in Jon Anderson of Yes on vocals, but most of the record is instrumental, and the players do the talking.

The song's restraint is its strength. The groove is slow and unhurried, and it asks you to stay inside it rather than wait for a payoff.

What sticks is that returning melody, which plays like a message answered across a long distance and leaves you feeling that the gap between two people can be crossed if someone keeps sending the next part.
