**The Dreamer** saves its worst line for the middle of a love scene. The verse sets a romantic table, with the best food, the best wine, bodies pressed together, and then Amigo the Devil slides in "My gun against your spine" in the same even, unhurried voice, as if it were one more detail of a perfect evening. The next line calls it leaving the world the way dreamers do. In this narrator's mind there is no difference between a candlelit dinner and a murder-suicide. That's the horror of the song, and it's why it works.

The music never tips its hand. A clean, fingerpicked acoustic guitar carries the song, a bass holds simple sustained notes underneath, and drums played with brushes keep a slow, steady sway. Clean electric guitar notes hang in the air above it all. The voice is low and smooth and clearly enunciated, the voice of someone who believes every word. It's a polished, natural room sound, the kind of arrangement you'd put under a wedding song. Andrew Kline produced it at Sunset Lodge Recording in Los Angeles, with Tyler Coomes on drums, Sean Otero on bass and Maksim Velachkin credited on cello. Nobody in the room plays like a villain, and that is the trick.

The lyric tracks a mind turning tenderness into ownership. Early on the narrator is bone-tired and lonely, stuck on the memory of someone's eyes, and impatient with that person's habit of waiting around to find the right man. The devotion is real and total: "I hate everything but you." Then the next stanza states the plan flatly. Follow you home, build a fire, hold you still, and close on the oldest threat there is: "And if I can't have you, nobody will." A later verse even remembers a winter of morning kisses, which makes the ending worse. By the closing refrain, the repeated promise that it's all for you sounds like a defendant explaining himself to a jury, and the vow to lie by your side until everything rots is offered as faithfulness.

The song comes from Volume 1, which compiles tracks from Amigo the Devil's first three EPs alongside several unreleased songs, and the early material is written from the perspectives of infamous serial killers. It sits squarely in the murderfolk corner of Americana, where country and folk pick up rock and punk edges. Danny Kiranos doesn't sneer at the narrator or wink at the audience. The song lets self-pity, longing and violence share one breath without sorting them out, and that is far more unsettling than a scream.

You end up swaying along to a lullaby before you notice it's a confession, and the song never lets you forget that you were charmed first.
