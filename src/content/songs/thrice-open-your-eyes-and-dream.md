The verb in the chorus is the strangest and best choice on **Open Your Eyes and Dream**. Thrice don't ask you to hope for solidarity or wait for it. They ask you to devise one, as if it were a machine you could draw up and build. The whole song turns on that idea: what feels permanent was made by someone, so it can be remade.

The verses earn the turn by sitting in exhaustion first. "Marred by myths of meritocracy" packs a whole argument into a tongue-twisting line. The ladder is a story, and the few who climb it get held up as proof that everyone could. The second verse gets more physical: sweating in the swelter, swallowing rage, breaking backs for half a living wage. Then comes the sharpest image here: "We've watched this film too often." The narrator isn't naive about any of this. The feeling is a tired, knowing anger, the kind that has seen calls for change met with violence and stopped expecting a plot twist.

That weariness never fully goes away, and the hope doesn't cancel it. They sit side by side. Dustin Kensrue has said the song is about how big systemic problems feel unchangeable because we've lived with them our whole lives, and how that sense of inevitability is an illusion. Most things, he said, seem impossible until they happen. The refrain, "Knowing nothing has to be like this," carries that idea. Late in the song it comes four times in a row, the last with a "be like this" echo trailing behind. It reads like someone talking themselves out of despair, one repetition at a time.

The band plays it with urgency. A distorted electric guitar runs a fast, arpeggiated riff over a bass line that pushes from below, and the drums lock into a steady mid-tempo beat with a prominent snare and kick. The groove keeps moving forward. The vocal comes in clear and melodic with a little grit at the edges. It holds its notes instead of barking them, and there are no harmonies to soften it. The production is clean, with a moderate room sound around it.

Thrice formed in Irvine in 1998 and are Orange County post-hardcore natives, and here they aim that weight outward, at wages and myths. Thrice produced the song themselves. The band is Kensrue, Riley Breckenridge, guitarist Teppei Teranishi and bassist Eddie Breckenridge.

The song lets you keep your anger and your hope in the same hand, and by the fourth time through, you start to believe it a little.
