In the video for **Inside A Bar**, the band behind Scott H. Biram only exists in a dream sequence. That fits a song about a man alone in two ways. Biram played every instrument on the record, and the narrator he wrote is both a lonely bar regular and the frontman playing to nobody on a Monday night. The man on the stage and the man at the bar are the same guy.

Biram calls it a classic country drinking song, written a couple of years before it came out. He was trying to catch a very slow night at a country dive, a handful of regulars hanging on at closing time. He has also put it more bluntly: he wanted the sound of loneliness and alcoholism colliding. The song doesn't pretend those are separate problems. The empty room is the reason for the next drink, and the next drink keeps the room empty.

The recording sounds like that room. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar keeps a steady pattern, the bass walks underneath, and the drums shuffle along on brushes at a slow, unhurried pace. Electric guitar slides in with bluesy fills between the lines. The mix is dry and close, with no gloss and no harmonies. Nobody comes in to sing along, which is the whole point. The voice is raw and a little strained, closer to talking than performing, like someone who has stopped trying to win over a crowd that was never there.

Biram told PopMatters he was reaching for an old Waylon-era sound, and the song came out more like a Johnny Lee, Urban Cowboy-type number instead: a simple one-four-five structure, no bridge. That miss works in its favor. There's no swagger here. It sits in the Texas outlaw and honky-tonk line of Waylon, Willie and Dale Watson, but it comes filtered through the one-man-band approach Biram has built from blues, country and punk. The plain changes keep it humble. Glide Magazine called it a dusty country ballad and heard the guitar solos that split the song in half leaning toward psychedelia, a little smoke drifting through an otherwise bare room.

The album it comes from took shape during the pandemic shutdown, when Biram wrote, recorded and produced nine new songs and two traditional covers on his own. That gives a song about an empty dive an echo it may not have been written with.

It sticks because it lets you sit in the quiet after last call, where the loneliness of playing to nobody and the loneliness of drinking alone turn out to be the same feeling.
