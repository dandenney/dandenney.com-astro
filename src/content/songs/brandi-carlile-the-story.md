The face does the talking first. **The Story** opens on the creases in a face, offered as a record of everything survived, and the first verse sounds almost proud of them. Then comes the turn: "But these stories don't mean anything / When you've got no one to tell them to." The pride doesn't go away. It turns out it was waiting on someone the whole time. By the song's own boasting, there were mountaintops climbed and an ocean swum, and the payoff for all of it is a single listener.

Phil Hanseroth wrote the song before he and his twin brother Tim began working with Brandi Carlile. So Carlile is singing another person's confession, and the fit is close enough that it's easy to forget. The third verse is where the confession gets honest. "It's hiding the words that don't come out," Carlile sings of a smile, and the friends who call the narrator blessed have no idea the head is a mess. Only the "you" knows. The devotion here is less pure romance than relief at being seen by one person while performing fine for everyone else. The swagger of breaking every rule sits right beside that admission, and the song never decides which is the truer picture.

Carlile has said songs are "malleable," that a love song can just as easily be heard as one about someone's parents. This one bears that out. Nothing in the lyric pins down what kind of love it is. It only says the love held up when the narrator was flat broke.

T Bone Burnett produced the album, recorded over eleven days at The Warehouse Studios in Vancouver. This song was cut live to tape, with everyone playing and singing at once: Tim Hanseroth on guitar, Phil on bass, Matt Chamberlain on drums. Burnett asked Carlile to put down the acoustic guitar to sing, thinking it was a distraction. Carlile disagreed, set it down anyway, and that take made the record. You can hear what it freed up. An arpeggiated acoustic figure, a root-note bass line and a driving rock beat with a strong snare backbeat carry the song. The voice rides over it clear and full, with a slight rasp, powerful and direct. Harmonies double the lead in unison and thicken it. The sound is clean and polished but keeps a natural room around it. Burnett's roots-music ear is pointed at folk-rock that wants to be loud.

It's an Americana ballad that builds like a rock song. That suits a lyric about a mess hidden behind a smile, because the arrangement keeps adding weight until the composure sounds like effort.

What sticks is the moment pride gives way to need, when a lifetime of stories turns out to be worth nothing without the one person you'd tell them to.
