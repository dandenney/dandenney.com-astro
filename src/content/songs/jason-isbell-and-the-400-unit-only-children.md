In **Only Children**, a mother at a funeral, with the hearse idling in the parking lot, says, "Heaven's wasted on the dead." Jason Isbell doesn't dress that line up. He sets it down and moves on, the way people do when grief comes out sideways as a joke.

The song is addressed to a friend, and its central question keeps coming back in the present tense: are you still taking notes? Will you read me what you wrote? Isbell has said the main character is drawn from a childhood friend who died, and that he only realized he was writing about her after the song was finished and other people had heard it. That shows in the writing. The narrator talks to her as if she might answer. He sees her in every kid in cut-offs on a night walk. He remembers cold coffee on a fire escape, a demo tape they bet everything on, and the time he said she stole something she wrote from Dylan. It is the kind of teasing that only happens between people who take each other's work seriously. The song started, by Isbell's account, as a love song to writing and playing music with friends when he was young, and that warmth never leaves it, even with hydrocodone in her backpack.

The hardest moment is the mother's message. Her child thought the world of him, she says, and was glad "they finally let me be an astronaut." The success is real, and so is the childhood daydream it's measured against, and the person who would have cheered loudest is in the hearse. Pride and loss sit in the same line, and Isbell leaves them there. The refrain names both of them: "over-encouraged only children," kids told they could do anything, who believed it in different amounts and paid for it in different ways.

The music stays out of the way. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar carries the song. A clean electric guitar adds color around it, a walking bass line moves underneath, and brushes work the snare. The tempo is unhurried. Isbell's voice is mid-range, a little raspy and conversational, with a second voice in close harmony beside him like a friend singing along. The production, by Dave Cobb at Nashville's RCA Studio A, has a natural room sound that leaves each part clear.

It was the first song Isbell wrote for *Reunions*, an album he described as full of ghosts, both people who are gone and the person he used to be. Rolling Stone calls it a classic Isbell tearjerker in the vein of "Elephant" and "If We Were Vampires," and it sits in the Americana and country-folk tradition of confessional, narrative songwriting. The final verse asks whether the dead believe in ghosts.

The song works because the narrator keeps talking to her even with the hearse in the lot, and so you end up asking her, along with Isbell, whether she's still writing.
