"Do you have to let it linger?" is not really a question. It's a plea from someone who can't leave and wants the other person to do the leaving for them. In their cover of **Linger**, Royel Otis keep that plea intact and set it over a clean, bright electric guitar picking out arpeggiated chords. A walking bass line and a steady kick, snare, and hi-hat drive it at a moderate tempo. The sound is easy to like, and the words underneath it are not easy at all.

The song belongs to The Cranberries, whose 1992 original was written by Dolores O'Riordan, with the music composed by O'Riordan and Noel Hogan. Its narrator holds evidence and still can't act on it. "So why were you holding her hand?" lands as a direct hit, and the questions that follow, about whether it was all a game, show someone circling an answer they already know. The craft is in the self-protection mixed into the hurt. "I'm sure I might be rude but it's just your attitude" apologizes and accuses in one breath, as if the narrator has to excuse their own anger before they're allowed to feel it. Then the chorus gives up the fight and admits to being a fool, wrapped around a finger. The pride and the surrender never settle. The last stretch of the lyric is chorus alone, "do you have to" stacked three times, the plea repeating because nothing has changed.

Royel Otis, the Sydney duo of Royel Maddell and Otis Pavlovic, sing it in a clear, slightly nasal voice with a relaxed, nearly conversational delivery, and a second voice joins in harmony. That ease turns the song into something more resigned and less wounded. It's heartbreak told to a friend after the worst has passed. The harmony helps, making the confession sound shared rather than private. The production is polished and the groove holds one steady gear. If there's a drag, it's that the playing sounds so even beside the lyric's desperation, but the restraint suits a narrator who has stopped expecting anything.

They recorded it for SiriusXM's Alt Nation in New York, picking the song in a panic because they weren't prepared. Pavlovic said there was less thought behind it than it might sound. It became their first Billboard Hot 100 entry, debuting at No. 94, and Paste reported it had outpaced their "Murder on the Dancefloor" cover with 72 million streams. Maybe that lack of planning is why it works. It sounds like a song they already knew by heart.

The bright, unbothered strum keeps you humming along until you notice you're singing the plea of someone who suspects they're being lied to and still can't let go.
