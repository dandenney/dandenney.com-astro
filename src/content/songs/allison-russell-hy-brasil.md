The chorus of **Hy-Brasil** is a spell, and it works the way spells do: by counting. Seven black rabbits, twenty-one daffodil petals, the thirteenth note of a blackcap's trill. A child could memorize it. In Allison Russell's song, a child did, and used it to leave.

The facts are hard. Hy-Brasil is a legendary island said to lie west of Ireland, and on Outside Child, Russell's debut solo album about surviving childhood abuse, it becomes the place her soul goes when her body can't. The song is a tribute to her Scottish Canadian grandmother, Dr. Isobel Roger Robertson, whom Russell calls "the brightest light of my childhood." The lyric starts a generation further back, with a great-grandmother who "was a magic weaver" and sang words she'd carried across the water. So the charm is inherited. What one woman sang to keep a homeland close, a later child learns to say to keep herself whole.

The craft is in how plainly Russell states the unbearable and how lushly she dresses the escape. "Though my brittle body was caught in his snare" is one plain line, and then the song turns away into heather and cold light: "Cup of cold sun and a winterpill." That image is lovely and slightly off, like something from a dream you half trust. The child isn't denying what is happening. She is choosing where to be while it happens. That's the feeling the song holds, and it doesn't pretend it was a good trade.

The last verse claims a victory, "Though I drowned for ten years, I'm still rising," and the next line says she is "stronger for my pain and suffering." Pride and damage sit right next to each other there, unreconciled, and the chorus comes back exactly as before. The spell never changes. That repetition says survival was a practice more than a single event.

The sound gives the words room. An acoustic guitar walks slow arpeggios over a low, sustained synth pad, and the production leaves plenty of open air around everything. Russell's voice is clear and resonant, with a gentle vibrato, unhurried enough to make the charm feel recited more than performed. She draws on the ballad tradition of her Scottish heritage, and the lineage shows in the storytelling verses and the refrain that returns like a tide. The band cut the album in four days at Sound Emporium in Nashville, three or four takes a song, with Dan Knobler producing. Russell has said she played all the clarinet live in the room and pointed to the end of this song as an example.

It sticks because it teaches you the counting rhyme, and once you know it, you understand what it was for.
