The last thing **Wonderful Nothing** says is the only thing it means without a sneer: "There's the rub, maybe I'm the fool / I'm trying to stop, but I still love you." Everything before that couplet is a roast, and a vicious, very funny one. The narrator mocks someone's tired party, their tiger costume, their table manners. Then comes a mock shopping list from the mini-mart: the bitter truth, a better mood, a point of view to replace the old one, because "Yours went off in June 1992." It's the voice of someone who has rehearsed every insult in the shower and finally gets to use them all.

Dave Bayley has called it "a song about love vs hate," about loving someone while hating parts of them with real force. The lyric keeps both feelings in the room and never lets either one win. The contempt is loud, but the cracks show early. The narrator admits to hoping and praying it would all change one day. The fight gets described as a gunfight where the other person drew blood and the narrator set themselves on fire. The cruelty works as armor, and the armor keeps slipping. In the refrains the requests turn inward: to be buried alive, to be put in a dungeon, just to make it out alive. Someone who wants to be punished this much is not over anything.

The music refuses to match the bile, and that gap is the song's best trick. Sustained synth pad chords lay a smooth floor. A synth bass repeats a simple line, a kick drum sets a steady, driving pulse at a moderate tempo, and a synth lead carries a melody on top. The production is clean and polished, with reverb opening up space around everything. Bayley sings in a clear, slightly breathy tone. His delivery is melodic and almost sweet, which makes lines about pile-driving someone land like a smile with a knife behind it.

That retro-futurist, 80s-tinted synth-pop sits squarely in the band's British psychedelic indie-pop lineage. Rolling Stone UK described the song as a post-mortem of the band's "Heat Waves" era. Bayley wrote the album alone over two weeks in April 2023, stuck with COVID in a hilltop rental in Los Angeles. He then took the songs back to his London studio, where he is credited as sole producer. The solitude shows. This is a song of one person arguing with someone who isn't there, winning every point, and losing anyway.

You come for the insults and stay for that last line, the moment a voice that's been laughing the whole time admits it can't stop caring.
