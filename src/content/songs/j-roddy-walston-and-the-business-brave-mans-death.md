A father who won't give his son his name, then dies proud the day the boy gets a gun. That's where **Brave Man's Death** starts, and everything after is the son trying to earn back what was held from him at birth. "Said he wouldn't put his name on me" is the wound. The rest of the song is the scar tissue.

What makes it hard to shake is how the narrator keeps telling on himself. The father's pride only arrives with a weapon, and the tear in his eye comes with the line about not thinking "of a better way to die." Love, in this family, is something you get by being hard enough to deserve it. Then comes the spoon the lord puts in his mouth: "It tasted so bitter, but I couldn't spit it out." It's an inheritance he didn't choose and can't refuse, tasting of his mother's money, which he steals because she took it to the grave. Her last note tells him to die like a brave man, so even the tender parent hands down the same creed.

He passes it on. He has a woman with kids, and admits flatly that he never loved them. When she dies she tells him life was worth the pain, and he can't take it in. Late in the song there's a knife, a dead man, blood on his hands, and a heart he holds, then hides, because it's tearing him apart. The cry that follows, "God, I want to die like a man," is bravado and begging at once. The chorus fears dying "in the middle of the night," and the closing borrows the child's bedtime prayer, the one that worries about dying before you wake. Underneath the gasoline and salted fields is a kid afraid of going unseen.

The band plays all this as rollicking boogie, and that clash is the point. A distorted, overdriven guitar riff locks in with a bass that holds the floor. The drums hit a strong backbeat with crashing cymbals, and piano chords fill out the middle in the band's Southern rock 'n' roll way, rooted in blues, soul and gospel out of Tennessee's Bible belt. The lead vocal is raspy and pushed forward, doubled by a second voice in unison and parallel lines, so the creed sounds like it belongs to more than one man. The groove is mid-tempo and driving.

The sound is raw and live because it was made that way. Producer Kevin Augunas cut the album in under two weeks at Sound City and Fairfax Recordings in Los Angeles. The band played mostly live to a tape machine about 40 years old, kept overdubs to a minimum, and took nearly everything from the first three takes. Billy Gordon, who engineered and mixed the record, shares the writing credit with the band.

You end up stomping along to a man who learned love only as a dare, and his fear of dying unseen gets under the swagger before you notice.
