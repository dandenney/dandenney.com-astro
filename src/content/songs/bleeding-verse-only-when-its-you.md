The chorus of **Only When It's You** hands one person two jobs at once. They are the only place the narrator feels safe, and the only thing that can actually hurt. Everything else (falling, breaking, losing) gets shrugged off in a single line, as if the rest of life doesn't register at all. That's the feeling the song holds: a love so total it has become the narrator's whole nervous system, and nobody in the song stops to ask whether that's healthy.

The best image is in the chorus. "You're the silence inside of the storm" turns the beloved into the calm eye. But the second verse quietly takes it back. The voice that pulled the narrator free is gone, and what's left is "static where you used to be." Silence was the gift; static is the loss. Across those two lines the narrator moves from shelter to interference without ever saying the word grief.

The bridge is where the song gets heavier than its hooks. "I was never whole when I was alone" arrives right after the narrator imagines disappearing, and the pairing is unsettling. It reads less like a romantic vow than like someone who has staked their sense of self on another person and is now watching it go. The last chorus swaps in a line about an ache that will never be shown, a confession that the steadiness on the surface is a performance.

The writing isn't always this sharp. The opening verse's stone-cutting thoughts are stock, and the second pre-chorus lets its rhyme go, setting "less" against "brain" with a thud.

The sound is built for size. Distorted guitars hold sustained power chords in a thick wall, the bass drives underneath, and the snare hits hard over a steady mid-tempo beat. The lead voice is clean and mid-range, held in long, sustained notes, with a harmony stacked above it. The mix is polished and wide. The project places itself in emotional post-hardcore, modelled on Dayseeker and Holding Absence, and you can hear that ambition in the big, wounded choruses.

That lineage got pointed. Bleeding Verse describes itself as using "AI-assisted instrumentation and vocals" on human lyrics, with Kyle Ward credited as songwriter, background vocalist, producer and engineer. When it passed Holding Absence in Spotify monthly listeners, frontman Lucas Woodland criticised it, and the creator answered that it was a "loving tribute." The song opens the six-track *The Anatomy of Hurt*, and an acoustic version followed in September.

It sticks because it says the thing people in deep attachment rarely admit, that the person who makes you feel safe is the same one who can wreck you, and it gets you to sing that along.
