The chorus of **Pain and Misery** begs someone to stay who has already gone. That's the whole knot of the song: a plea for presence wrapped around a plain report of absence. The narrator isn't building a case or trying to win anyone back with reasons. They're still asking after the answer has been given.

Josh Teskey sings it in a slightly raspy voice, mixing long, held notes with short, bluesy phrases, the way someone talks when they've rehearsed what to say and can't get through it. A second voice often sits above him in a higher register, and that harmony makes the begging sound a little less alone than the words claim to be. Underneath, the band keeps a relaxed shuffle at a medium-slow pace. A walking bass line moves under a snare that lands on the backbeat, while a clean electric guitar rolls out arpeggiated chords and small fills between the lines. Nobody hurries, and the groove holds steady so the singer can come apart on top of it.

The lyric is built from old soul stock: honey, baby, love as a crazy game. Some of it is familiar to the point of being worn. But the second verse turns. "Sometimes I curse that day of when you came along," he sings, then: "I was happy." It's not clear whether he means happy before her or happy with her, and the song doesn't need to choose. Resentment and longing sit in the same breath, and neither cancels the other. Then comes the line that shows how big the loss really is: "Now that I've lost the best friend that I ever had." It isn't only a lover who's gone. By the last lines the reasoning drops away entirely, leaving bare want and need, repeated.

Critics have compared the band to Stax acts and Otis Redding, and the debt to 1960s American soul is open. All four members share the writing credit: brothers Josh and Sam Teskey with bassist Brendon Love and drummer Liam Gough. They cut it at Half Mile Harvest Studios, the analogue room Sam built himself in Warrandyte. He was the main engineer, and the band chose to record to a Studer A800 tape machine. They put out the album with no label and no management. When they released the song as the first single from their debut, Half Mile Harvest, the local record store sold out, and the band restocked the shelves with the album. You can hear that home-built warmth in the room sound, live and slightly raw.

The song sticks because it lets you sit inside the moment after someone leaves, when you're still pleading with an empty doorway and a steady band is the only thing holding you up.
