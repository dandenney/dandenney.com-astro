The house in **Piece of You** is a wreck, and the narrator is the one giving the tour. One verse locks the gates and swears not even a knock will get through. The next swings the door open: come in, look around, mind the shattered glass. "Watch your step, take your time" sounds like hospitality until you realize the guest is being walked through somebody's worst rooms and asked to take note of everything.

That pivot is the whole song. hey, nothing, the Athens, Georgia duo of Tyler Mabry and Harlow Phillips, wrote about wanting to be seen and dreading it in the same breath. "Let's see how much you can bear" is a dare, the kind people make so they won't have to ask whether someone will stay. The mirror waiting to fall, the vacant rooms and torn-up sheets, the flat admission that all they ever do is leave: it's damage laid out on purpose, by someone who seems half ready for the visitor to flinch so the leaving gets decided early.

The chorus is where the guard slips. "There's a piece of you that I just can't ignore" sits right against a missing piece of the self, and the song never decides which loss cuts deeper. Later, the narrator asks whether the visitor would rather go home to a life with open blinds and a warmer heart, then lands on a skull slammed into a wall. It's the most violent image in the song, and it comes framed as a question, almost as permission: you can leave.

The sound keeps all of this close. A bright, open strum of acoustic guitar carries it, with a plain bass line and drums on a steady, driving mid-tempo beat, snare on two and four. One clear, mid-range voice, no harmonies to share the weight. The recording is dry and a little raw, fitting for an EP written in a cabin in Maine and recorded in the sunroom of Phillips' grandmother's house there. After the emo-flavored pop-punk of their 2023 debut, We're Starting to Look Like Each Other, this is a more rustic turn, closer to Pinegrove, one of the duo's named influences. Their other cited touchstone, Modern Baseball, still shows in how bluntly the hurt gets said.

Zillions Magazine heard it as love that lingers after it's gone, and the closing lines bear that out. Once the visitor is inside, the narrator claims to be free, with nothing left to see. That reads as relief and as emptiness, and the song lets both stand.

It sticks because it lets you walk through someone's wreckage as an invited guest, where the invitation and the warning to leave turn out to be the same gesture.
