The whole bargain of **"The Warrior"** hangs on three words tucked into the chorus: "If you survive." The song sounds like a victory lap, all swagger and stacked harmonies, but the promise it makes comes with conditions. Heart-to-heart you'll win, the narrator tells the person being chased, and then comes the catch. Love here isn't shelter. It's a test you might not pass.

The verses play out as a hunt. Smyth's narrator watches someone running from their own heart, calls out their hunger, and dares them to break loose. Then the frame flips: "Who's the hunter, who's the game?" That question is the song's most honest moment. For all the chest-out bravado, the singer doesn't know who's in control, and the wanting runs both ways. The key image, "Shootin' at the walls of heartache," says even more. The gunfire isn't aimed at a lover. It's aimed at the hurt itself, the kind of fight you only pick once you've already been wounded. The pride and the pain never settle into one feeling, and the song doesn't try to make them.

Holly Knight and Nick Gilder wrote it, and producer Mike Chapman, who'd written with Knight before, brought it to the band. Knight later said she hadn't realised how much warrior spirit was in her until she wrote it. To her, that spirit meant standing up for what you believe in and following through. Smyth didn't write a word, but she loved it and knew it was a hit. It went to number one on the rock charts and number seven on the Billboard Hot 100.

The record earns that confidence. The album was made largely at the Record Plant in New York, with Thommy Price on drums, Ivan Elias on bass, Zack Smith and Keith Mack on guitars, and Peter Wood on synthesizer. Here the kick, snare and hi-hat lock in with the bass for a driving mid-tempo push. The guitars mix a rhythmic, arpeggiated figure with sustained chords, and a synth pad fills the space underneath. Smyth's voice is clear and strong. The backing vocals move in parallel with it, thickening the lines they want you to shout back. It's polished, full and built for radio, squarely in the lane of Pat Benatar, The Cars and Foreigner. Knight also co-wrote "Love Is a Battlefield" with Chapman, and the titles alone suggest the same idea: romance as combat.

The song outgrew its costume. Smyth wanted a live performance video and got a concept piece she begged the label not to release. She said she had no idea it would look like an off-Broadway production of *Cats*. It's a nice irony that a song about refusing to be tamed got dressed up anyway. Warrior was Scandal's only full-length album, and Smyth left soon after.

It sticks because it lets you feel armored and exposed in the same breath, swinging at heartache while admitting out loud that you might not make it through.
