Underneath **Crabbuckit** is a bassline that re-creates lines from two older records: Ray Charles's "Hit the Road Jack" and The Cure's "Lovecats." k-os said what drew him to the Charles record was how it sounds happy and spooky at once, and that doubleness runs through this whole song. It struts. It also keeps glancing over its shoulder.

The hook does most of the work: "No time to get down cause I'm moving up," three times, then a nod toward the crabs in the bucket. "Get down" splits two ways. It means don't dance, and it means don't sink. The crab image finishes the thought: crabs drag back any crab that tries to climb out. So the chorus is buoyant and a little defensive. It's the voice of someone who has decided to leave and can feel the hands on the ankles. k-os described the song as a man saying he has grown up and these are his beliefs, and the verses read that way, plain and local. The narrator is out on Yonge Street on a Friday, refusing to follow the crowd, taking no fast lane, and still staying on the highway.

What keeps it human is that the certainty wobbles. In the first verse he says truth comes back when you let it go, then admits it only seems complicated because it's really so simple. In the second he walks it back: "I know it's not that simple, I know it's not that hard." Pride and doubt sit right next to each other, and the song doesn't pick one. The jabs at "clones" and "fools" carry real contempt. They also sound like a person talking himself into his own path.

The sound is clean and dry, close to the ear. A drum machine lays a steady groove with a prominent snare. The bass repeats its simple figure, and a bright, high keyboard line plays against it. k-os raps in a slightly nasal, conversational flow, with call-and-response answering his lines. The arrangement leaves air around every piece. k-os produced and arranged it himself, programmed the drums and played piano. The credits list Maury LaFoy on double bass and Christopher Plock on saxophone.

The song belongs to Toronto's alternative hip-hop line, built on live players and loose with pop, jazz and funk. It's a cousin of Dream Warriors' "Ludi" and Feist's "Mushaboom." k-os said he consciously tried to make a Thriller-type record with Joyful Rebellion, and this was its first single in 2004. It went on to become the first hip-hop song to win Single of the Year at the Junos. He has said it's a pop song at the end of the day, which wasn't his intention. When Apple wanted it for a commercial, he wasn't comfortable with that, which suits a song about holding your own ground.

You hum the chorus as a boast, then catch yourself hearing it as a promise you're still trying to keep.
