Nobody sings harmony on **Empty House**. For a song with that title, the choice carries weight. Jacob Reina's voice comes in clear and mid-range, smooth and melodic, and it stays on its own. No second voice arrives to keep it company. In a song named for a place where people used to be, a lone voice does a lot of the emotional work before you could name a single line.

The Gringos are a five-piece out of Athens, Georgia, a band of University of Georgia students, and this is college rock in the plain sense of the tag: melodic, unfussy, loose around the edges. Reina, who says he writes most of the band's lyrics, shares the writing credit here with guitarist Pat McNeill.

Start with the guitars. An acoustic is fingerpicked in a rolling arpeggio, steady as a clock in a quiet room. Over it, an electric guitar holds sustained, clean lines that hang rather than move. The two set up a gentle tension: one keeps time, the other lingers. The bass plays a simple, foundational line, and the drums are brushed, slow and steady. That keeps the floor soft under everything, so the song never pushes forward. It sways in place, like a ballad that has nowhere to be.

The production is clean and polished, in what sounds like a medium-sized room. Nothing crowds anything else. A house only sounds empty when you can hear how little is in it, and the arrangement lets you hear that.

What the song holds is a stillness that isn't peace. The relaxed tempo and the brushed drums say calm. The lone voice and those sustained electric lines say something is missing. The band doesn't settle which one is true. The restraint reads less like a style and more like care, the way people talk quietly in a room after someone has left it.

It's a young band's song, and it doesn't reach for size. The Gringos have taken it on the road, though. It turns up in a setlist from Workplay in Birmingham, alongside "Strange" and "In Retro," and the band was interviewed ahead of its first SXSW appearance. Later singles like "Fading" and "Keepin' The Road" followed it. But this one is a good place to meet them: modest, patient, and sure of its mood.

The song sticks because it makes a quiet room sound full of something, and it leaves you to sit in that absence instead of hurrying you past it.
