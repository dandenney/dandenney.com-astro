The Gringos' **"Empty House"** pulled me in because it opens mid-argument, with no throat-clearing: *"We got into another fight, honey what are we gonna do?"* It sounds like a song written the morning after, by somebody who still isn't sure how bad the night was.

The Gringos are an Athens, Georgia five-piece who met as freshmen at UGA and started out as a cover band, playing bars and parties and later building an audience on porch videos of other people's songs. "Empty House," released as a standalone single in March 2024, is one of the originals that broke through, and it makes a decent case that they have their own stories to tell. Singer Jacob Reina wrote the lyrics, with guitarist Pat McNeill sharing the composer credit.

The line that sticks is the second one: *"Maybe you were always right, I ain't good with drinkin' it's true."* It's a confession tucked inside an apology, and it's more honest than breakup songs usually let their narrators be. He isn't the wronged party here, and he knows it. The chorus is plain almost to the point of bareness, *"We both tried to find our way out / But please don't leave me in this empty house,"* and the plainness works. "We both tried" is generous; "please don't leave me" undoes it right away. Then comes the small, specific hurt: *"Well you stand there, on the corner alright / But darling you never said goodnight."*

It loses some momentum in the second verse, which leans on familiar moves (a heart *"split in two,"* *"nothing to lose"*) that don't land as hard as the opening's self-indictment. At nearly four and a half minutes, it also asks you to sit with the same chorus a few times. The credits list McNeill on slide guitar alongside the electric parts, with bassist Connor Grimes producing, so I'd guess that slide carries a lot of the ache between lines, though I won't claim more about the arrangement than that.

Replay value comes down to that chorus and that one admission, which is enough to bring me back.

Verdict: **"Empty House"** is a simple, sincere heartbreak song whose best moment is the narrator admitting the drinking was the problem.
