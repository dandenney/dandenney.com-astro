"Give me my last cigarette" is a condemned person's request, and on **This Is the End** The Ghost of Paul Revere make it a chorus a whole room could sing. The song belongs to someone at the very end, someone who dug the grave and figures they might as well get in, yet it moves like a front-porch singalong. Acoustic guitar strums steadily, a banjo runs a melodic line over the top, and bass and a simple drum beat hold a relaxed, unhurried pace. The song lives in the gap between what it says and how it sounds.

Watch how the chorus changes. Each time it comes around, the narrator sends a different message home. The mother is told not to worry. The father is told it was worth it. The sister is told not to wait. The last one goes to the friends: tell them I love them. It's a goodbye in installments, the way you'd go around a room, and each errand shows someone trying to shape how they'll be remembered. The message to the father stings most. It could be pride, or a plea for absolution, sent to someone who isn't there to answer.

The verses are where the composure cracks. "I've always hated all the skin I was in" is the confession under the bravado, a lifetime of discomfort set down plainly between invitations to pour a glass and talk about old times. The third verse gets darker and funnier at once, with a gift card for getting cancer and an unanswered question about whether everyone is truly alone. Then comes the line that undoes the cheer: "Just don't ask me if I'm doin' okay," followed by the admission in parentheses that they're not. The narrator wants company but not concern, the drink but not the pity. Those wants sit side by side, and the song never picks one.

The vocal is clear with a little rasp, earnest rather than theatrical. When harmony voices join, the solitary goodbye briefly sounds like a crowd. That fits how it was made. The band formed in Portland, Maine, in 2011 as a trio of guitar, banjo and bass, and they recorded Believe live, with Jonathan Wyman producing and mixing at his studio, The Halo. The room sounds warm and natural, more gathering than performance. This is Americana built on folk with rock & roll's push, and the song closes the band's first full-length album, a last word that takes its own title literally.

It lets you raise a glass with someone who isn't okay and mean both the toast and the worry, and that is why the chorus keeps coming back to you.
