**Dragula** was built to stomp. In Lissie's hands it glides. The words haven't changed. A dead thing still narrates, still goes digging through ditches and burning through witches, still slams in the back of its Dragula. But the voice delivering them is clear, strong and a little breathy, sustaining its notes with a vibrato that sometimes goes wide. The menace hasn't been removed. It's been made to sound beautiful, and that's harder to shake.

The song is Rob Zombie's, written with Scott Humphrey, and Lissie recorded it for Haunt, the horror film by Scott Beck and Bryan Woods, the writers of A Quiet Place. Her version plays over the end credits, a softer, slower take on the original. It fits a habit of horror films: the screaming is done, and a familiar song comes back hushed and strange. The job has a hometown thread too. Beck and Woods come from the Quad Cities, the same area as Lissie, who was born in Rock Island, Illinois. She said she was honored they asked, and they said they'd wanted to work with her for years. She built the track with Lance Conrad at Humans Win! Recording Studio.

The arrangement stays out of the way. A bass holds simple, sustained notes. A clean electric guitar picks out arpeggiated chords. The drums keep a steady kick-and-snare pulse that moves the song forward without hurrying it. The production is clean and polished, and there are no harmonies, so the single voice carries everything. That plainness is the trick. Nothing in the sound winks at you.

Zombie's lyrics are pure monster brag, a catalog of things the narrator has become: the sky, the pool, the rat. "Dead I am the sky, watchin' angels cry" reads as gloating on the page. Sung this sustained and melodic, it sounds closer to grief, or to a calm that's scarier than grief. The best line is "Tender is the fur, dyin' as you purr," and the cover's whole approach is in it: tenderness and killing in the same breath, neither one cancelling the other. The final stretch repeats the coaxing "do it baby" and the command "Burn like an animal," and a voice this clear and breathy turns the order into an invitation. That's the unsettling part. The narrator isn't raging. It's enjoying itself, and it wants company.

The cover lets you feel what a monster might feel from the inside, unhurried and almost loving, and the sweetness is what keeps it in your head after the credits roll.
