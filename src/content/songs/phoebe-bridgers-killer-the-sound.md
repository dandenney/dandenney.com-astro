The tenderest request in **Killer + The Sound** is a mercy killing. "I hope you kiss my rotten head / And pull the plug," the narrator asks, picturing a body kept alive by a machine and asking the person they love to end it gently. It's a strange way to say forever. It's also exactly how this song loves: through dark jokes, worst-case scenarios, and a refusal to look away.

The first half is Phoebe Bridgers' "Killer," a song she first put out in 2015 and later included on her 2017 debut *Stranger in the Alps*. Its narrator builds a scary self-image and then can't live up to it. They spook a partner by talking about Dahmer on the couch and call themselves a killer, but they can't stand the thought of sleeping next to a body. In the same breath they admit they'd fake sleep just to count the other person's breathing. The menace is a pose, and the pose keeps slipping. A chorus that starts out hungry for blood comes back confessing it's helplessly in love, and nothing in the song settles which one is true.

Then the song turns into Noah Gundersen's "The Sound," from his album *White Noise*, and the voice turns outward and gets sharper. Its "you" is a burnout picking fights with strangers, coasting on other people's forgiveness, holding on to a Johnny Cash middle finger that's gone washed-out. The chant gets blunt: "shut up and listen." Coming after "Killer," it plays like an answer. One half admits how frightening it is to love someone that much, and the other is fed up with someone who won't take the love on offer. Exasperation and devotion sit right next to each other.

The sound stays modest. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through slow arpeggios. A breathy, close-miked voice with a slight vibrato leads, and a second voice harmonizes with it. The room sounds small. Abby Gundersen played piano and sang. They cut it at Seattle's Studio X in a few spare afternoon hours on the last day of a tour Bridgers and Gundersen played together. That backstory explains the mood. Gundersen said he'd listened to her record obsessively. Bridgers said she'd been a fanatic fan of his since her teens, and that he made her more comfortable being honest in her songs. So this is two confessional writers from the Seattle and Los Angeles folk scenes trading songs out of mutual admiration.

It sticks because it hands you both sides of loving someone hard, the fear of what you'd do for them and the anger at what they won't do for themselves, and lets them harmonize instead of argue.
