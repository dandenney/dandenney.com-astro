The song opens on a blasphemy that turns into a prayer partway through the first line. **Jesus Christ** starts as the kind of thing you mutter at a pretty face across a room, then becomes the person being spoken to. Later the narrator asks Him directly, "So what did you do those three days you were dead?" It's a wisecrack and a real question at once, and the song never settles which one it means.

Jesse Lacey wrote it, and it appears on Brand New's *The Devil and God Are Raging Inside Me*, which also put it out as a single in 2007. The lyric is lonely in a plain way: nobody in the bed, the night hard to get through, the fear of dying alone and arriving somewhere you won't know anyone. The narrator isn't scared of death, only of what comes after, and the reasoning is clear: "my bright, is far too slight, to hold back all my dark." Under the jokes is someone who has done the moral math and doesn't like the answer.

The confessions get sharper from there. The narrator owns up to having had time alone to hone a talent for lying, then makes the song's ugliest and most honest admission: "I'm scared I'll get scared." That's the reason given for why the narrator might try to nail Christ back up. It isn't a rejection of faith. It's fear of one's own panic. A request for a sign, so the narrator will know it's over and won't fight it, is bargaining dressed up as surrender. The Doubting Thomas story gets flipped too: at the gates, it's Thomas asking to see the narrator's hands.

Dustin Kensrue, who fronts the Irvine band Thrice, cut this version for *Thoughts That Float on a Different Blood*, a live album made up entirely of covers, recorded over two nights in December 2015 in Santa Ana. Acoustic guitar is strummed with a pick, a bass line sits underneath, and a plain kick, snare and hi-hat beat gives it a steady, driving feel at a moderate tempo. His voice is clear with some grit at the edges, and the delivery is earnest and direct, with no harmonies to soften it. The recording is dry and close, and he has said he likes leaving live records raw. That suits a song this exposed. Kensrue has also called covering songs a good way to study how other writers structure them, and you can hear that respect here. He doesn't add flourishes. He lets Lacey's writing carry the weight.

Kensrue sings the sarcasm with a straight face, so the jokes read less like armor and more like a person trying to stay brave out loud.

It sticks because it says the thing most people won't say about faith, that the scariest part isn't God but how you might act when it matters, and it lets you laugh at that right before it scares you.
