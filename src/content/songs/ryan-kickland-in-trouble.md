The trick of **In Trouble** lives in one small word. Each chorus states the problem twice, and the first time Ryan Kickland follows it with "And it's where I belong," a shrug, almost a boast. The second time the "and" becomes a "but," and the shrug turns into a confession. Same lines, one conjunction apart, and the song keeps flipping between those two readings until you can't say which one the narrator believes.

That push and pull is the whole emotional engine. This is someone who has burned the bridge home, who says no road leads back and decides to call that a choice. When someone offers comfort, the answer is a flat refusal: "I don't need your shelter babe." The pride is real, but so is the howling wind in the first line, and the loneliness doesn't go away just because the narrator won't name it. The third verse is where the act slips. Darkness spreads across the skin, and then the narrator admits not knowing "where I end and where my shadow begin." It's the most frightening line in the song because it's the least defiant. The trouble isn't a place anymore. It's become part of who this person is.

The arrangement doesn't comment on any of this. A fingerpicked guitar rolls through arpeggios over a walking bass line, and the drums hold a plain, steady beat with the snare on two and four. The tempo sits in the middle, unhurried. Kickland's voice is clear and mid-range, sung straight, with a little rawness left in. A second voice joins in harmony, so the claim of belonging gets sung by two voices, which makes it sound less lonely than it is. The room is dry and close, nothing dressed up. After the second chorus, the lyric drops into a run of wordless "ah"s, as if the narrator has run out of ways to explain.

Kickland writes from the Black Hills of South Dakota, in the folk and Americana singer-songwriter tradition of stark, story-driven songs about characters from the dark side of the tracks. He also sang and played guitar in the psychedelic rock band Animalcule, which disbanded in 2006, and he'd been releasing solo folk recordings since around 2001. He wrote this one himself and released it as a 2012 single. It was featured on FX's *Justified*, then landed as track 11 on the show's soundtrack from Madison Gate Records, alongside Gangstagrass and Dave Alvin. It suits that country and roots-leaning company easily.

The song sticks because it lets you sing along with a defiance you can hear cracking, and somewhere between the "and" and the "but," you start to feel what it costs to believe you belong in the dark.
