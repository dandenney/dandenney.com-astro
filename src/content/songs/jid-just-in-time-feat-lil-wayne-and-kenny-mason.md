The first thing anyone in **Just In Time** does after waking up is pray, and the prayer goes to a gun. Kenny Mason's chorus opens on "Woke up at six (six), pray to a .9," then keeps stacking its numbers: six, then twelve, which shows up again as something to run from. From there it turns to a boast that sounds more like a dare: I got the time today. On a song this crowded with clock talk, that claim carries weight. Time here means danger, punishment, and survival. Having any to spare is the flex.

The verses work every idiom the word allows. They kill time, do time, get handed more time, and put in time since the beginning of it. It plays as a dazzling game, and it's meant to. But the wordplay keeps snagging on memory. In the middle of the swagger comes a scene that lands harder than any punchline: "Ma would go inside behind the window, hide." The line stays short and gives no detail beyond the hiding. Then, a few bars later: "God left when the winter came, then the summer died." Two seasons gone, with no one to answer for it. The pride and the fear sit side by side and never settle. The song brags about having all the time in the world because it remembers a place where nobody could count on any.

Christo and Monte Booker built the track lean and dry. A distorted bass loops a short, stubborn pattern. A drum machine drives a medium-fast beat with a snapping snare and busy hi-hats, and a synth pad holds sustained chords overhead. There's barely any reverb, so every syllable hits close and clean. The groove has a slight bounce, enough to keep the menace light on its feet, and the rapping rides it fast and clear, with no harmonies softening anything.

The title carries an irony the record never says out loud. JID told The Breakfast Club he got booed on a small 2016 college run opening for Lil Wayne, because Wayne was late. Six years later Wayne shows up on JID's third album, and JID said he didn't hear the verse until mixing began. One review framed it as a hero who is now a peer. It's the 12th track on The Forever Story, the conceptual sequel to his 2017 debut The Never Story, a record whose recording began at the Spillage Village house in Atlanta. The song sits in a lyric-first Southern tradition, and a New Orleans veteran fits in it comfortably.

The song keeps telling you it has all the time in the world, and the more it insists, the more you hear how close a few of those years came to running out.
