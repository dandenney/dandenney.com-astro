"She didn't slam the door." That's the line in **Hell Without the Flames** that tells you what kind of hell this is. Nobody screams. Someone leaves on a Monday, quietly, and the narrator explains why in one more line: "But it was always one day more." A better life was promised for a date that kept sliding, and she got tired of waiting. The song never lets the narrator off that hook. It never lets the narrator fully own it, either.

The title names a damnation without spectacle: a long night shift, a drive home half-dead, cracks in the pavement that turn into cracks in a heart the narrator insists is stone. Josiah Leming, who records as Josiah and the Bonnevilles and wrote the song with Joel Little, keeps the misery ordinary on purpose. When friends ask how it's going, the answer is a shrug about waiting for the world to end. It's the joke people tell so they don't have to say the true thing. Then the chorus says it anyway: "Rat inside a cage holding back the rage." Exhaustion and anger share one body, and the anger has nowhere to go but inward. The closing lines make it worse. Another hill to climb, "another axe to grind," and it's night again.

The band doesn't play it as a dirge. A strummed acoustic guitar sets the pulse, the bass walks underneath, and Ryan Levine's drums keep a steady, driving beat. A clean electric guitar plays a melodic counterpoint to the vocal. Leming's voice is clear with a little rasp at the edges, and the delivery stays melodic rather than strained. The production, by Leming and Konrad Snyder, is clean and a bit roomy. That polish is the trick. It moves like a song for the drive home, which is exactly where its narrator is stuck.

It was the lead single from *As Is*, Leming's first full-length since 2023's *Endurance*. This time he deliberately went more electric instead of making another acoustic record, and critics hear Dylan in the plugged-in, rootsy turn. The ten songs were whittled from nearly a hundred, written over about a year while he pulled back from the internet. He shot the video in Colombia, he told The Bluegrass Situation, because that's where jobs like the narrator's have gone, for even less pay. That widens the song's sympathy. The cage isn't one person's, and the rage being held back belongs to a lot of people punching the same clock.

Leming has said all he wants is to make something real and honest that can get people through the day. This one does it by refusing to pretend the day is better than it is. It sticks because it hands you a chorus built to sing on the way home, and every time you sing it, you're admitting how tired you are.
