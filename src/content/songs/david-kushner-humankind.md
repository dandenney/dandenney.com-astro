The devil in **Humankind** isn't waiting at a crossroads. He's in church on a Sunday morning with his hands in the air, and he "blew his paycheck on the plate that they were passing." David Kushner's opening verse makes him the most generous man in the room. He has the spirit, he testifies in the light, he dresses well. The only flaw the song lets slip is that he was heartless in prayer. That's the sharpest move here: the hypocrite doesn't look like a villain. He looks like the man everyone admires.

The song comes out of Kushner's own experience of church trauma, and the chorus sounds like someone who walked in thirsty: "I was looking for living water / You just let me drown." Living water is a churchy image of being sustained, and here it's the thing that let him go under. The anger is real, but listen to the vocabulary: slaughtered, heavens, sinners, tradition. He can't describe what hurt him without the language it taught him. That's the grief under the bitterness. He still wants what he was promised.

The second verse widens the blame and quietly takes some of it on. Loving the ones who hurt us and hurting the ones we love is a "we" line, not a "you" line, and it keeps the song from becoming a sermon against sermons. Kushner said he wanted to show the struggle between light and darkness, something he feels every day, and that there are always two sides to everything. Then the bridge drops the case entirely. He admits he's only human and asks someone to take his hand. After all that accusation, what's left is need.

The sound keeps its distance and lets the words land. A clean electric guitar picks out arpeggiated chords over a low bass line, a synth pad sits behind them, and the reverb gives everything room to hang. The tempo is slow. Kushner's voice is raspy and a little breathy, holding notes long rather than pushing them, and on the wordless "oh" refrains a second voice layers in harmony. It's polished folk-pop, in the lane of Hozier, the Lumineers and Bon Iver, with church language running through it.

Written with Sean Douglas, Jeremy Dussolliet and Tim Sommers and produced by One Love, it arrived as the announcement of his debut album, The Dichotomy, with a video that opens on Kushner alone in a dilapidated church. The album's title fits the song exactly.

What sticks is the split it leaves you holding: a chorus that lets you rage at the people who failed you, and a bridge that admits you still want someone to reach for.
