The whole song turns on one line at the end of the chorus. **Nobody** keeps circling a single claim, that no one has ever loved the narrator the way this woman does, and then it lands on the catch: "Like she tells me she does." The love is real enough to name. Whether it's love or just her word for it, the narrator can't say. The second time around, the line shrinks to a shrug, so she tells me. Near the end the narrator drops the hedge once and says she loves me, plain, before falling back to it. You can hear someone trying out the sentence they want to believe, and failing to hold it.

The verses build the woman out of opposites. She's soft-spoken as a spider, her voice swings like a hatchet through the trees, and she lights cigarettes like lanterns against the dark in the narrator's head. Then comes the line that holds the whole relationship: "Tender as a bruise, sharper than a razor." A bruise is tender because something hurt it. The comfort and the injury come from the same place, and the narrator can't pull them apart. Her storm is the only one the narrator has ever known, which is the saddest admission here. There's nothing to compare it to.

Then the bridge, where the song stops describing and starts arguing with itself. Something is better than nothing, the narrator says, and asks it back three times, as if the question might change its answer. The repetition is a person talking themselves into staying. "What does it hurt, a little bit?" gets asked and half-answered, and the answer trails off into the same words said again. Gratitude and fear sit in the same breath, and the song won't choose between them.

The Crane Wives, a Grand Rapids band whose folk leanings and three-part harmonies set them apart in a mostly indie-rock scene, all share writing credit on the song. It isn't new to them. A version appears on their 2020 live album *Here I Am*, and they opened a 2023 New York show with it, Emilee Petersmark carrying the vocal. Writers have heard it as a toxic love song between two women, and Petersmark is openly queer. This Dogtown Studio take was shared with the band's patrons as a video before it went to streaming services.

The arrangement keeps a steady hand under all that unease. Strummed acoustic guitar and bass hold a driving, mid-tempo groove, and an electric guitar weaves a melodic line alongside. The lead voice is clear and mid-range. When the harmony voices come in, the sound fills out, and the claim at the center starts to sound like more than one person repeating it.

It sticks because the hook is a lie the narrator almost believes, and singing along makes you almost believe it too.
