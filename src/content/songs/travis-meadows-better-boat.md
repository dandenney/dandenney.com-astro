"I ain't lonely, but I spend a lot of time alone." That's how **Better Boat** opens: a denial and a confession in the same breath. Travis Meadows doesn't wait for anyone to ask whether he's all right. He answers first, then quietly takes some of it back. The rest of the song lives in that gap between what a person says to stay upright and what is actually true.

The image came from a recovery meeting. Meadows has said he heard there that serenity comes from building a better boat, not from a calmer sea. He knew the territory. He battled cancer and addiction, worked as a preacher, and entered recovery in 2010. Then he built a Nashville songwriting career, with songs cut by Dierks Bentley, Eric Church and Jake Owen. He wrote this one with Liz Rose, and you can hear the old program wisdom being worn down into ordinary speech. The verses list small, unglamorous progress: smiling more, hating the waiting, friends on the phone who let him talk through whatever he feels like cussing out. The anger stays in the song. It just has somewhere to go now.

The sound gives all of it room. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls out patient arpeggios. Bass holds long, simple notes underneath, and brushed drums keep a slow, swaying pulse. The room feels warm and natural, which fits what Meadows says his producers, Jeremy Spillman and Jay Joyce, wanted from *First Cigarette*: him as he'd sound live in a bar. He has also said Joyce stepped in during tracking because he didn't believe what they'd cut. Here, belief isn't a problem. Meadows sings in a clear, middle-range voice, conversational enough that the repeated chorus feels less like a hook than a thing he says to himself.

What lifts the song is a refusal to oversell. Admitting it's hard to change a man comes with no self-pity, and "I'll let God do what He does" sounds less like a sermon than like setting down something heavy. The writing saves its best move for the end, where new lines slip in after the last repeat of the chorus. It lands on "When I get done, the thing may not float." The song stops mid-construction, with hope and doubt holding the same hammer.

Kenny Chesney later cut the song with Mindy Smith, and Meadows said hearing it made him cry. You can see why. The original is lean enough to hand to someone else.

It sticks because it lets you admit you're not okay while still claiming you're getting somewhere, and it never forces you to pick one.
