The food is real good, there's turkey, and the pistols are "carved out of wood." That's how John Prine opened **Christmas in Prison** on his 1973 album *Sweet Revenge*: a joke you almost miss the escape plan inside. Bright Eyes keep the joke and sing it easy. Their version, made by Conor Oberst, Mike Mogis and Nate Walcott, adds one thing nobody else could: Prine himself, talking.

The narrator is locked up and in love, and the two facts keep tripping over each other. "I dream of her always, even when I don't dream" is a lovely, dizzy line. The longing is so constant it doesn't need sleep. The chorus swings the other way into bravado, with old mother nature outdone and the couple rolling and flowing, by God. It's the swagger of someone who can't afford to sound as lonely as he is. Then come the comparisons, which get stranger and sweeter as they go: a chess game with someone he admires, a picnic in the rain after a prairie fire, saccharin at a drugstore sale. They're funny, but they're also the sound of a man reaching for anything to keep her vivid.

Oberst sings it clear and relaxed over a steady acoustic guitar pattern, the groove gentle and swaying. Voices stack around him, one an octave below and one above, in three parts. The room sounds warm and close, more like a kitchen than a stage.

In the middle, the song stops for Prine. The voice comes from his 1993 record *A John Prine Christmas*, a spoken monologue about the Montgomery Ward catalogue arriving as early as late August, eating a red ornament at three because he thought it was an apple, and finding a dark aqua Silvertone guitar under the tree. He stood in front of the mirror trying to look like Elvis until his brother Dave taught him a couple of chords. Now he's in your living room, he says, and he laughs at how things work out. Oberst has said it's strange to share a track with a hero who has died, and that he doesn't like holograms. He used the sample anyway because hearing Prine talk about Christmas made him smile. That smile is all over the recording. Before the talk is done, Prine tells you to slip a stranger a buck, and the single's proceeds go to his Hello In There Foundation and four of its grantees.

The final verse returns to the prison, with a searchlight swinging round like a gun over the snowflakes. Then the narrator signs off with "I'll probably get homesick, I love you, goodnight." He tucks the sadness into the middle of the line, between a shrug and a kiss.

The song sets a man in a cell beside the laughing voice of someone who's gone, and you're left holding a homesickness the narrator admits only in passing and the voice never mentions.
