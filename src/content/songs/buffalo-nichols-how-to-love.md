The last choruses of **How to Love** change a few small words and turn the whole song inside out. For most of its length Buffalo Nichols sings a wounded thank-you note, "the way you hurt me showed me how to love," the kind of thing people tell themselves to make a breakup worth something. Then, in the final verse, years have passed. He barely recognizes the person who walked out the door, decides they couldn't hurt a fly, and the refrain swings around to face them: "the way they hurt you showed you." The hurt stops belonging to him alone. It becomes a chain, handed down from whoever got to his ex first.

That turn lands because the song lets him be a mess before he earns it. The opening blames a pretty girl for every ugly thing he did, the cigarettes and the diamond rings, half joke and half alibi. He insists he isn't heartless, then admits there's a lot he hardly understands. He sold everything for a lie, and the second verse ends on the bitterest image in the song: love could have been the cure, "but your love was a disease." Gratitude and self-pity share the same chorus and never settle. Even the first refrain carries a sting he leaves alone, sitting right after the admission that in the end he wasn't good enough.

The playing keeps all of that low to the ground. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar carries the song, a slide catching some of the notes, over a walking bass and light drums: brushes on the snare, a kick underneath. The tempo is slow and the shuffle relaxed, never pushed. A clean electric guitar traces a simple melody against the vocal. Nichols sings in a slightly raspy, conversational voice with no harmonies to cushion it, telling the story more than performing it. The room sounds warm and natural, finished without being buffed to a shine.

The guitar style draws on Bentonia, Mississippi players like Skip James. Nichols has said he wants to put more Black stories into folk and blues, and Fat Possum calls the album its first solo blues signing in nearly 20 years. Here he bends that tradition toward a love song about forgiveness. He has also said he wrote many political songs that demand something of an audience, and that he now tries to put the idea into the music so nobody has to tell him what they think afterward. In this song the idea is empathy, and he never says the word. He just changes who's getting hurt.

It sticks because it walks you from blaming someone to understanding them inside a single refrain, then leaves you wondering who taught you to love the way you do.
