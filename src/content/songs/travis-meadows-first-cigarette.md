Travis Meadows named a song after a cigarette and then insisted it has nothing to do with smoking. **First Cigarette** is, by his account, about contentment and learning to accept yourself. He has called its title line the heart of the record that shares its name. That's a strange place to hang the word contentment, and the strangeness is the point. He told Rolling Stone he quit smoking but still sneaks one, and he called that first cigarette his "legal buzz." He battled addiction and cancer young and entered recovery in 2010, so the phrase carries weight. The peace he describes isn't purity. It's a small, allowed pleasure at the start of the day, held by someone who knows exactly what the bigger ones cost.

The song started in that ordinary moment. Meadows has said he wrote its opening phrase in his phone after a morning cigarette, then took it to Jeremy Spillman for a co-write. He also said he knew it was his own song to record. That matters, because other artists had already taken his songs to the top: Dierks Bentley's "Riser," Eric Church's "Knives of New Orleans," Jake Owen's "What We Ain't Got." The royalties from that success paid for this album, his first with label support, after the indie releases *Killin' Uncle Buzzy* and *Old Ghosts & Unfinished Business*. It sits squarely in Nashville's confessional singer-songwriter line, a Music Row writer stepping out front to sing his own life.

The sound matches the subject. Fingerpicked acoustic guitar rings clean and bright. An electric guitar holds long, sustained chords behind it, and the bass sticks to a simple line that keeps the floor steady. The drums are soft. The tempo is slow and the groove relaxed, with nothing pushing. Meadows sings smooth and mid-range, melodic and unhurried, in a medium-sized room with some natural reverb. Spillman produced, with Jay Joyce as executive producer, and the album was cut at Neon Cross in Nashville with Oran Thornton engineering. The polish is real, but it's the polish of a clean room rather than a glossy one. Everything sounds clear and easy to follow.

What moves you is how unbothered it all sounds. A song about self-acceptance from a man who entered recovery could easily become a testimony with its fist raised. This one leans back instead. It trusts that contentment doesn't need to be argued for, and it doesn't pretend the person feeling it has become flawless. The pride and the old damage sit in the same easy tempo, and neither one is asked to leave.

It sticks because it lets you hear what peace sounds like to someone who knows its price: slow, a little imperfect, and allowed one small buzz to start the day.
