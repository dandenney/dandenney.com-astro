The consolation in **Ladder Song** is blunt enough to sting. Bright Eyes sings that you're going to lose what you love most, then offers comfort that sounds at first like a shrug: "You're not unique in dying." That line is reassuring, but the comfort is cold. Grief here doesn't get to feel special. It gets folded into everyone else's, and that turns out to be the only kindness on hand.

Stereogum describes the song as an elegy to wasted youth and a paean to a friend who committed suicide, and the lyric holds both without picking one. There's tenderness, a "Precious friend of mine," right beside weariness with traitors who keep changing sides, people the narrator once counted as friends. There's a darker pull too. The narrator muses that if they have to go first, it'll be on their own terms, and the line sits in the song unexamined. It reads like someone who understands the friend's choice a little too well and is scared of that understanding.

Conor Oberst wrote it, and it sits apart from the rest of **The People's Key**. Oberst, Mike Mogis and Nate Walcott made that album at their own ARC Studios in Omaha, trading the orchestra of *Cassadaga* for synths and keyboard strings and a lot of science-fiction talk. "Ladder Song" keeps the vocabulary of that album, the silver ship, the new age, the lights from the jungle to the sky, but strips the sound back to the spare confessional mode Oberst helped popularize. Pitchfork found the album impersonal and still praised this track. It's easy to hear why.

The recording is close and dry, like a small room with the mic pulled in tight. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar plays a slow arpeggio, and the lead voice is slightly nasal, with a gentle vibrato and a conversational, intimate delivery. The tempo sways rather than drives. The restraint makes every cosmic image land smaller and more human, a mind reaching for Jesus, Buddha and spaceships because the plain fact of a death is too much to hold.

Then the song turns to a newborn star that "Looks just like a blood orange," and asks whether it doesn't make you want to cry. Something beautiful appears, and the first instinct is to show it to someone who isn't there to see it. The last verse brings back the opening refrain and changes it. "You're not alone in anything" becomes "You're not alone in trying to be." The sentence stops short at being. Staying alive becomes the shared effort.

The song gives you permission to grieve without being singular, and that small, chilly comfort ends up feeling like the warmest thing on the record.
