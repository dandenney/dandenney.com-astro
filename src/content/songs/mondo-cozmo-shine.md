**Shine** opens as a prayer from someone already on their knees. Mondo Cozmo's Josh Ostrander asks Jesus to stay with him through a coming storm, admits he's come looking for something he lost, and then starts to bargain: "I promise you I will return if you take me back." That line is the whole first verse in small. It's a deal offered by someone who isn't sure they deserve one, the voice of a person who has left before and knows it.

Then the song turns outward, and that's where it gets generous. The second verse stops pleading for himself and starts worrying about everyone else: "My friends don't understand that we all are lost." He doesn't set himself above them. The "we" puts him in the same ditch. The refrain that follows, letting them get high and letting them get stoned, could read like a shrug. Set against those verses it sounds like mercy. He isn't going to lecture anyone. He's going to pray for them and leave them be. The guilt in the bargain and the tenderness in the refrain never settle into one feeling. Both stay in the room.

"Shine" was written during a dark period in Ostrander's life, and the delivery carries that. His voice is slightly raspy and mid-range, held long and pushed almost to a shout, with a higher harmony climbing in beside him. The band moves at a moderate, driving rock pace: a prominent snare, a simple bass line keeping the floor steady, clean electric guitar holding sustained chords, and keyboard pads filling in underneath. The production is polished and full. Here that "band" is Ostrander himself. He made the album, *Plastic Soul*, over about two weeks in Joshua Tree with only his dog Cozmo for company, playing every instrument, some he didn't know how to play. It was his first time recording alone after years fronting Laguardia and Eastern Conference Champions. He said he knew the song was special as soon as he finished the vocal.

The lineage is Arcade Fire's kind of anthem, built for big rooms and shouted together, with a Dylanesque plainness in how the verses talk straight to God. It landed squarely in adult alternative radio and went to number one on Billboard's Adult Alternative Songs chart. The writing, credited to Ostrander and Aria Fern Pullman, saves its last verse for one more plea. He asks Mary to come with him through these modern lies and asks to be held and never let go. Then the song gives itself over to the refrain, again and again, with voices answering each other: "Everything will be alright if you let it go."

It sticks because it lets you pray for your own lost friends without judging them, and once that refrain has come around enough times you've stopped judging yourself too.
