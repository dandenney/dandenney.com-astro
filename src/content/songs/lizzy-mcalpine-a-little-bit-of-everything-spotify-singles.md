A man stands on a San Francisco bridge with traffic at his back, one leg over the rail, and Taylor Goldsmith gives him a line so dry it almost reads as a joke: "Setting out to join a demographic." That's the cruelty of a statistic, a life about to become a number. Then a sergeant asks the only question that matters, what he's doing it for, and the chorus answers with a shrug that is really a sob. It's the mountains, the fog, the evening news, a first dog dying. No single reason. Just the weight of all of it at once.

**A Little Bit of Everything** first belonged to Dawes, who released it in 2011, and Goldsmith built it in the Americana and folk-rock storytelling tradition: three strangers, three verses, one refrain that bends to fit each of them. The second is an older man at a buffet who has outlived his own bright future and his only son. When the server asks, "Have you figured out yet, what it is you want?" he answers with biscuits, beans, an extra chicken wing, anything that helps him forget. Grief and appetite stand side by side here, and the song never pretends one cancels the other. He's smiling, and he's piling his plate to stay upright.

The third verse turns the same phrase toward love. A bride planning a fall wedding isn't having fun, her fiancé notices, and she tells him to worry about his shirt size. What she wants is smaller and larger than the ceremony: the way he jokes, the way he aches, waking before him to watch him wake. The structure says a lot. Each verse hangs on a question someone asks, and each answer refuses to be one thing. Despair, hunger and devotion turn out to have the same shape.

Lizzy McAlpine cut it as an acoustic ballad for a Spotify Singles session in Los Angeles, with Ryan Lerman producing, pairing it with a new take on "ceilings" just after that song became her breakthrough. She said she picked it because she thinks it's one of the best songs ever written, simple yet profound. Choosing a stripped setting fits that faith. The words carry the load, especially the final chorus, which pulls the camera all the way back: psychics and doctors all right and all wrong, and the advice that "It's like trying to make out every word / When you should simply hum along."

The song lets you stop hunting for the one reason someone hurts or loves, and just sit with the pile of small and enormous things that add up to a person.
