The kindest thing about **Kocaine Karolina** is that it never raises its voice. Elle King sings to a woman worn down to the bone, wasting on cocaine and loneliness, and she does it over a relaxed, slightly swung groove that feels closer to a porch than an intervention. The cruelty of Karolina's situation and the gentleness of the telling sit right next to each other, and the song never settles which one wins.

Listen to how plainly it's built. An acoustic guitar strums a steady rhythm, a bass walks underneath, and the drums keep a light, even beat. The banjo is plucked with a slight waver and slips in short fills, like someone finishing your sentences. The room sounds dry and close. King's voice has a rasp to it, but she sings clear, alone, with no harmonies to soften anything. It's the sound of one person sitting with another.

The words are where it hurts. The first verse slips in a line that could be Karolina's own shrug or the singer's fear of what Karolina believes: "Who would wanna live longer anyway." It's tossed off, and that's the point. Despair this settled doesn't announce itself. Later comes the image that does the most damage, "What's left of your flesh slipped from his hand." Love and the body both fail at once, in a single physical picture.

Then there's the chorus, a lullaby with a trapdoor. "Easy days will come / When you rest your heavy soul." It sounds like comfort, and it is, but "rest" cuts both ways. That rest could be getting clean. It could also be dying. King repeats it until both meanings feel true, which is how it goes when you love someone who's disappearing. You promise them peace without knowing which kind you mean. The closing lines drop the ambiguity for a plain plea not to waste away, sung twice, as if once might not reach her.

King wrote it with Jeff Bhasker, who produced the track and is credited with keyboards and Moog. The Diffuser review of *Love Stuff*, King's debut, pointed to the banjo and lilting twang here. Apple Music heard her soul chops, and both are true. The country is in the picking, the soul in how she leans into that chorus. King has called the album "a jumble of rock, country and R&B," and here the jumble runs at a low simmer.

It lets you feel the particular helplessness of loving someone who's slipping, the urge to comfort and the urge to beg, carried in one easy, swaying refrain.
