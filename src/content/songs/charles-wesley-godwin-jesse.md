Somebody spray-painted a question under a bridge in bright red letters: "Jesse do you miss me like I miss you tonight?" **Jesse** starts there, on a walk under the bridge where the log trucks roll, and Charles Wesley Godwin builds a whole broken love affair out of one line of graffiti. That's close to how it really happened. Godwin has said he saw the message while out on a run, wondered who wrote it and why, and started the song soon after. He has talked about how often he sees words painted on walls, trains and overpasses, and how he wonders about the circumstances behind them. He co-wrote this one with Zach McCord, and it's a clear example of the turn he took on How the Mighty Fall, away from the autobiographical songs of his debut Seneca and toward characters living with regret.

The narrator isn't the heartbroken one, and isn't Jesse. It's a passerby who has decided they know the whole story, and they aren't gentle about it. The chorus shuts like a door: "Jesse don't hear you and neither do I." The verses lay out the charges. The writer had the chance to confess and didn't, and they swore a love that would last until death. Then comes the sharpest line in the song, cutting at the gap between big words and small courage: "But only so much that you wrote it in red." That is love declared in paint under a bridge, where Jesse may never read it. Now Jesse has a girl with a ring on her hand, and the chance to make a stand is gone.

But look at the frame. The song opens and closes on the same two lines, back under that bridge, as if the narrator can't stop walking past it. Nobody circles back to a stranger's graffiti unless it got to them. Contempt and fascination sit side by side, and the song never says which one is stronger. Maybe the coldness is just how the narrator keeps from feeling the same loss.

The music doesn't take sides either. A fingerpicked acoustic pattern moves at a slow, unhurried pace, and Godwin's voice is slightly raspy and mid-range, single-tracked, with nothing to soften the judgment. A second acoustic guitar strums underneath, a bass holds a simple line, and steel guitar adds long, sliding notes. The room sounds natural and close. Al Torrence produced the album, which was made during the pandemic touring halt, when Godwin moved his family back to West Virginia. This is Appalachian country-folk storytelling, a way of singing someone else's trouble. Godwin bends it by making the teller a judge who can't quite walk away.

You come away wondering who you'd be in that story: the one who painted the words, or the one who couldn't stop reading them.
