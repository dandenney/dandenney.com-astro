A state doesn't usually get to talk back, but in **I Am California** it does, and it sounds like an ex who knows you'll call. The chorus hands everything over: drink the wine, cut the trees, make love on the beaches, smoke the weed. It reads like generosity until you hear the shrug under it. Take what you want. You'll be back.

John Craigie wrote it, and he has said where it comes from. He was born and raised in Los Angeles and loves much about California. He has also watched people leave, criticize the place, and then drink its wine, smoke its weed and vacation there. The song gives the state the last word on that. There's pride in it and a wounded streak too, and neither cancels the other. When the list stretches to digging the gold and conquering the mountains, there's a sting: the state lets itself be used up, whatever you need, like a lover too proud to beg and too hurt to pretend it doesn't care.

The verses belong to someone else, a person stuck in a cold, lonely place. Craigie's opening is the kind of joke that lands as a bruise: "You tried to drown your sorrows / You shouldn't have taught them how to swim." It's a Prine-style turn, funny for a half second and then just true. A later verse about lovers says the new ones end up paying for the old ones' sins, and that's the whole song in small. Whatever you ran from comes with you, and whatever you left keeps a light on.

The sound stays out of the way of all that. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls under a slow, easy tempo, and Craigie sings clear and a little breathy, never pushing. Gregory Alan Isakov sings harmony, tracking close to the lead, and the second voice thickens the line without adding drama. It sounds like a small room, and it was cut in a living room. Craigie made No Rain, No Rose in his Portland home over three days, modeling the loose, guest-filled sessions on the Nitty Gritty Dirt Band's records. There's an irony folded in: the album is a tribute to Portland, the city he moved to from California a couple of years before. So the song is a native son's goodbye that gives the place he left the final say.

By the end, the line "You'll always want me" repeats nine times, less a boast than a calm statement of fact. The song lets you feel both sides of leaving a home at once: the relief of going, and the quiet certainty, said back to you in two close voices, that part of you never did.
