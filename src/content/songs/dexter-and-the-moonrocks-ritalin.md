**Ritalin** opens with a nosebleed and a promise nobody believes. "I said I swear that I'm clean," James Tuffs sings, right after someone worried has asked him to stay off the slope. In two lines the whole relationship is there. One person is worried, the other is lying, and both of them know it.

What makes the song hurt is how often the narrator contradicts himself and means every word. He begs for help and, a verse later, says he doesn't need your sympathy. He says he's getting over it, then admits it always makes him sick. The chorus hinges on a dark little joke about dosage: the narrator took some Ritalin, and it "wasn't a little bit." Then comes the line about not being himself without his medicine, which works two ways at once. It could be an honest account of needing a prescription, or the oldest excuse an addict has. The song never settles which. That refusal is the empathy here. Tuffs doesn't sort the narrator into victim or villain. He lets shame, defiance and a plea for rescue all sit in the same body.

Then comes the section that starts "This is the part where I freak out." Here the narrator narrates his own collapse as it happens, like someone watching himself from across the room. There's a rebound romance in it, the word "geeked," and a confession that all he ever does is shoot. The final line changes one word, from getting over it to getting over this, and that small shift lands like he's finally pointing at himself.

The sound holds all that pressure in a steady frame. A clean electric guitar picks out arpeggiated chords over a root-note bass line and a plain, driving backbeat at a moderate tempo. A distorted guitar sustains a big chord and fills the space. Tuffs sings in a mid-range, melodic voice, and the production stays clean and polished. The whole thing feels less like a breakdown than someone trying very hard to keep it together.

The band calls what it does Western space grunge, and Texas Monthly places it in a y'allternative line: country-style lyrics set over 1990s alternative and grunge guitars. That fits. The song tells its story with the plain detail of a country song and turns it up like a 90s alternative record. It follows the band's live cover of Tyler Childers' "Messed Up Kid," and the two make sense side by side. All four members share the writing credit. Drummer Ryan Fox has said many listeners connect with it even without any history of drug or prescription trouble, and the lyric explains why. The song is less about pills than about telling someone you're fine when they can see you aren't.

It sticks because it lets you hear the lie and the cry for help in the same breath, and you end up caring about the narrator even while you don't believe him.
