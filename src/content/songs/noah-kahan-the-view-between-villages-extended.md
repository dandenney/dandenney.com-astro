Most songs about going home end at the front door. **The View Between Villages** stops a mile short, then throws the car into reverse. Noah Kahan has said the song tracks the drive between South Strafford and Strafford, Vermont, the town where he was born. He feels at peace on that road, and the anxiety comes back once he passes his old house. The whole song lives in that gap. It's a map of one stretch of asphalt where a person can stand to be himself.

The first half is pure release, and Kahan writes it like a held breath let go. The bugs are leaving their homes, the road splits down the middle, and for a moment the world seems simple. Then comes the line that gives the joy away: "I am not scared of death, I've got dreams again." The word "again" is doing the work. This isn't a happy person on a drive. It's someone surprised to remember what happy felt like, 17 for the length of a valley curve.

Then he crosses the bridge, and the song turns on a single line: "A minute from home, but I feel so far from it." What floods in is ordinary and crushing: the death of a dog, the stretch of his own skin, the people he knew. Anger arrives before grief can. He doesn't sort any of it out, and the song doesn't make him. The escape is almost funny in how small it is: "The car's in reverse, I'm grippin' the wheel." He doesn't run anywhere. He backs up to the stretch of road where everything goes still. It's an honest picture of how people actually cope, by finding the narrow place where the hurt can't reach and staying there a while.

The sound stays out of the way. An arpeggiated acoustic guitar runs steady over a plain root-note bass line, and the drums keep a driving snare backbeat at a moderate clip, like tires on a road you know by heart. Kahan's voice is clear with a little rasp at the edges, and the production is clean, with an open-sounding room around it. It's the folk-pop of rural New England storytelling that carried his Stick Season era, grounded in a place name like Alger Brook Road.

The extended version, written with Todd Clark and produced by Kahan and Gabe Simon, adds a spoken bridge with Hazel Lewis and Melvin Coburn, two people from Kahan's hometown community, alongside Kahan himself. It turns the song's private ache into something communal, the people who surround him for a mile or two given actual voices. The image hasn't left him either. His later album The Great Divide reaches back for "the last of the bugs."

The song gives you a name for the place between where you came from and who you've become, and makes you want to idle there too.
