"I've got affection to criticise." That's the first line of the first song on P.S. Eliot's first full-length, and it sets out the whole problem. Before **Tennessee** gets anywhere near a love song, Katie Crutchfield lists her defenses: monogamy to scorn, a cold heart, an altered state of mind. Then the turn comes, almost a punchline: "baby, you're just what I'm looking for." The narrator isn't giving up the armor. They want someone who will wear it with them.

That tension carries the whole song. The narrator brags about feeling indestructible, says they'll quit when they're 25, waves off sleep until death. It's the swagger of being young, and it's real. But the lines keep giving away their own doubt. The narrator calls themself a pessimist. They point out that this other person has made a mess of this road before. Then, in the last section, comes a line that undercuts every boast: a "mental image of the way you used to look at me." "Used to" is the giveaway. Even as the song pushes forward, part of it is already looking back at something that may be slipping. Hope and dread sit side by side, and the song doesn't sort them out.

The escape it offers is small and specific, which is why it lands. Tennessee is roughly three hours up I-65 from Birmingham, where the band started in 2007. The details are road-trip details, sharp and a little ugly: "Butterfly guts on the windshield," smoke trailing out the window. And the plan is only as big as the tank: "I've got a racing mind and enough gas to get to Tennessee." That isn't freedom forever. It's one night's worth, and the triple "tonight" at the close admits as much.

The sound is clean and plain. A strummed acoustic guitar carries the chords, the bass holds the root notes, and the drums keep a simple kick-snare-hi-hat beat at a relaxed medium tempo with a slight swing. Crutchfield's voice is clear and a little breathy, a single line with no harmonies, melodic but carrying weight. There's nothing between you and the words. That directness fits how it was made. The band wanted to record everything live, and they cut the album at their old house in Tuscaloosa in November 2008, mostly over two days, with Matt Whitson engineering. Allison Crutchfield, Katie's twin, plays drums.

P.S. Eliot came out of Birmingham's DIY punk scene around the Cave 9 venue, an openly feminist band in a mostly male world. Katie Crutchfield went on to form Waxahatchee, but here she's writing a narrator who refuses to look better than they are. "Tennessee" is pop-punk with the volume turned down and the nerve left in.

It sticks because it captures the exact moment when you know a thing probably won't last, decide to drive toward it anyway, and count the gas instead of the odds.
