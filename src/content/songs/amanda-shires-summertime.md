In **Summertime**, a fiddle holds long notes and slips short fills between the sung lines, fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls underneath, and a walking bass steps along at a slow, swaying pace. Bobbie Nelson's piano lays down plain, steady chords. The room is open and a little reverberant, and everything is clean and unhurried. It sounds like a lullaby because it is one. By the time anyone heard it, the woman at the piano was gone.

Amanda Shires and Bobbie Nelson spent six days together in Austin making the album *Loving You*, and Bobbie died on March 10, 2022, a few months later. The record grew out of the sessions for Shires' *Take It Like a Man*. Shires booked time with Bobbie for "Always on My Mind" because, as she put it, "the only piano that fits that song is Bobbie's." Once they agreed to make a whole album, Shires recalled, Bobbie named the next song herself: "Summertime." She'd already cut it with her younger brother Willie for his Gershwin record, *Summertime: Willie Nelson Sings Gershwin*. Here Willie comes back to sing on it again, and the Nelson family's blend of country, gospel and standards gets one more pass at a jazz classic.

The lyric is a promise no parent can fully keep. "So hush, little baby" is the job of a lullaby: calm the child, make the world sound safe. The "one of these mornings" verse carries the real weight. Someday the child will rise up singing and fly off, but until then nothing will do harm, "Not with your daddy and your mama / Standin' by." Shires said Bobbie related to the song because she was a mother and family mattered to her, whether or not she had the idealized version. That clause is the key. The song paints a home where daddy's rich and mama's good-looking, a picture almost too tidy to be true. The comfort isn't a lie, though. It's a wish said out loud on behalf of someone smaller.

The voices carry that double feeling. The lead is clear and smooth, a little breathy, never pushing. A second voice joins on certain phrases, sometimes in unison, sometimes close by in a lower register. The song ends the way lullabies end, circling the easy living until it lands back on "don't you cry." The sessions happened at Arlyn Studios, which is run by Bobbie's son, Freddy Fletcher. So the family really was standing by while it was made.

The song gets to you because it's a promise of protection played by someone who won't be there to keep it, and the tenderness doesn't waver at all.
