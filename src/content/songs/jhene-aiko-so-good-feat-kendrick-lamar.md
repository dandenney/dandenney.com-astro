The first word of praise in **So Good** is "stupid." Face, body, the whole person: all of it stupid, and the speaker made stupid right along with it. It's slang at its most affectionate, the kind of word you only use when the usual compliments have stopped working. Then the hook turns it around and goes high, with "Sinful, but godly." That push and pull between the street and the sacred runs through the whole song.

Jhené Aiko has always been good at making desire sound unhurried, and here the setting does a lot of that work. The tempo is slow and steady, with kick, snare and a ticking hi-hat. Underneath sits a low synth that holds the floor rather than pushing it. The production is clean, with air around every part, so the rapped verses and the sung, breathy lines never crowd each other. The rap has a relaxed, conversational roll to it, almost lazy in the best way. Sounwave, M-Tech, Scott Bridgeway and Jahaan Sweet built it, and they knew to leave it alone. Rated R&B called it a two-step anthem, and the lyrics agree: there's a line about doing a little two-step and then wiggling with it, repeated like an instruction.

This pairing goes back. Aiko and Kendrick Lamar first worked together on his 2010 mixtape *Overly Dedicated*, and he added a verse to "Stay Ready (What a Life)" on her 2013 EP *Sail Out*. Both of them later turned up on Playboi Carti's "Backd00rs." So "So Good" sounds like two people who don't need to introduce themselves. The writing trades in big leaps, like a "ghetto intellectual" swerving through jet fuel. Then comes the line that names the whole appeal: "Make it physical, spiritual, sexual."

The song sits on track 15 of *Westside Whimsy*, Aiko's first album in six years. She has said the record draws on 1990s music and West Coast car, surf and beach culture, and called it "a lifestyle," not a project. That fits. This is Los Angeles R&B in its cruising mode, built for a slow drive with the windows down, and it never hurries to make its point.

The real hook is the "a little bit" chant: high, low, fast, slow, chase, wait. It reads like a nursery rhyme and works like a seduction. The song ends on it, with "Stay a little bit, longer, I'm serious," and the plea lands because everything before it was so easygoing.

It sticks because it keeps asking you to stay a little longer, and the groove makes it very easy to say yes.
