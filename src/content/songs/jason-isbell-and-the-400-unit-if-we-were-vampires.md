**If We Were Vampires** spends its whole first stretch saying what love isn't. It isn't the dress, the light on her skin, her hands in the dark, her nails leaving "love's watermark." Each verse sets up a lovely, specific image and then waves it off. Jason Isbell writes the catalog of a devoted husband and then says none of it is the point. The point is that one of them will die first.

That's a strange thing to put at the center of a love song, and Isbell knows it. The vampire verse flips the idea around. If death were a joke, the two of them would stand on the sidewalk smoking, laughing at couples with plans. Then comes the line that holds the whole song: "I wouldn't feel the need to hold your hand." Forever would make love casual. The clock is what makes it urgent, and his reach for her comes from fear as much as tenderness.

The sound stays out of the way. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through steady arpeggios at an unhurried, flowing pace. Isbell's voice is clear with a little rasp at the edges, and a harmony line sits low and close beneath it, the two voices blending side by side. The room sounds natural and warm. Amanda Shires, Isbell's wife, sings harmony on the song, and it has become a duet showcase for the couple onstage. That fits a song about two people looking at the same ending together.

The writing has its own backstory. Shires told Isbell to stop watching *Hoarders* and write something, since he was heading into the studio that Monday. The first few times he tried to play it for her, he couldn't get through it and had to leave the room. Dave Cobb produced it at RCA Studio A in Nashville for *The Nashville Sound*. He recalled Shires telling him on the first day of tracking that she couldn't wait for him to hear "the 'Vampires' song." It went on to win Best American Roots Song at the 2018 Grammys. It belongs in the plainspoken Americana tradition, where the craft lies in saying something huge in words a person could actually say across a kitchen table.

The final verse is the most honest. He'll work hard to the end of his shift and give her every second he can find, "And hope it isn't me who's left behind." It's a small, selfish wish, because sparing himself the grief means wishing it on her. He says it anyway, and the song doesn't apologize. Then the chorus repeats twice, as if saying it again might make it easier to accept.

The song makes you count the years you might have left with someone, and it makes that counting feel like the most loving thing you could do.
