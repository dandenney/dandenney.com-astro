The cleverest move in **forefathers** comes in the chorus, and it lands like a joke until you sit with it. A father who no longer picks up the phone gets replaced by a roll call of whiskey labels: Jim Beam, Evan Williams, Jack Daniels. "Johnnie Walker picks up day and night," Liam St. John sings, and the line works because the bottle is the one thing in the song that never stops answering. The gag and the wound are the same sentence. He doesn't wink at it, and he doesn't break down over it either.

The verses earn that turn. A mother works the graveyard shift, a father is gone, and the kid fills the nights with records: "I cried to Louis Armstrong." That detail has roots. St. John has said his mother introduced him to Ray Charles, Louis Armstrong, Stevie Wonder, Lauryn Hill and Tracy Chapman when he was very young. So the music that kept the child company came from the parent who stayed. The song's gratitude to her sits right next to its grievance against him, and neither one cancels the other out.

The second verse is the hardest part to hear. A child plays make-believe in a maple tree, "Guessing which car was yours." The father finally does show up, 15 summers down the road, and the narrator offers him something close to grace: he knows the man tried. Then comes the flat fact that the calls go unanswered again. The narrator gives some credit and holds on to the hurt, and the song lets both stand. That restraint is what keeps the drinking chorus from sounding like a pose.

This version is the one St. John calls live and uncut, and it plays that way. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar carries both rhythm and harmony. A simple bass line sits underneath, and brushes on the snare give the slow tempo a loose, swinging lean. The room sounds small and dry, with no reverb to soften anything. St. John's voice is slightly raspy and mid-range, delivered conversationally, with no harmonies stacked behind it. Apple files it under blues, and the bones fit: a hard-luck story told with a shrug and a groove. It also suits the soul records he names as his education.

St. John later recut the song with a band for Man of the North, his first studio album recorded with a band. He has said, though, that stripped-back recordings like this one are what changed his career. You can hear why. With nothing between the voice and the listener, the whiskey jokes sound less like bravado and more like a phone list kept by someone who got tired of waiting.

The song sticks because it lets you laugh at the punchline and then feel the empty phone line underneath it, the kid in the maple tree still guessing which car is his father's.
