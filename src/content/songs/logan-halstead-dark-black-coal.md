The guitar on **Dark Black Coal** is fingerpicked and patient, with a steady bass line rolling under the melody. It's recorded close and dry, the kind of sound that puts you in a small room with whoever's playing. There are no harmonies, no other voices. The singing is low and a little gravelly, the feel relaxed and unhurried. Nothing is dressed up.

That plainness suits where the song comes from. Logan Halstead wrote it at 15, in a family where his dad, his uncle, his grandfather, and most of the men worked the mines around Boone County. He has said he wrote it out of growing up in coal country, out of feeling trapped there and holding a love/hate relationship with the mines. That's a complicated thing for a teenager to carry. The mines are what fed his family, and they're also the walls around his future. He also called it the first song he was ever proud of, and he said he was thinking about the sacrifices his family and many others made for coal.

So two feelings sit together in this song and neither cancels the other. One is the urge to get out. The other is gratitude, maybe even reverence, for the people who stayed and gave years to the work. Halstead doesn't have to pick one, and the spare arrangement doesn't make him. A bigger production might have tipped the song toward anthem or toward lament. This one just leaves a guitar and a voice to hold both.

The song's own story has a turn in it, too. Halstead had nearly forgotten it by the time he cut it for RadioWV, on the same day he recorded "Far From Here." The video went viral in December 2020, and Tyler Childers comparisons followed. Halstead has said that seeing that support, and people offering to pay to see him play, was when he realized music could be a career instead of a hobby. A song about feeling stuck in coal country turned out to be his way forward.

It sits in the Appalachian coal-country songwriting tradition, and critics have set Halstead beside Childers and Arlo McKinley, who guests on the album. The song is the title track of Halstead's debut, released when he was 19. The album was produced by Lawrence Rothman and recorded at Sound Emporium, and he played much of the guitar himself. The song keeps the bare, close feel of someone working through his own history.

What sticks is how the song lets you love a place and need to leave it in the same breath, without asking you to feel guilty about either.
