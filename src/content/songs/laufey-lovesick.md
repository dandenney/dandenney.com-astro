Three nights. That's the whole span of the affair in **Lovesick**, and the narrator still can't quite believe it. Laufey sings that this is out of character, then runs straight into "Fell in love in just three nights," as if the admission slipped out before it could be stopped. The surprise sits as heavy as the loss. This is someone who thought of themselves as careful, and who now sits alone listening to the floorboards creak in a silent home.

The chorus is where the song shows its hand. It's built from a memory polished until it looks like a film still: gold light on skin, hair caught in the wind, and "The choir sang a melancholic hymn." That last line gives the game away. A memory of pure happiness wouldn't come with a sad soundtrack. The narrator is already scoring the romance as a loss even while recalling its brightest moment, so the euphoria and the grief arrive in the same breath and neither one wins.

Then the morning comes, and the chorus changes between its two passes. The first time, the narrator is mourning and trying to hold on. The second time the bed is empty and the plan is to sleep until falling dead, a line so overblown it almost winks. It takes lovesickness seriously by letting it go too far. The verses carry a quieter fear that's easier to recognize. The narrator wants in, inches closer, and worries that loving too much is exactly what will make the other person slip away. Wanting and bracing for loss happen in one motion.

Laufey wrote and produced it with Spencer Stewart, Junia Lín is credited on violin, and it sits sixth on *Bewitched*, the 2023 album that won the Grammy for Best Traditional Pop Vocal Album. Laufey calls the Great American Songbook a "bible," and the debt shows in the craft, not in imitation. This is an original ballad in that tradition: a small situation, a big confession, and a hook phrased as a question to the absent lover, "What have you done to me?" Laufey has called *Bewitched* a love album, whether the love is for a friend, a lover or life, and set it against her more hopeless-romantic previous record. "Lovesick" is the track where that line gets blurry.

By the end the song gives up on words entirely and repeats wordless "ah-ah" phrases, the chorus's hymn turned into the whole song, as if the feeling had outrun the vocabulary.

It sticks because it lets you be thrilled and wrecked by the same three nights, and doesn't make you choose.
