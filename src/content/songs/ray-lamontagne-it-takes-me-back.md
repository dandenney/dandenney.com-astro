The best line in **It Takes Me Back** gives the landscape a better poker face than the narrator: "The countryside just shrugs and sighs," and then it looks the other way. Ray LaMontagne opens with a life spent calling out the changes to a pickup band, in a tired van that breaks down in the rain. The hills don't care who passed through, and that indifference lets the song grow nostalgic without turning sentimental.

The sound fits a song about memory. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through an arpeggiated pattern. A bass holds a simple line underneath, and brushed drums keep a slow, steady pulse. The room sounds warm and open, and no harmonies pad the vocal. LaMontagne sings low, with a rasp at the edges but a smooth line through the middle. That's the voice critics have long tied to Van Morrison, Nick Drake and Tim Buckley. He wrote, produced and engineered the single, and he's credited with guitars, bass, piano, percussion and harmonica. My Morning Jacket's Carl Broemel adds pedal steel, and Ariel Bernstein plays congas. It's singer-songwriter folk worn in like an old jacket. This is Americana from a man who worked in a Maine shoe factory before hearing Stephen Stills' "Treetop Flyer" on the radio, and the song itself is about his travels on the road.

The verses slowly pile up feelings the chorus won't settle. In the second verse the narrator runs from Utah toward Abilene and ends up in cuffs. He makes his defense in the same breath: no angel, just born free. That's self-justification and hurt in one shrug, and the song leaves both standing. The bridge is where the hurt comes through. Away and alone, he longed to tell her he was sorry, "But there ain't no turning back." That line quietly argues with the title. The chorus keeps saying the sound of wind through hickory takes him back to where he wants to be, but the bridge admits there's nothing there to return to. The light in her eyes went out "Like windows painted black," a hard, plain image of a house still standing with nobody looking out.

So the chorus is a comfort he knows is partial. He can go back in his head, but not to her. The single came out about a year before *Long Way Home*, his ninth studio album, and the title fits the song.

The song sticks because it lets you feel nostalgia as a refuge and an admission at once: the place you most want to go is the one memory can reach and you can't.
