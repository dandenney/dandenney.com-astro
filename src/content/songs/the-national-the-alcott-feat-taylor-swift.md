Someone is sitting in the corner at the back of a place called **The Alcott**, writing in a golden notebook about "someone / Who used to be me." The narrator walks in already knowing this. That's the setup Matt Berninger built, a single contained scene between two people. He has said he wrote it with his wife, Carin, in mind. Then Taylor Swift answered it, and the song stopped being one man's confession and became an argument with two sides.

The music came first. Aaron Dessner wrote it and Berninger wrote the main body of the song over it. Dessner sent it to Swift, who sent back a voice memo with all her parts, lyrics she wrote in dialogue with Berninger. It's their second duet after "Coney Island," and it extends the partnership Dessner and Swift started on Folklore and evermore. The National produced it at Long Pond in the Hudson Valley.

The arrangement stays out of the way. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar circles under a slow, sustained bass line. Slow, steady drums keep time, and a synth pad hangs in a big, clean room. Berninger sings low and breathy, intimate and conversational, like he's talking across a small table. A second, higher voice joins him in close harmony, and the blend is so smooth it takes the edge off words that are anything but.

The friction is the song. The narrator knows exactly what he's doing. He waits for her to look up, sees her smile, and then wonders how many times he can pull this before she stops believing him. The pride of still being wanted sits right next to the shame of using it. The chorus swaps its pronouns as it goes, so the reluctance passes from one of them to the other. By the final choruses the narrator owns it: falling back in was "the last thing I wanted."

The lines in parentheses crowd in around that chorus and won't let it settle. "Have I become one of your problems?" turns the confession back on the one making it. Then comes the line that keeps the song from being a simple apology: "'Cause I love this curse on our house." Neither of them wants out. The long run of "I'll ruin it all over" isn't a threat or a plea. It's a forecast, and the lines answering it sound like something close to an invitation.

Nothing gets fixed. The song's last words are "Back in love with you," and that's the point. What sticks is the sound of two people who can each see the wreck coming, and who reach for each other anyway because the wreck is theirs.
