Two voices lean on the same note in **Foolish and Blue**, and that pairing makes the song's case before anything else does. A slightly raspy, mid-range lead holds its lines long and steady. A higher voice settles in beside it, sometimes in unison, sometimes a close interval above. It sounds like company. That fits how the song came to be. Keshia Bailey has said she was a little heartbroken when she and Allen Thompson wrote it, and that singing through the heartache helped.

The title was Bailey's idea, and it holds two feelings that never cancel out. "Blue" is the hurt. "Foolish" is the part that stings worse, the embarrassment of having let yourself get there in the first place. A song named for that pairing isn't asking for pity so much as owning up, and the harmony keeps the owning-up from sounding lonely. Thompson says it was the first song the two wrote together and that they finished it in about 20 minutes. Nothing here sounds labored over.

The band plays it slow, with a sway rather than a march. A clean electric guitar rolls through arpeggiated chords. The bass walks underneath, which keeps the sadness moving. The drums stay soft, a snare marking time and a ride cymbal adding a little shimmer. The room is medium-sized and warm, with enough reverb to put air around everything and enough separation that you can follow each part. It's polished, maybe smoother than some listeners want their heartbreak, but the gloss reads as care, not distance.

LadyCouch is a big Nashville outfit, built on the model of The Band, Little Feat and the Grateful Dead. The credits for this one read like a roll call. Thompson, Bailey, Dana Kalan and Olivia Burnett are on vocals, with two saxes, trombone and trumpet. Jimmy Matt Rowland plays keys, Clint Maine and Mike Ford Jr. play guitar and Ray Dunham plays drums. Gordon Persha plays bass and co-produced with Thompson, then mixed it. They cut it at Thunder Sound Studios in Franklin, Kentucky. Bailey brought East Tennessee mountain music, gospel, soul and delta blues into the group, and gospel's pull toward voices singing together is easy to feel in how the pairing works here.

The song later found a home on the band's debut album, *The Future Looks Fine*, a fitting title for a record that holds a song about getting through it.

It sticks because it lets you admit you were a fool without having to admit it alone.
