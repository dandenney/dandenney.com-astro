The trick of **i've been thinking** is one word. In its last verse the narrator asks, "Is it terrible that I don't care?" A couple of lines later the question has turned into a statement, and the "I" has become a "we." Somewhere in that small gap the guilt stops being private. Elizabeth doesn't announce the change. She just lets the pronoun move.

The situation is plain and messy. Two friends crossed a line, and somebody else, the "she" of the third verse, was on the other side of it. The opening verses are memory: touch, friendship, "the way that you moved," the title phrase repeated like someone circling a thing they can't put down. Then comes the excuse, and it's a funny, flimsy, very human one: "What a chemical thing, I should be let off." It's a plea in a courtroom where the narrator is also the judge. Blame the body, blame the chemistry, ask for leniency. It doesn't quite work, and the song knows it.

What makes the song honest is that it never picks between shame and wanting. "Would you do it again? I would" lands as a dare and a confession in the same breath, answered before the other person can speak. The closing image, hands in hair, is small and physical. That's the point. Everything abstract, whether it was terrible or whether anyone knew, gets pushed aside by one remembered touch. The last line drops "see" for "want." Memory has become desire again.

That question hangs over *The Wonderful World of Nature*, the first solo record from Elizabeth Mitchell, the Melbourne writer who used to front the indie-pop quartet Totally Mild. She has called it a break-up album that works through her divorce, one that asks whether you're terrible because it's simply in your nature. This song puts that question in the mouth of someone who has done the thing and still can't make themselves sorry. Mitchell is credited as its writer. The album was produced by John Castle, whose résumé runs through Hatchie, Cub Sport, Jack River and Vance Joy, and she spent the better part of two years figuring out what music her words needed.

The record gets filed under dream pop and indie pop, and the song sits in Melbourne's indie scene as a melancholic sad-pop break-up record. One outlet reached for Lana Del Rey, and you can see why in the lyric's mix of seduction and self-incrimination. Triple R made the album its Album of the Week. The framing around it called her a new patron saint of sad girls and a queer pop anti-heroine, and "anti-heroine" fits here. This narrator isn't asking you to forgive anything, only to admit you know the feeling.

The song sticks because it catches the moment you stop asking whether something was wrong and start wishing it would happen again, and it doesn't pretend that's an easy thing to hear yourself say.
