The confession comes in the form of a legal defence. "What a chemical thing, I should be let off," the narrator says in **i've been thinking**, as though desire were a substance that went into the bloodstream without consent. It's a funny line, and a sad one, and it's also not quite meant. The plea for leniency comes right after the narrator admits there was a moment "before when we could have stopped." The narrator knows the exit was there. The chemistry excuse is something said aloud to see whether it holds.

Elizabeth Mitchell wrote the song, and it sits on the deluxe edition of *The Wonderful World of Nature*, her solo debut after the Melbourne indie pop band Totally Mild disbanded in 2019. She spent the better part of two years on the album with producer John Castle, and AllMusic hears it as heartbroken songs about grief, desire and indulgence set against dense, cinematic guitar and synth-pop. The record is about the end of her marriage. She has described reflecting on whether you're the monster or the other person is, rather than casting herself as the victim. This song fits that stance. The narrator never asks for sympathy. What comes out instead is appetite, admitted plainly.

The writing works by repetition. "I've been thinking" opens line after line like a person circling the same memory: the touch, the friendship, the way the other person moved. Then the questions start, and they're asked half to the lover and half to the narrator's own conscience. "Would you do it again? I would" asks, then answers before the other person can, so nobody else gets a say. There's a "she" in the third verse, someone who may or may not have known what was going on, and she gets one line before the song turns back to the two people in the room.

The final stretch is where the feeling sharpens. "Is it terrible that I don't care?" sounds at first like a request for absolution. A few lines later the question has become a statement, and "I" has become "we," a shared shrug in place of a private doubt. The guilt hasn't gone anywhere. It has been outvoted. What's left is the image the song returns to, hands in hair, and the last line is about wanting rather than remembering.

That fits the closeness Mitchell said she wanted from the album, the sense of a trusted friend or a conscience sitting on your shoulder. Here the conscience is present and gets heard out, and then it loses anyway. The song doesn't ask the narrator to be good, and it doesn't ask you to forgive. It asks you to recognise the pull.

The hook is the honesty of "I would": the song lets you stand inside a choice you might condemn from outside, and feel exactly how good the bad thing was.
