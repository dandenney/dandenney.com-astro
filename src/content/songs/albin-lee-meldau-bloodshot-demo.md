Few heartbreak songs hang a line as bleak as the one in **"Bloodshot - Demo."** "As I hang myself up to dry," Albin Lee Meldau sings. It sounds at first like laundry, a body wrung out and pinned to a line after a long cry. Then the darker reading arrives a beat later and won't leave. Meldau has called the song "dark and horrible," and NPR describes it as a song about the wreckage of a tortured relationship and the crazed paranoia of jealousy. Both descriptions fit. What makes the demo hard to shake is how quietly it carries all that.

The setting is spare. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through an arpeggiated pattern at a slow, swaying pace. The recording is dry and close, as if made in a small room. There are no harmonies, so no second voice comes in to keep the singer company. Meldau's voice is raspy and breathy, with a little vibrato he lets tremble instead of polish. It's the throaty, old-soul instrument of a Gothenburg songwriter who counts Sam Cooke and Van Morrison among his idols. Here there's little around it to soften the blow.

The lyric, co-written with Justin Parker, is a study in need curdling into suspicion. The verses circle the bed: lying in it, crying in it, needing something, fearing something, and finally admitting the something is a person. Then the chorus turns, and the real wound shows: "Is there someone new next to you?" The song never answers, and the narrator never stops asking. The best moment is the mixed signal of the later verse. He tells his lover not to worry and offers to be there, then points to the wet pillow as proof of what it costs him. Then he sends them off with "Drive on, Thelma and Louise," a movie getaway that is half blessing, half bitter dare. Generosity and self-pity sit in the same breath, and neither wins.

The finished "Bloodshot," the title track of Meldau's second EP, goes big, with horns and enough cinematic scope that one reviewer heard a James Bond theme in it. That version has its own pull. The demo, though, keeps the jealousy at bedside scale, where it actually happens. When the lyric ends on "Now I know," said twice, it's unclear whether he has learned the truth or simply decided to believe the worst.

You come away feeling like you've sat on the edge of that wet pillow, close enough to hear someone talk himself into heartbreak.
