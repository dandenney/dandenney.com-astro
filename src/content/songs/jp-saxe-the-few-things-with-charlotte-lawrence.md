The first verse of **The Few Things** is an apology, sort of. Its narrator has been told, over and over, that they don't share enough, and the reply comes in a breathy, close-up voice: "I've been hearing that my whole life / I promise, it's not just you." It's a tender line and a defensive one at once. It tells the partner they aren't the problem. It also quietly tells them this isn't going to change. The song never settles which of those it means, and that's why it feels true.

JP Saxe wrote it with Ryan Marrone, the producer behind his debut single "Changed." Saxe has called it the most honest he's been in a love song, and said it came straight out of a conversation. You can hear that origin in the writing. The verses argue the way people do at a kitchen table, half reassurance and half self-justification. When the partner says they feel insecure, the narrator admits to not taking it seriously, which is a startling thing to put in a love song. It's confidence that tips into dismissal, and the very next breath tries to make up for it with an offer to say more. Later comes a plainer confession: the narrator keeps a lot to themselves, but this person is more a part of them than anyone else.

Then the chorus, which does the saying more. The partner becomes one of the few certain things in a life the narrator doesn't narrate out loud, solid enough to build a world on. The real turn is the request underneath it: "And I want you to unravel me." It's a strange kind of romance. Someone who can't open up asks the other person to do the opening, and "come closer, come closer" repeats until it sounds less like an invitation and more like a plea.

The sound stays out of the way. An acoustic guitar is fingerpicked in a steady pattern, a bass holds simple sustained notes, and light drums keep a slow, gentle pulse. The vocals are breathy and close, recorded dry and near. This is the confessional, lyric-first pop Saxe works in, and the plain production fits a song about saying less than you feel.

Saxe first released it solo in March 2018. This version brings in Charlotte Lawrence, whom he calls his friend and his favorite, and a second voice shadows the lead, often in unison or a close interval. Saxe said the song exists because love can be very real and very complicated at once. Two voices singing a confession meant for one makes that complication easier to hear. The private admission starts to sound like something both people are working through.

It sticks because it gives the quiet partner's side of an old argument, and you feel how much love can sit inside the words someone can't quite say.
