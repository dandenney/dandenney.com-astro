The father in **Hang in There** gets the best line, and he gets it from the passenger seat: "boy would ya go a little slower? / You're worrisome son, make your old man look a little older." It's a dad joke with a worry folded inside it, the kind of thing a parent says to a grown kid so the fear comes out as teasing. Stephen Wilson Jr. sets it right after the flattest sentence in the song, the plain admission that it's not right he's not here, and the pairing does the work. Grief arrives as a complaint. Then memory answers with a voice that's still alive, still needling.

The song sits on Wilson's debut double album *søn of dad*, a 22-song tribute to his father, Stephen Wilson Sr., who died in September 2018 after emergency surgery. The record came out on the fifth anniversary of that death. Wilson wrote this one alone, and it grew out of a memento of his father's. In the lyric it's a beaded necklace from a spring trip to Jamaica, and the narrator is honest about how it looks from outside: it's worth next to nothing to most people. That's the song's quiet defense of private value. The beads aren't proof of anything. They're the one thing he gets to keep.

The chorus turns the title into a pun and a plea at once. The necklace hangs from the rearview mirror, so the father rides along again, moved from the passenger seat into the corner of the son's eye. "Hang in there" is also what you say to someone who's struggling, and the narrator says it to himself by way of a dead man's souvenir. He calls the necklace a prayer, not jewelry. Then comes the stranger line, "an empty page to write a better ending." Whose ending isn't clear. It could be the father's, cut short, or the son's own. The song lets both stay open, and that unease sits under the comfort.

The sound stays close. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar carries its own bass line under arpeggiated chords, slide guitar draws long notes behind it, and the tempo is slow and relaxed. Wilson sings low and a little raspy, warm and conversational, with no harmonies to soften it. The room sounds small and dry, as if he's talking from the driver's seat. It's a long way from the indie-rock and grunge edges he folds into the "Death Cab for Country" blend he calls his sound. Here it's a plain country ballad, and the plainness fits. The chorus repeats more than it needs to near the end, and the keepsake talk in the second verse leans on stock phrases. But the repetition works like a mantra.

The song sticks because it shows you how grief really travels: hanging from the mirror, swinging every time you take a turn too fast.
