The phrase arrives so often in **I Love You** that it stops sounding like a declaration and starts sounding like a hand pressed over a mouth. By the last stretch of the lyric it's stacked line after line, "I love you" three to a row, and the effect isn't romance. It's someone talking over a thought they don't want to finish.

Scarlett McKahey has said as much. She calls the title ironic: the repetition is somebody trying to convince themselves they love a person when they might not, and the song is about knowing you'll leave a relationship even though you don't want to admit it to yourself. The repeated "I love yous," she says, drown out the couple's problems. That's the whole design, and the verses do the quiet work underneath it.

Look at the domestic details. "Leave my socks on when we go to bed" is a small, odd, slightly guarded habit, and the partner never seems to ask why. Crying first thing in the morning gets the same easy shrug: it doesn't worry them. On paper that's a patient partner. In the song it reads as a kind of loneliness, being let off the hook so completely that nobody's really looking. The narrator's own boast of being anything but boring gets undercut right away with a quiet "is that good?"

Then the chorus turns the confession into a dare. The narrator asks whether you mind if they ruin it, then adds "Blow it up like I always do." It's polite on the surface, asking permission, and self-sabotaging underneath, admitting a pattern the narrator already knows by heart. The second verse brings the exit closer. Shoes come off at the door as the thought hits on the way out, and then comes the question that has been circling all along: "Is this what you signed up for." The answer is "I'm sure," said three times, which is how you say something when you aren't.

Anna Ryan takes lead vocals here, flanked by group vocals. Apple Music's editors heard the track as optimistic, and that reading isn't wrong so much as half the picture. There's real hope in the cross-my-heart plea for "me and you now," and real dread sitting right beside it. The song doesn't choose between them.

It's the title track of the Canberra band's debut album, which they made over a few months living in producer Oscar Dawson's house in the seaside town of Rye. The band is placed in a riot grrrl-inspired punk and pop-punk tradition, and this song keeps that tradition's bluntness. Here the bluntness points inward, though, at the narrator's own heart rather than at anyone outside it.

You keep coming back because the song says "I love you" more times than anyone could mean it, and you recognize the exact moment the words stopped being for the other person.
