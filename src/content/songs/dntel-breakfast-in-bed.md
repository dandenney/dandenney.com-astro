The tenderest line in **Breakfast In Bed** is also the bleakest. In the last verse, the singer hopes the other person will always find someone to take them home, put them to bed, and hold a hand under their nose to make sure they're still breathing. That's love described as a wellness check. Then comes the admission: "I wish that could be me, but it's just not possible."

Conor Oberst sings it, and the words are his too. Jimmy Tamborello, who records as Dntel, sends collaborators blank instrumentals with no instructions and lets them write their own lyrics. He had a track with no vocals and asked Oberst if he wanted to sing on it. Oberst came over and recorded it, which Tamborello called "all pretty casual." Oberst thinks it happened in Tamborello's LA apartment. The two met through Jenny Lewis, whom Tamborello had worked with on The Postal Service album, and the song sits right in that indie-electronic neighborhood, next to the Bright Eyes and Rilo Kiley circle.

The scene is a morning after, somewhere away from home: wine in the coffee cup, birds outside, still drunk, the maids due to kick everyone out. The narrator is warm and wary at once. Last night the other person said they wanted to die, and now they want to live. They said they loved the narrator after forty-five minutes. The narrator sees all of it and signs on anyway, asking, "If this is all a game can you just say it is?" and then going along either way. That's the knot of the song, clear eyes and total surrender in the same breath. The morning has a clock on it, and a secret too: back in New York nobody can find out. "We can say anything, but we just can't say it loud."

The music keeps that hush. A drum machine lays down a sparse, hip-hop-leaning beat with a prominent snare, and the tempo is slow and unbothered. A synth pad holds the chords while a synth bass walks underneath. Tamborello has said the main melodic loop is a simple synth line run through a delay modeler with the dry signal removed, so what you hear is only the echo. That suits a song about a moment that's already turning into memory. Oberst said he loved the beat and an "Alka-Seltzer" sound in it. He stays breathy and close to the mic, almost conversational, with a second voice adding harmony. The production is clean and polished, with a little reverb on the voice and nothing pushed.

It sticks because it lets you stand inside a hard kind of tenderness: caring for someone completely in a room you'll both have to leave before anyone back home notices.
