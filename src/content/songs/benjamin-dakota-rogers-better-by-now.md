The stars in **Better by Now** are doing everyone else a favor. They're "Shining down like kerosene lights," guiding sailors home, sending sinners to bed, chasing the monsters out of somebody's head. Benjamin Dakota Rogers opens the song with that whole list of comforts, then turns around and admits none of it reaches him. "Ain't it funny" opens both verses, and it isn't funny. It's the wry shrug of someone who can see the cure working on everybody but himself.

That gap between knowing and doing is the whole song. The refrain says the same line three times, as if repetition might make it stick, and then gives up: "But damn it I don't." In the second chorus that surrender comes three times in a row before the "Yeah baby" closes it out. It sounds less like a lesson learned than a confession made in the open, the narrator embarrassed and still unwilling to pretend otherwise.

The second verse holds the sharpest moment. The narrator can't even say her name, because it hurts, and the very next line he's talking straight to "you." The grief won't stay in the third person. Then comes the line that keeps him from simple blame, "you're not bad you're just bad for me," followed by an image of begging at your door like a dog looking for something to eat. Self-respect and longing sit right next to each other and neither one wins. Before the last chorus the song drops the excuses and just reports what was promised: "You said I'd always be your home." Said twice, it lands as the real wound underneath the self-scolding.

The sound stays close and plain. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through an arpeggio over a steady bass note, and the tempo is slow enough to let each line breathe. Rogers sings in a mid-range voice with a little rasp, unpolished, with no harmonies to soften it. The recording is dry, like a small room. Nothing is dressed up, so the words have to carry the weight, and they do.

Rogers wrote it, and Dan Hosh produced the record at Catherine North Studios in Hamilton, Ontario. It opens the album of the same name. Rogers came up as a fiddle player before he became a singer-songwriter, and the song carries that roots-folk plainness, a ballad more interested in an honest sentence than an ornament.

It sticks because it says out loud the thing you'd rather keep to yourself: you know exactly what's bad for you, and you go back anyway.
