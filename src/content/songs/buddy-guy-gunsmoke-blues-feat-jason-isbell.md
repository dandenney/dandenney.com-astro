The first surprise is the groove. **Gunsmoke Blues** is about school shootings, and it swings: a walking bass line, a shuffle with the snare up front, clean electric guitars trading lines, a medium tempo that never hurries. That ease is deliberate-sounding. The steadiness reads as exhaustion, the sound of people who have heard this story too many times to raise their voices about it.

Tom Hambridge and Richard Fleming wrote it in a repeat-and-answer shape: state the line, state it again, then close it out. "Trouble down at the high school" comes twice before the verse finishes with how most people meet these events, in the morning paper and on the evening news. The repetition does double duty. It's the blues habit of circling a line, and it's also the news cycle, the same headline coming back around.

The verses move from a school to a house of worship to a mother and her son. The church verse lands hardest. It ends flat on "God's people ain't here no more," and nothing softens it. Then a mother tells her son he isn't acting right and says she has a worried feeling. The song never says whether he's grieving, scared, or something worse. That gap holds the dread of a parent who can't name what she's afraid of.

The last verse turns on the people watching. Some blame the shooter, some blame the gun, and the song won't referee, because the bullets keep coming either way. Then comes the line the whole track leans on: "A million thoughts and prayers." The rest of it says they won't bring anyone back. Buddy Guy and Jason Isbell both sing it, and the lyric keeps circling back to it, with someone calling out for it to be said again. It's anger, but tired anger, the kind that has stopped trying to be polite.

The pairing tells its own story. Guy came to Chicago from Baton Rouge in 1957 with his guitar and the clothes he had on, and this was the lead single from his 34th studio album, which came out on the 65th anniversary of that arrival. Isbell comes from the Americana side. His part was recorded separately in Leipers Fork, Tennessee, and he said he couldn't imagine a higher honor than being on a song with Guy. The album sessions were at Blackbird in Nashville: Hambridge producing and on drums, Reese Wynans on piano and Wurlitzer, Michael Rhodes on bass, Rob McNelley on guitar. The vocals are conversational and a little raspy, set in clean, moderate room reverb. If anything, the polish keeps the rage tidier than the words are.

It sticks because the song won't shout, and that calm makes you hear how ordinary the grief has become.
