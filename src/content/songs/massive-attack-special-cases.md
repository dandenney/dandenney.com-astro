The sharpest word in **Special Cases** is a curse, and Sinéad O'Connor barely raises her voice to say it. "But check yourself for your own shit" arrives in the same hushed, breathy delivery as everything around it, low and slightly raspy, with no harmonies to cushion it. It sounds like a friend leaning across a table, keeping her voice down so only you hear the part that stings.

The song is advice, and not the comfortable kind. It speaks to someone about their man, and it opens with what not to do: don't list his failures, don't hand him every reason you cry. Then it turns the mirror around. "The deadliest of sin is pride," she sings, and that line explains the whole lecture. Pride is what makes you sure you're right. The song asks for a harder feeling, the humility of admitting a fight has two authors. Even its gentlest idea, that it takes two to make love and two to make a life, still carries the scold inside it.

What lifts it past a sermon is the refrain, which changes between its two appearances. The first time, the world is full of bad things and there are plenty of good men, so the question is whether he's one of them. The second time, the world has gone mad and the good men have thinned to a few. The question becomes gratitude: "Thank your lucky stars that he's one of them." Doubt turns into thanks, but only because the dark outside got darker. The relationship becomes shelter, and the song never says whether holding on is wisdom or fear. Both stay in the room.

Massive Attack build the track out of patience. A drum machine keeps a slow, steady beat, a prominent kick with sparse open hi-hats. A bass guitar repeats a simple line, and a clean electric guitar sits above it. The groove is laid back and melancholy, and nothing hurries. It sits in the Bristol trip hop tradition of Massive Attack, in the downtempo mode their later albums moved toward, with the tension handed to the voice.

Robert Del Naja and Neil Davidge, who produced 100th Window, wrote the song with O'Connor. It was one of three tracks she co-wrote for the album. Del Naja had said the band threw out most of the earlier material, that September 11 pushed him away from the record's original tone, and that the album used no existing samples. Davidge said they recorded every note played. The song was cut at Sony in London, became the album's first single, and reached #15 in the UK.

It offers a hard kind of kindness: it tells you to set down your pride, then shows you how cold the world is, and you come away holding on a little tighter.
