**NRICH** opens with a sample of the Last Poets' "Niggers Are Scared of Revolution," a voice from 1970, and the lyric starts by cataloguing all the ways a people can shoot: off at the mouth, craps, dope into an arm, guns and rifles on New Year's Eve. The sample pulls in the Black-nationalist spoken-word tradition. Killer Mike takes that list and spends four and a half minutes answering it, not with denial but with a plan.

The plan is money, and Mike means it literally. He name-checks Greenwood, the banking company he co-founded, and stacks the bars with ownership talk: "They used to own us, but now we own us, that's independence." It plays as a sermon, and AllMusic places it among the church organs, choral vocals and booming beats of the album MICHAEL, his first solo studio album in 11 years. But the boasts carry a sore spot. He remembers what he used to wear as something he's "embarrassed" by, then moves "through the Louvre smooth" in Paris. He traces his start plainly, "Born at Grady, a bastard baby to single lady," and credits God with getting him through. Then comes the line that gives the rest away: "It's underwhelmin' to be so overly underrated." The pride is real, and so is the wound of not being seen. Another line makes the bind explicit: they should be celebrated, but like everything else they'll have to fight for it. Mike doesn't settle that tension. He puts on the gloves.

Cool & Dre produced it, with 808-Ray co-producing, and the sound stays clean and close. A low synth bass repeats a simple line under kick, snare and hi-hat. A high synth pad hangs above the beat, and the mid-tempo groove stays relaxed. The rapping sits dry and upfront, every syllable clear, and a harmonized second voice layers over it. A 1977 Pleasure record, "Tune In," is folded into the sample bed, so a late-'70s record sits alongside the 1970 spoken-word one.

The guests do different work. 6LACK delivers a sung-rapped verse. Eryn Allen Kane, whom Hannibal Buress introduced to Mike, takes the chorus and turns the economics into a hymn: run up billions, build a village, walk by faith. Her refrain that time will reveal the truth lands like a benediction against Mike's ledger of grievances and gains.

The song holds the Last Poets' disappointment and Mike's ambition in the same room, and you leave carrying both: the hurt of a people told they're less, and the stubborn joy of someone who decided to own the answer.
