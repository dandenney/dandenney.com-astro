The scariest line in **American Man** is a prayer: "I pray to God that I never have to take it too far." The narrator sings it twice and means it each time. He doesn't want to hurt anyone. He also has guns, a grievance, and a flag, and the song spends four and a half minutes watching those things pull against his hope.

Nate Cook wrote it from inside the head of someone from his conservative small-town Texas hometown, a man he called a case of "patriotism to the point of psychosis." Cook has said he doesn't necessarily agree with him. The song never sneers, though. It's one of twelve narrators on The Yawpers' Bloodshot debut, a loose concept record about the American West, and Cook gives this one real grief to carry. The man's father held the ground sacred. Now the streets are trash, the culture wars are lost, and "This is my home and I'm a stranger here." That's displacement, the ache of outliving the world you were raised for, and it sits right next to the self-pity and the menace without canceling them out. He's out of tears, he says, and in the next verse he's bringing the plague.

The music refuses to panic with him. A clean electric guitar rolls through arpeggiated chords, the bass walks underneath, and the drums keep a plain, unhurried beat at a moderate pace. Cook sings in a slightly raspy, conversational mid-range with no harmonies to soften it. It's dry and close, recorded live to tape over four days at the Blasting Room, Bill Stevenson's Colorado studio, with Cracker's Johnny Hickman producing. The raw, small-room sound makes the narrator feel like he's talking across a kitchen table, calm enough that you almost nod along.

That's the trick of it. The Yawpers blend blues and country with punk energy, and here the heat stays in the words. The flag goes from grand to hollow, its applause canned. He salutes her with blood on his hands. Then the last stanza turns the violence inward: "I'll die for her and by my own hand." The devotion and the despair end up the same act, and the title line repeats and fades.

You come out of it understanding a man you'd cross the street to avoid, which is harder and more useful than hating him.
