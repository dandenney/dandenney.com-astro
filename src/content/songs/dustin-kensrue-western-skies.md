The joke in the chorus lands like a punch. **"Western Skies"** spends its opening verse on a familiar cowboy fantasy, freedom and open country and roaming until the mountains rise. Then it lets its narrator name the culprits for how it all went wrong: "Double-crossed by my blood / And some goddamn country song." It's self-pity with a sense of humor, and also a confession. He blames his restless nature, he blames the myth of the lone rider he soaked up from songs, and underneath both he knows he chose this.

The cruelest detail is in the second verse. The love at home didn't fight the leaving. They said they'd "never been a fan of fences" and told him to go see what he could see. That generosity is what wrecks him later, when he realizes he never had to choose between the horizon and the person who already let him roam. Later still he's tossing down another empty bottle, stumbling beneath those same western skies, admitting the view from Angel's Landing must be lovely but he can't see it through his tears. Kensrue has said the desert on this record is both a real place and a metaphor for waking up to what's in front of you. This narrator wakes up too late, staring at a vista he can't take in.

The last verse is where the language goes old-fashioned and biblical: "The band on my Stetson like thorns on my brow." The cowboy hat turns into a crown of thorns, and the costume of freedom becomes a penance. In the final chorus the line shrinks, losing the curse and the "country," as if he's run out of the energy to blame the genre and only the bare fact of the song is left.

The sound stays out of his way. A fingerpicked acoustic guitar rolls through arpeggios, a bass holds down the low end, and the tempo is slow and unhurried. The room sounds natural, which fits a record Kensrue wrote on acoustic guitar and mostly cut at home. His voice is clear with a slight rasp, one voice with no harmonies to cushion it. Sputnikmusic picked this as one of the album's standout vocal performances, and The Scope Of heard a ballad that would make Johnny Cash proud.

That Cash comparison is earned. Kensrue, the Thrice frontman who grew up a punk kid steering clear of country, leaned heavily on the country and western of the 1950s and '60s for *Desert Dreaming*. He doesn't wink at the form. He takes its lonesome-rider archetype and asks what it costs the people left at home.

The song gets under your skin by letting a man laugh bitterly at the songs that made leaving look noble, right up until he can't see the view he gave everything up for.
