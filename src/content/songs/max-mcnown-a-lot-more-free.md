The harmonica on **A Lot More Free** is supposed to hurt. Max McNown has said the harmonica, a harsher sound, stands for pain and heartbreak, while the whistle stands for freedom. So the song carries its argument in the arrangement. Over a strummed acoustic guitar and a plain bass line, the harmonica plays its melodic phrase, and the song holds both feelings the way the narrator does.

That split is the whole hook. "I'm a little bit hurt, but a lot more free" refuses to pick a side. It doesn't claim the wound has closed, and it doesn't wallow either. The line after it is the honest one: "I ain't saying that you never took a toll on me." He's guarding against a version of the story where he's simply fine, where the other person never mattered. He wants credit for getting out, and he wants it on record that it cost him.

McNown wrote it with Steve Fee and Jesse Reeves, and Fee produced. The opening stanza is seasons: leaves buried under snow, birds and flowers coming back through thawing ground. It's an old, plain image of time doing the healing, and the next stanza admits it's offering a picture of the feeling rather than a confession. After the chorus he stands on a mountain, sees rivers running like scars and carrying lifeblood, then asks what the song has been working toward: "Is it crazy that I'm grateful for all the pain?" It's the most exposed moment, phrased as a question because he isn't sure the gratitude is allowed.

McNown has said the song came out of relational betrayal, and that too many songs skip the redemption of escaping something bad. He keeps the details private, since people come to it for their own reasons. That choice shows. The "you" never gets a face, which leaves room for listeners to put their own person there. Onstage, he's said, he tries to return to the headspace he wrote it in, so fans still in the middle of their own hurt can feel met.

The sound is clean and close, moderate in tempo and relaxed, with drums behind the guitar and bass. The voice is clear with some rasp, sung straight with no harmonies stacked on top. It sits in the acoustic folk-country wave alongside Wyatt Flores and Sam Barber, and it took off online, a sleeper hit about a year after release. The spark was McNown's sister filming him walking in front of the Columbia River Gorge, which led him to pitch it as a travel song. For a song about leaving, that fits.

It sticks because it lets you be relieved and still sore at the same time, without asking you to decide which one is true.
