The cruelest thing about **Glimpse of Us** is how kind it is to the person it hurts. The new partner takes the world off the narrator's shoulders and turns rain into rainbows, and none of it is enough. "Perfect don't mean that it's working," Joji sings. It sounds like wisdom and works like an alibi. This is a song about lying in someone's arms and looking past them, and it never fully lets the narrator off the hook.

The chorus is where the confession lives. Looking into her eyes, the narrator finds the ex there, tries to fall for her touch, and keeps drifting back to the way it was. Then comes the giveaway: "Said, 'I'm fine' and said, 'I moved on.'" That doubled "said" marks these as things told to other people, maybe to the new partner herself, and the narrator knows they aren't true. The admission that this relationship is just a way to pass time is the ugliest moment in the song, and it's sung in the same soft voice as everything else.

The second verse turns toward the ex, and the feeling shrinks into something pettier. "Tell me he savors your glory, does he laugh the way I did?" Jealousy, then a quiet hope that the ex will get lonely someday and catch a glimpse of what was lost. Grief and spite share a breath and never sort themselves out. The chorus then comes around twice more to close, as if repeating the lie might make it true.

The sound keeps all of this close. A slow, arpeggiated piano carries the chords. The vocal is breathy and a little strained, slipping into falsetto or head voice with vibrato on the held notes, and a second voice shadows the melody a third below. The production is clean and close-miked, so every catch of breath lands right at your ear. Charlie Puth was sent the song first and never got to it while touring. He has said Joji's relaxed delivery suits it, and he's right. The unhurried singing makes quiet betrayal sound like ordinary weather.

Its roots run deeper than its plain surface. Riley McDonough first sketched the idea in a 2019 voice memo titled "Glimpse," about calling out to God and tied to the 2016 murder of YouTube singer Christina Grimmie. The co-writers found that memo in a later session and built the song around it that day, with Connor McDonough producing. Joji heard it about two years later and made changes of his own. A prayer became a breakup song, but the reaching for someone gone survived the change.

It was Joji's first new music since *Nectar* and the opener of *Smithereens*. It put a plain piano ballad at the front of the album from an artist who began as Pink Guy, and it became his first Hot 100 Top 10.

It sticks because it puts you inside the worst thing a lonely person can do, using someone's love as a window to look past them, and makes you feel how much it costs the one doing it.
