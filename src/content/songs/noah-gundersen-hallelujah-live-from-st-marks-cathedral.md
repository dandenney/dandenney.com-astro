A second voice joins on the title word, and the cathedral takes both of them and holds the sound a beat longer than either singer does. That held note is the whole argument of Noah Gundersen's **Hallelujah - Live from St. Mark's Cathedral**: a song of praise sung in a room built for praise, by someone raised inside that language.

The setting matters. Gundersen grew up in Centralia, Washington, home-schooled by conservative, religious parents, and left home at 18 to play around Seattle. On December 4, 2021, he played Saint Mark's Episcopal Cathedral in Seattle, a show announced as a full performance of his sixth album, *A Pillar of Salt*. Leonard Cohen's song turned up that night as a cover. A companion single, his own "Sleepless in Seattle," came from the same cathedral recording. He has called his work primarily autobiographical and described songwriting as a therapeutic process. Put those pieces together and a cover starts to feel personal. He isn't just borrowing a famous song. He's carrying an old vocabulary into a building where it has long been at home and seeing whether it still fits.

The arrangement doesn't try to answer that question. An acoustic guitar picks out arpeggiated chords, the tone clean and bright. The tempo is slow and unhurried, close to the pace of someone thinking out loud. Gundersen's voice is clear and sustained, and the notes are given room to hang. A harmony voice joins on the word itself. Nothing else is in the mix: guitar, two voices, and a lot of air. The room reverb works like a third instrument, and it makes the recording feel raw and close even as the space opens out behind it.

That restraint is the right call. Here the release comes from the room, not from the singer reaching for it. Gundersen sings the word plainly and lets the cathedral decide how much weight it carries. What he feels about it stays in the delivery, steady and careful. Reverence and distance can sit side by side in a performance like this, and he doesn't settle which one wins.

The second voice matters for the same reason. Praise sung alone can sound like a private wrestling match. With a harmony under it, it becomes something shared.

You hear someone stand in a church and say the oldest word in the room, and the building gives it back to you, longer than it left, as if it still believes for both of you.
