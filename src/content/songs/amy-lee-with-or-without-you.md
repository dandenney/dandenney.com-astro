**With or Without You** has always been a song about a bind, and Amy Lee strips it down until the bind is all that's left. A low synth pad hums underneath like something left running in an empty room. A clean electric guitar picks out arpeggios. The drums keep a slow ballad pace, kick and snare landing with weight, all of it soaked in reverb so the space feels huge and cold. Rolling Stone heard it as a minimalist, industrial take on an arena-rock original. That is the right frame. U2's 1987 version, from *The Joshua Tree*, played to the back row. Lee's keeps the walls up.

The swap matters. Lee traded the piano and strings of the original for heavy synth and pushed the lyrics to the front. Her voice is clear and strong, held long and steady, with a slight vibrato and no harmonies to soften it. That loneliness is the point, because the song describes a person caught between two kinds of pain. The refrain, which says the narrator can't live with or without this person, is not a romantic flourish here. It sounds like a diagnosis.

The writing holds more than one feeling at once, and the cover lets them sit unresolved. "On a bed of nails she makes me wait" brings in a third figure and turns patience into punishment. Then comes the line that gives the narrator away: "You give it all, but I want more." There's grievance in it, and also an ugly, honest admission that nothing would be enough. The refrain, "And you give yourself away," cuts in both directions. It could be an accusation or a lament for someone erasing themselves, and the repetitions trail off into a bare "and you give" as if the sentence can't be finished. One stanza reads like a body counting its damage: hands tied, bruised, nothing to win and nothing left to lose.

Lee has said love is rarely simple and more often tears your guts out, which is why the song lingers in her dreams. She saw U2 at Madison Square Garden in 2015, called this her favorite of their tracks, and came away wanting to play live again. The dark version was playing in her head as she woke up one morning, and she went straight upstairs and laid down the synth and a basic vocal. It became the second cover on her new YouTube channel, a home for her work outside Evanescence, after Portishead's "It's a Fire." The black-and-white video shows her by foliage and at work in her home studio, and the track was later collected on the *Recover, Vol. 1* EP.

What sticks is the refusal to let the chorus lift you out of the trap, so you stay inside that cold, echoing room with someone who can neither leave nor stay.
