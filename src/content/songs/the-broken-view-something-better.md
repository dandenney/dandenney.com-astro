The most telling line in **Something Better** is a correction: "I'm not insane, I'm only a fool for you." The narrator defends against a charge nobody has made yet, and that tells you how far this has gone. Austin Kranick, The Broken View's frontman, has said he wrote the lyrics about moving on from a toxic relationship. He described the anger of being shut out of someone's life until it turns into an obsession. The song sits inside that obsession, even as it starts to turn toward leaving it.

Listen to how the repeated passage is built. A run of lines lets the other person off the hook: you don't have to lose, don't have to lie, don't have to choose, don't have to say goodbye. It sounds generous. Then it flips. The narrator doesn't have to absorb all the time wasted on dishonesty, and then comes the stubborn heart of it: "I will not be the one who never got the chance to change your mind." Someone is leaving and still arguing the case on the way out. Wounded pride and real longing sit in the same sentence, and neither gives ground. Elsewhere the bitterness shows plainly, "It's all gone sideways," and both people end up scared and wrecked, but blame never fully lands on one side.

The sound carries that pressure. A distorted guitar holds thick, sustained chords over a bass that keeps the low end planted. The drums push a driving rock groove at a moderate tempo with a hard kick and snare. The vocal is doubled and held long on the notes, clean in tone but with some grit at the edges, as if holding it together takes effort. It's dense and polished, and it's the most metal-sounding of the five songs on the *Something Better* EP. That fits a band from Saratoga Springs, New York, working in the independent alt-hard-rock and alt-metal scene upstate. Drummer Tyler Shoemaker started the band in 2018 after recording Kranick's solo work at his own studio, Greyscale Recordings in Malta, NY. The song is credited to the two of them, and the band records its own material.

The band name comes from the "break views" of graphic design, which show how an object works from the inside. That's a fair description of what this song does to a breakup. The ending goes long, the title phrase repeated again and again, and at 5:15 some listeners will feel the stretch. But the repetition reads as a mantra more than filler. Kranick has said he hopes people in the same spot find their own "something" that gets them to a better state of mind. The closing lines sound like someone still trying to believe it, "Believe in something better than you," saying it until it might be true.

You keep coming back because the song catches the exact moment when walking away and refusing to let go are the same sentence.
