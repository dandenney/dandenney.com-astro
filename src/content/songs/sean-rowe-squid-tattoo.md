A squid tattoo is a strange detail to fall for, and that's the point. It's the kind of thing you'd notice on a stranger across a room and build a whole life around before they ever turned to look at you. Sean Rowe has said **Squid Tattoo** is about exactly that: the brief, romantic rush of imagining a future with someone you haven't actually met, a future he admits will most likely never happen. Holler heard it as a new romance with the person wearing the ink. Both readings land on the same pleasant ache, the early glow where everything is still possible because nothing has been tested.

What keeps the song from turning mopey is the groove. An acoustic guitar strums steady time, a clean electric picks out arpeggios that hang in the air a little longer than they need to, and the bass walks instead of sitting on the root. The drums keep a level pace with the snare out front. The tempo is moderate and the feel is relaxed, a band settled into a pocket rather than pushing at it. Holler also points to James Brown-style honking horns, which pull a folk-rock songwriter toward a soul-revue strut. The Wall Street Journal once compared Rowe's baritone to late-'60s Van Morrison, and a groove like this gives that comparison room to breathe.

Rowe's voice is slightly raspy and stays conversational. He doesn't oversell the fantasy. He sounds like someone narrating a daydream while fully aware it's a daydream, enjoying the picture and keeping one foot outside it. That's the honest part. The hope and the doubt sit together, and the song never asks you to choose between them. The upbeat groove carries the pleasure, and Rowe's own account of the song carries the knowledge that it won't last. You get both at once, the way you do when you let yourself picture a life with someone and already know you'll never say hello.

Rowe, who grew up in Troy, New York, wrote the words and music. Most of the album was cut in ten days at Hive, Brian Joseph's studio in Eau Claire, Wisconsin. Joseph, a Grammy-winning engineer who has worked with Bon Iver, recorded it alongside producer Troy Pohl, and Pohl and Rowe then spent months finishing it. The result is clean and well balanced, every instrument with room to stand. Courtney Hartman flew in from Colorado to sing background vocals and play guitar on the album.

The song lets you enjoy a crush on someone you'll never know, and it makes the impossibility feel like part of the fun.
