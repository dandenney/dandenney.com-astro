The most revealing moment in **Im Fine** is a single word that changes between choruses. The first time, the narrator sings "This ain't like me." The second time, it's "They ain't like me." A confession about losing yourself turns into a reason to pull away from everyone else, and the song never stops to notice. That small slide is the whole emotional mechanism of the track. It's how a person talks himself out of asking for help.

The verse lays out the despair plainly. Not wanting to wake up, feeling vile, and then a shrug: "But hey I been here before." That line carries two feelings at once. There's a weary pride in having survived this place, and there's the grief of knowing the place by heart. Then the prayer arrives, and it isn't eloquent. "God help me" repeats four times in a row, then comes a fifth, reversed and running into the admission of being messed up all the time. The repetition sounds like someone who has run out of other words. Next comes the suspicion that the world itself is an evil simulation, or else that the mind is gone. The song lets both explanations stand.

The chorus is where the hiding starts. A throat that can't swallow, the certainty that people don't like him, a decision to leave before anyone has to ask. "Save our time" is generous on its surface, sparing everyone the trouble, and it's what makes the closing "I'm fine" land as the lie it is.

The sound matches that composure. Witchz is a solo project out of Las Vegas, with Michael Lattino credited as writer, and the artist has said he makes all the music himself. The bed is clean and polished. A low synth holds sustained notes, a clean electric guitar picks arpeggiated chords through a light delay, and kick and snare keep a slow, almost dragging pulse. The voice is breathy and slightly strained, with some vocal fry, moving between sung lines and phrases that are nearly spoken. There are no harmonies. Nobody joins in, which suits a song about deciding to go it alone.

The project's reputation runs toward bass-heavy darkness, with lyrics mostly about "the mental battle within," and this track fits that internet-era alternative lane, electronic production alongside guitar. It's the same lane as his Nirvana cover, which has passed 13 million Spotify plays. What sets this song apart is restraint. The playing stays smooth and even under words that are coming apart.

The song puts you inside the moment someone tells you they're fine, and it lets you hear everything they left out.
