"Wakin' up worthless" is the first thing **The Hard Way** says, and the next line goes further. Cameron Whitcomb sings that he should have worked up the courage to kill himself, with no metaphor to soften it. Then comes a strange, almost funny image: a Mona Lisa sitting on his shelf, and the question of whether he deserved it. That's the bind the song lives in. Something precious is right there in the room, and the narrator can't decide whether he has earned the right to look at it.

The chorus doesn't resolve that. It concedes. He tells someone never named that they were right all along, and what he admits to is odd and exact: "I was addicted to heartache." The craving gets moved from a substance to a feeling, to the lies he was sold and wanted to love anyway. "I had to do it the hard way" works as an apology and a quiet boast at once, the pride of someone who made it through his own stubbornness next to the grief of what it cost. The song lets both stand. The lines set off in parentheses, where the other voice says he was right to call him wrong, read like the people around him saying it back.

The second verse turns outward, and it's the loneliest part. The friends he admired have grown up into liars, and they answer him with a rehearsed "I'm doing fine." He's tired of chasing a peace he was promised, and goodbye becomes a lonely hill to climb. Whitcomb has said getting clean didn't solve everything for him, and that his mental-health ups and downs became more extreme. The song sounds like that admission. It is a recovery song that refuses to pretend recovery ends anywhere.

The band pushes hard under it. A strummed acoustic guitar keeps time beneath a distorted, sustained electric melody. The bass sits on root notes while the drums drive a rock beat. The voice is raw and slightly strained, and the open room has a raw edge to it. Apple Music places the album beside Zach Bryan and Sam Barber with alt-rock and punk touches, and this track leans toward the rock end. That fits a writer who says, "I don't write in genres. I just write songs."

Whitcomb wrote it with Jack Riley, Cal Shapiro and Nolan Winfield Sipe. Riley and Shapiro produced it. The chorus comes around again and again, and the repetition can feel heavy, but that's the confession: he has to keep saying it.

The song makes you sit with someone who is still here and not sure what that's worth, and it won't let you choose one feeling over the other.
