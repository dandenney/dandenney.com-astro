Someone on a bicycle rolls past a window at night, then comes back on roller skates at daylight, and only then admits what's going on: "I'm okay alone, but you've got something I need." That line is the whole trick of **Brand New Key**. It's a brag and a plea in the same breath. The narrator wants you to know they're fine, really, and also that they've been circling your house since last night.

Melanie Safka wrote it alone in about fifteen minutes and never pretended it was deep. She called it cute, "a kind of old thirties tune," and dismissed it as something she was fooling with on a practice guitar in the van. By one account she wrote it right after finishing a McDonald's burger that broke a fast, while living in New Jersey. Her husband, Peter Schekeryk, overheard it and told her it was a hit. He was right. It went to No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100 across December 1971 and January 1972 and sold more than three million copies.

The record keeps that throwaway spirit. Roger Kellaway arranged it, and it opens with a simple piano intro. The track itself runs on steady acoustic strumming, a walking bass line, and a plain backbeat. The groove is relaxed and slightly swung, a moderate stroll rather than a sprint, which suits a narrator who gets around without a car. The sound is dry and close, almost like the band is in the room with you. Melanie's voice is clear and a little nasal, and the delivery is conversational, like someone talking to you over a fence. Melanie recorded it at Allegro Studios in New York and left for California. When she got back, Schekeryk had added doo-wop backing vocals.

It's easy to hear only the novelty, and some radio stations heard only innuendo and banned it. But the verses hold a quiet sting. The narrator asks the mother if you're home: "She said, yes, but you weren't alone." The narrator doesn't dwell on it. One more "sometimes I think you're avoiding me," then straight back to the chorus, and later a run of la-la-las. The bruise is there, and the song won't let it slow things down. Another verse is the narrator's real defense: "Don't go too fast, but I go pretty far." It's pride from someone who has been told they've done all right for a girl and decided to take the compliment and keep pedaling.

The song was a one-off for an artist from the Woodstock folk scene whose catalog was mostly earnest, and she described her 1972 album Stoneground Words as a statement that it was exactly that. Maybe that's why it works so well. It's the sound of a serious songwriter being silly, and the silliness has a little hurt tucked inside it.

It sticks because it lets you be rejected and unbothered at once, humming along while you skate past the window one more time.
