**Snowbank Blues** started as a bit. Joshua Harmon has said he was imitating the riff of a classic country tune to make his father laugh, then realised the parody had real potential. He and Jonas Swanson knocked out a garage demo in about two hours, improvising the structure and shaking a rice-filled Altoid case for percussion. That origin explains a lot. The song opens on the stock phrases of a working-stiff lament: the boss, the daily grind, smoking with friends to get through the night. Then it quietly stops joking.

The turn comes before the first verse is out. Harmon sings, "Catching up on sleep's harder when I'm waking up next to me." It's a clever line, a near-pun on sleeping alone, and it's also the loneliest thing in the song. Someone is gone, a thousand miles off. The narrator is left with a girl down the road sending looks and a question he asks out loud so he doesn't have to answer it: how can he say no when you're that far away? Self-justification and longing arrive in the same breath, and the song doesn't sort them out.

Then the frame widens. Coffee, cameras in his face, too many people in this place, a mask shelved and a stage to take. He admits the smile isn't for himself, that something on his chest is wearing on his health. It's the strain of being watched while missing someone. The refrain pins it to place: he'd roll the windows down if the snow hadn't swallowed the town. Even the urge to get out is snowed in. When he says tomorrow he'll be gone, maybe a thousand miles away, he's measuring himself against the distance between them.

The sound keeps the demo's promise. A fingerpicked guitar line, clean and unhurried, moves at an easy, moderate pace. Harmon's voice is slightly raspy and conversational, with no harmonies around it. Strummed chords fill things out, and a plain kick-snare-hi-hat beat gives the groove a push. The production is dry and close, raw rather than buffed. That's the feel Harmon said they tried to carry from the garage into the studio, on their first record made with an outside producer, David Greenbaum.

The last lines drop the cool. He grants that they had to leave, and then the accusation comes: "But you didn't have the right / To take the sun." It's unfair, and he half knows it. That's why it stings.

The song leaves you holding that lopsided grief, the kind that understands why someone had to go and still blames them for the cold.
