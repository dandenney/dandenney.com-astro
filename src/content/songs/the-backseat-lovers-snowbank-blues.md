The loneliest line in **Snowbank Blues** comes early and almost slips past: "Catching up on sleep's harder when I'm waking up next to me." Someone else should be there. Instead the narrator wakes beside the one person they've had too much of. Everything after that is a list of ways to get away from yourself: the job, the smoke with friends, the girl down the road, the promise of being gone tomorrow.

The Backseat Lovers wrote it as a band, with Joshua Harmon, Jonas Swanson, Juice Welch and KJ Ward all credited. Harmon has said it began as a joke, a way to make his father laugh by imitating the riff of a classic country tune. Then he saw potential in the parody. He and Swanson made a garage demo in about two hours, improvising the structure and using a rice-filled Altoid case as a shaker, and the band tried to keep that homemade feel when they recorded it for *Waiting to Spill* with producer David Greenbaum. You can hear the parody's origin in the shape of the complaints: working for the man, a girl down the road, a town snowed in. They're familiar griefs, and the song hands them to someone who can't stand still.

What makes it more than a joke that got out of hand is how honest it is about guilt. The temptation verse doesn't pretend to be innocent. The narrator asks how they can say no when "you're" a thousand miles away, and the question is half excuse, half confession. Then the distance flips. Later the narrator is the one who'll be gone tomorrow, with coffee in the cup, cameras in the face, and enough of the people in the room.

That's where the anxiety comes up. Something on their chest is messing with their health, and the smile everyone sees isn't for them. Then comes the strangest line: "Take the stage and put the mask back on the shelf." It might mean the stage is the only place they can stop pretending. It might mean the mask is just waiting there for the next show. The song doesn't choose.

The repeated couplet wants something simple, the windows rolled down, and the snow won't allow it. Then the closing lines turn on the person who left. The narrator grants they had to go, but they had no right "To take the sun." Understanding and accusation sit together in that line, and neither one wins.

The song lets you hold a grudge and a heartache at the same time, and admit you're not sure which one is keeping you up.
