The kindest line in **Rich People** isn't a punchline. It's "Don't be ashamed if you get confused," sung to anyone who has walked away from the news or an argument with friends feeling stupid for not knowing what to say. Carsie Blanton's answer is a gift: "Now you don't have to argue just sing this song." The song is a lyric sheet for the tongue-tied, a chorus you can hand to someone who knows something is wrong but keeps getting told to blame the wrong people.

That gentleness is what lets the anger land. Blanton has said she wrote it after Charlottesville, and late in the song it turns into a run of questions and answers that names the scapegoats one at a time: Jews, Black people, immigrants, and then, pointedly, rednecks. Each question gets a flat denial and a redirect to the same answer. Putting rednecks on that list matters. It refuses to let a song against prejudice keep a target of its own, and it reaches toward the people a protest song usually writes off. Under the joke there is grief about neighbors turned against each other, and the song never sets that grief and the joke apart.

Blanton calls it a jazzy, jokey song about capitalism, and the band plays it that way. Fingerpicked acoustic guitar, a walking bass line and light brushed drums sit in a relaxed, slightly swung groove at an easy moderate tempo. The room sounds dry and close. The voice is clear and conversational, almost talking the lines in rhythm, so the profanity in the chorus comes off less as a shout than as a shrug from someone who has run out of polite ways to say it. The 1979 verse does the history lesson in four lines and closes the joke with a twist of the knife: Reagan and Thatcher "fixed it right up, but not for you."

The craft shows up in the revisions. Blanton rewrote the third and fourth verses before the tour that preceded the release, to get more consistent laughs, and the song works like a bit built for rooms full of people. Patrick Firth produced and mixed it, Joe Plowman played bass and arranged it, and Will Tyson engineered it at The Studio in Nashville. The song went viral on TikTok, about 50,000 strangers asked where to get it, and Blanton, an independent artist without label money, put it out on day seven of a 21-day run. It belongs to a long line of American topical songs, next to her own Love & Rage and Ugly Nasty Commie Bitch. It works best as a singalong, a song meant to be passed around.

It takes the shame out of not having the words and gives you a chorus you can sing back at the news.
