In the last choruses of **Hey Baby**, one word changes. At first the singer has to "bear" a cross. Later it's "bury." The weight he's been carrying out the door gets put in the ground, and the man who kept leaving finally sounds like he's coming home. It's a small rewrite, and it holds the whole song: a promise that the long absence was worth it, made by someone who badly needs that to be true.

The opening verse is a leaving speech, and it argues with itself. "I got to do what I must do to be a better man," Stephen Marley sings, which is pride and apology in the same breath. He insists the road is how he keeps his blessings from turning into curses. He also keeps asking to be forgiven for it. Watch the plea shift between verses: first it's "please don't fuss," then "please don't cry." The person at home has gone from annoyed to hurt, and the singer knows it. He doesn't resolve that. He just keeps promising.

Read it as a love song and it works. But it was written for Marley's children. He used to sing it to them on the road, and they now call it theirs. That changes the "baby." The guilt becomes a parent's guilt, and the promise is being made to someone too young to argue back. The tender images, wind and tree, warmth of day and cool of night, start to sound like a father trying to describe a closeness he can't be there to give.

The band keeps all of this gentle. A steady, mid-tempo reggae beat lands its snare on two and four. The bass line stays simple. A clean electric guitar picks out an arpeggiated figure, and keyboards hold sustained chords underneath. The groove has a slight swing, relaxed and unhurried. The lead voice is clear with a little rasp, and a second voice shadows it in unison or a third above. That gives the song the feel of a family singalong. The polish is real, and one review hears a neo-soul sheen in it, part of the blend that drew grumbling from some reggae purists on the album. Here, though, it suits a lullaby.

Mos Def's verse was a rap he'd had for two years, remixed for the album. Marley says it adds "swagger," and he picked his friend as a conscious voice. Mos Def brings the loneliness the singing smooths over: "And the spot is hot it's cold and lonely at nights." He's admitting the road costs the traveler too, not just the people left behind. Then he lands on the song's real comfort: every road comes to an end, and he's back home again.

What sticks is how hard the song works to reassure. You can hear a parent soothing a child, and at the same time soothing himself that the leaving was worth it.
