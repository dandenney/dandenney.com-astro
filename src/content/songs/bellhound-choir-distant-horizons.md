The first line breaks in the wrong place. "I'm too tired to / To sleep" hangs on that dangling "to," doubles it, and only then reaches the verb, the way a sentence comes out at three in the morning when the mind has stopped finishing its thoughts. **Distant Horizons** opens on that kind of tiredness, the kind past the point where rest is still possible, and everything after is sung from inside it.

Christian Hede Madsen, who wrote the song and records as Bellhound Choir, keeps the words few and the picture blurry. The narrator works on orders from something called the beast. In the first verse a lesson rolls through the air the narrator breathes. By the second, the beast lives in the poison the narrator drinks. That shift is the confession. A threat that was in the atmosphere, something you can't help taking in, is now something you pour for yourself. The song never says what the poison is, and doesn't need to.

Between the two choruses sits the strangest moment. The narrator is too close to someone, that someone is too close back, and there's a whole explanation held behind "if you only knew." Closeness here is both what the narrator wants and the danger. Tenderness and warning arrive together, and neither cancels the other.

Then the chorus. The distant horizon is falling, said three times, then "Well, and it won't be long 'til it hits me." The far edge of the world, the one thing that's supposed to stay far, is coming down. That "Well" is a shrug before impact. There's no fight in it and no plea, just dread worn as patience.

The band plays it calm. A strummed acoustic guitar with a clean, bright tone carries the song, over a simple bass line (the album credits Mads Uldall-Jessen on upright bass) and a slow, steady kick, snare, and hi-hat. Sebastian Wolff of Kellermensch plays drums on the record and produced it. A clean electric guitar sustains above it all. The lead vocal is clear, mid-range, and unhurried, and Camilla Munch, who sings on several of the album's songs, also appears on this one. The room sounds open and natural. That steadiness is the trick: the words describe collapse, and the music won't flinch.

Madsen came to this from Danish stoner rock, fronting Pet the Preacher before turning Bellhound Choir toward darker country, folk, and blues, a strain one writer called "Scandinavian blues noir." Bad Afro Records was drawn to the album's dark, stripped-down alternative blues, and Rockfreaks picked this track as one of its most stunning. It shows what that move kept. The heaviness is still there, only without the volume. The lyric is spare enough to read as a sketch, but the gaps are where the fear lives.

It lets you stand next to someone who can see the sky coming down and feel how tired you'd have to be to sing about it that calmly.
