A dog at the back screen porch doesn't hate the house. It just can't stop looking at the yard. That's the image Keni Titus opens **hands to myself** with, "I look at you like my dog sees the back screen porch," and she has said the line sums up the song. It's a funny, tender way to describe wanting: a little ridiculous, a little helpless, nose pressed to the mesh. Titus has described the song as being about temptation and the idea that the grass is greener elsewhere, and about wondering whether you took something for granted. The screen does the work. Whatever's out there is visible, and close, and you're still on the inside.

The verses are full of half-steps. The narrator wonders whether being the friend who holds you while the weed kicks in could turn into something more, then lays the cards on the table with eyes on the door. The chorus is where it gets slippery. "I love you, but not 'til I've had someone else" is a confession with a loophole built in. Then comes the sharpest turn in the song: "I mean it, I'm mean but it's good for my health." The narrator is sincere and knows the sincerity is a little cruel, and justifies it as self-care in the same breath. Those feelings don't settle, and the song doesn't make them.

The second verse is where the cost shows. The narrator stays up at someone else's place, finally has the nerve, and ends up missing the heart they broke. By the last chorus the lyric stretches out with extra lines. The fling is "harmless," the narrator insists, then admits the name is already hard to remember. The other person fades almost as soon as they appear. What lingers is the old feeling the narrator was trying to test. Even keeping your hands to yourself turns into a way of remembering.

The music never raises its voice about any of this. A strummed acoustic guitar and a walking bass carry a relaxed shuffle, with brushes on the drums and a sustained, slightly distorted electric guitar line drifting over the top. It's warm and polished, with an open room around it. Titus sings alone, with no harmonies, in a clear, slightly breathy voice that keeps its melodies long and even. That calm is the point. Big guilt is delivered at an easy sway.

The song was co-written with Will Taylor of Hovvdy. It sits in Los Angeles indie-folk-pop, leaning on West Coast folk and the confessional diary song. It is the third single from Titus's debut album AngelPink, which Jake Weinberg and Hudson Pollock produced. She made the album through a breakup and a falling-out with a friend, and has said the project reminded her that making art is supposed to be fun, "Not perfect — but fun." You can hear that lightness here, even with heartbreak in the room.

It sticks because it lets you admit the restless, unkind thing you want, then sends you back inside missing what you nearly traded away.
