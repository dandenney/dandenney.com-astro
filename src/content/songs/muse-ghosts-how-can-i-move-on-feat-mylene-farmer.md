The cruelest line in **Ghosts (How Can I Move On)** is a question about property. "When all the best things I have we made together?" Grief is supposed to be about absence. Here it's about everything that's still in the room, every good thing in the narrator's life carrying a second set of fingerprints. The second time through, "made" becomes "built," and the shift makes the shared life sound more solid.

Matt Bellamy wrote the song during lockdown, and French press describes it as dedicated to the loss of a loved one. Read that way, the confession in the second verse lands hard. The narrator wants one more conversation to set things straight and believes the person they lost would "love and forgive" them. That belief sounds like hope and like guilt at once, and the song never decides which. Meanwhile the chorus raises a glass to letting go in the same breath that admits being lost in a void with a ghost. The toast and the drowning sit side by side. Nobody moves on.

The arrangement keeps close to the piano. It rolls out arpeggiated chords at a slow, deliberate pace, and a simple bass line holds the floor. Strings come in on sustained chords that swell and pull back like breath. The room sound is spacious, the production polished. Bellamy has said Muse looked over their albums and setlists and found they lacked a good ballad. He was surprised Chris Wolstenholme and Dominic Howard agreed to put this one on *Will of the People*.

This duet version gives the song a second mourner. Bellamy said the band always felt the emotion would grow with a powerful female voice, and Mylène Farmer joins his clear, slightly breathy high tenor in unison. Two people singing one line is a different picture from one person singing alone. Farmer also adapted part of the lyric into French and is credited as co-writer under her legal name, Mylene Jeanne Gautier. The French press reads her version as a break-up song, and the lyric holds up either way. A death and a breakup leave the same coldness, and two lines say it with one word changed:

"How can I sleep with this coldness beside me?" / "How can I sleep with this coldness inside me?"

The empty side of the bed becomes the empty space in the chest. That's the whole song in a single rhyme on itself. Farmer said Bellamy gave her a huge gift by letting her sing alongside him, and she has called him as fascinating as Freddie Mercury. You can hear a singer handling someone else's grief with care.

It sticks because it lets you hold the toast and the grief at once, raising a glass to letting go while admitting you haven't let go at all.
