The chorus of **Girl Like Me** states its boast once, whole, then keeps tripping. "Never met a girl like, never met a girl like," again and again, the sentence cut off right where it should land. The song's last line is the one that finally closes it with "me." The tease is built into the grammar. Dove Cameron spends two and a half minutes describing herself to a man, and the sentence only comes full circle at the very end.

The bones come from Edwyn Collins' 1994 hit "A Girl Like You," and Collins shares the writing credit with Cameron, Evan Blair and Lowell. Cameron said she'd loved that song for about 15 years and first thought of covering it on tour, then decided to flip it so the question became "have you ever met a girl like me before." The spark was a specific image: Demi Moore's villain scene in *Charlie's Angels: Full Throttle*, where the Collins song plays. That tells you the stance. Collins sang about the girl. Cameron answers as the girl, and she plays her as the villain who knows the camera is on her.

The verses are pure taunt. "Nothing makes you smaller, than being a big man" is the sharpest line here, an insult built as a neat little paradox. She calls him heathen and handsome like pet names with a blade in them, mocks his tantrum, and leads him through the dance while asking if a woman has ever been allowed to do that. Then one line slips sideways: "And I'm not even here I'm just a phantom." It comes tossed off between jabs, and it lets in a cold draft. The power she's describing might belong to a character more than a person, a role worn so well that whoever's inside it half vanishes. The song never stops to sort that out. Contempt, appetite and a flicker of absence share the same verse, and the swagger holds.

Blair, who also produced "Bad Idea," "Boyfriend" and "Breakfast," frames it in glossy, driving pop: a four-on-the-floor kick and snare with hi-hats, synth bass, a sustained pad, and a plucked arpeggiated figure that keeps everything leaning forward. After the first chorus a distorted electric guitar comes in with long held notes, a rougher edge for a song built on a 1994 hit. Cameron's voice is clear and bright, the delivery confident and rhythmic, sliding toward speech in spots, while layered harmonies fill out the sound behind her. The mix is open and reverberant, roomy enough that every taunt carries.

The stutter is clever for a long time, and it runs a little long by the last pile-up of repeats.

What sticks is the unfinished phrase: the song keeps stopping just short of the word, and by the time the last line lands on "me," you've already agreed.
