**Raising Hell** opens on a brag that's really a worry. "Well my little brother's raising hell," Ben Nichols sings, and the next lines pile up the evidence: whiskey nights, rodeos, strippers in Austin, a kid who probably ought to be in jail and can't remember the good times he keeps telling everyone about. There's pride in the telling, the way an older sibling passes along the wild stories. There's also worry, because the brother's money is running out and a job is the one option he won't consider.

Then Nichols, who wrote the song, turns to someone else. The next portrait belongs to a "you" in a place where winter is cold and wet at once and the roof leaks. "Make keeping dry a losing game" is the line doing the work: a practical complaint that is plainly about crying too. Your boy is coming home and it won't help. The narrator tells the "you" to dry their eyes and say goodbye. The song never says who the narrator is to them, and the tenderness holds either way.

The last portrait is his own, and it's the least flattering. It's the same old pull downward and the same big plans always in the works, with a bet that they'll pay off before his luck gives out. He admits he doesn't know if he's right, then digs in: "but i can tell you that i'm having fun." That line holds the song's mix of self-defense and ache. Fun is the alibi. All three people end up facing the same choice of where to run, and he chooses running itself. Then the lyric circles back to the brother in Texas, as if the whole cycle starts over.

The band plays it easy. A clean electric guitar picks out arpeggiated chords, the bass walks underneath, and the drums keep a steady, relaxed mid-tempo. Nichols sings in the middle of his range, raw and unvarnished, and a second voice slips in to harmonize on some phrases. The room sounds natural, like a band standing in one space. That fits, since Kevin Houston recorded the album in the Dickinson family's barn in Coldwater, Mississippi, across sessions in 1999 and 2000, with Cody Dickinson and Lucero producing. This is Memphis alt-country with punk still under its fingernails, though here the storytelling is up front. Nichols has said the debut shows where Lucero was heading. You can hear it in this song's character sketches, its shrug, and the hurt underneath.

The song sticks because it lets you feel proud of the people you love and scared for them in the same breath, and it counts the singer among them.
