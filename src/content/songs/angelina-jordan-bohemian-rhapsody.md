Angelina Jordan's **Bohemian Rhapsody** opens on a shrug: "Anywhere the wind blows." Nothing has happened yet, and the narrator is already acting like nothing could. Everything after it is a goodbye. This is an understated, abridged take on Freddie Mercury's rock epic, and what's been cut tells you as much as what stayed. What's left is someone who has run out of time.

The arrangement is about as bare as it gets. A piano rolls out arpeggiated chords over a sustained low bass note, and the tempo is slow and loose, working in rubato rather than locking to a beat. The room has some air in it, the feel of a medium-sized space around the voice and piano, and the sound is clean and polished. There are no harmonies. One voice carries everything, clear and resonant, with a wide vibrato.

That voice belonged to a 13-year-old. Jordan sang this on the premiere of *America's Got Talent: The Champions* in January 2020, and Heidi Klum hit the Golden Buzzer. Howie Mandel told her it would change her life and career forever. The single followed a few weeks later. She was born in Oslo and had been at this since age seven, when she auditioned for Norway's Got Talent with "Gloomy Sunday" and went on to win with "Summertime." That jazz-leaning background shows in how she takes the melody. Billboard heard a lilting ballad that reinvented a bombastic classic, with added riffs, runs and trills.

The emotional trick is the gap between the frame and the middle. The narrator opens claiming indifference, then cracks: "I don't wanna die." The next line is worse, the wish to never have been born at all. Then the narrator retreats to "Nothing really matters to me," the same pose as the opening, now plainly a pose. The fear and the calm never settle the argument. They just take turns.

Jordan has kept performing her own arrangement since, including at 16, and it's easy to hear why it held. Stripping out the bombast leaves a frightened person trying to sound brave, and the bare piano and bass give nowhere to hide either one. The result is closer to a lullaby for someone afraid of the dark.

What sticks is the moment the shrug stops working, when the insistence that nothing matters starts to sound like the most desperate line you've heard.
