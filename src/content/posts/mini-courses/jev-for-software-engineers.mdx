## Lesson 1: Fast thinking for software

The name comes from Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. System 1 is the fast, intuitive judgment you make without deliberating. System 2 is the slow, effortful reasoning. Most of the LLM conversation for the last few years has been about System 2: models that write, plan, and reason out loud.

Jev goes the other way. TypeSafe's one-line pitch is **unstructured state in, typed probabilistic decisions out**. You hand it some text or JSON, ask it questions, and get back numbers your code can branch on. It can't write a sentence, generate code, summarize, or chat. [The docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/system-one) are blunt about that, and it's the point.

If you've ever wired an LLM into a product to answer "is this a refund request?" you know the routine: write a prompt, ask for JSON, parse it, retry when it isn't JSON, and then wonder what `"confidence": "high"` actually means. A System One model skips straight to the part you wanted. One of the TypeSafe founders described it on Hacker News as a "frontier-intelligence function call," and the docs call the use case "smart if-statements."

The vendor numbers are eye-catching: 70 to 500 ms per request, $0.042 per million input tokens, and output tokens are free. Those are TypeSafe's claims from their launch, comparing against frontier models doing the same decisions. Keep them in mind as a reason to look, not a reason to believe. Lesson 6 is about that.

## Lesson 2: Three primitives, three statements

Every Jev question is one of three types. The trick that made it click for me: each one maps to a control-flow statement you already write every day.

- **Noul** (as in Bernoulli) is a yes/no question. It returns one number between 0 and 1: the probability the answer is yes. It's an `if`.
- **Choice** picks one option from a set you define. It returns the winner, a probability for every option, and a confidence. It's a `match` or `switch`.
- **Score** rates something against ordered levels you describe. It returns a scalar position, probabilities per level, and a confidence. It's what you `sort` by.

You define the questions at request time. There's no fine-tuning or training a classifier per task. You change behavior by changing the question, its instructions, and its criteria.

## Lesson 3: State in, decisions out

A request has two parts. The **state** is the thing being judged: a string, a JSON object, or an array of strings. Text only for now; no images, audio, or video. The **questions** are what you want to know about it. Every question sees the same state, each one is answered independently, and they run in parallel, so asking five questions doesn't cost five round trips.

Keep those two things separate. The state should be facts about the world, not instructions. Put your framing in each question's `instructions` and `criteria`. Criteria are where most of the quality comes from: for a Noul you can describe what counts as true and false, for a Choice you describe each option, and for a Score you describe each level.

The [state docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/state) recommend a JSON object for most requests. For bug triage that's natural, because the interesting context is already structured: which repo, the title, the labels, the body.

<RequestBuilder />

Notice what happens in the code tab. Each answer feeds ordinary code. The model never decides to assign the bug or link it to a deploy; your `if` does. That's the whole design: Jev provides judgment, your code holds the policy.

One more design rule from the docs: **one condition per question**. "Is this a checkout regression?" is two questions pretending to be one. Ask them separately and combine them with `&&` where you can see it, test it, and change it.

## Lesson 4: Thresholds live in your code

A Noul gives you a probability, not a verdict. Deciding what counts as "yes" is your job, and the right line depends on what a mistake costs. The [Noul docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/noul) put it simply: use 0.5 when both errors cost the same, raise the bar when a false yes is expensive, lower it when missing a yes is expensive, and send the middle to a person.

In a subscription product, the two mistakes are not equal. If the bot keeps a customer who asked for a human, they're a lot more likely to cancel, and that's exactly the kind of churn conversion engineers get asked about. If you escalate someone who didn't need it, a support person spends a minute. So you'd probably lean toward escalating.

If you bet on sports, this already feels familiar. A 60% read on a game isn't a bet by itself; at -200 the book is asking you to be right 66.7% of the time, so a 60% read is a pass. A probability only becomes an action once you've priced what being wrong costs. Thresholds are that price.

Two things to notice. First, the "ambiguous" messages are ambiguous to people too. "Are you a bot?" might be curiosity or might be the first sign someone wants out. A probability near the middle is the model telling you that, and a review band gives you somewhere to put it. Second, you can't pick good thresholds from six examples. An [independent evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37647) of Jev found Noul probabilities were good at ranking but often badly placed relative to a fixed 0.5 cutoff. Translation: tune your thresholds on your own labeled data, and pin the model version once you have, because the `jev-latest` alias moves.

## Lesson 5: Reading a score

A Score returns a `score` that's the probability-weighted average of level positions, which is why you'll see values like 1.43 on a 0 to 2 scale. It also returns the per-level `probabilities`, a `legend` of your level descriptions, and a `confidence`.

The single number hides a lot. The first two presets are real bug reports from the docs: a Safari-only crash on the settings page, and a broken PDF export with a CSV workaround. Compare the second and third: they produce the same score for completely different reasons.

<ScoreMixer />

The level descriptions matter more than anything else here. The [Score docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/score) show what happens with numbers-only levels like `["0", "1", "2"]`: a cosmetic bug scored 0.55 with 0.33 confidence. With descriptive levels ("Cosmetic; no impact to functionality" and so on), the same bug scored 0.0 with 1.0 confidence. Their rule of thumb is **describe situations, not degrees**. "Blocking issue; no workaround exists" beats "high." If you've ever written a bug severity guide or a grading rubric, this is the same skill.

Keep each Score to one dimension too. "Severity and customer impact" is two scales. A model that's sure a bug is cosmetic and sure it hits every customer will give you a muddy middle.

## Lesson 6: Where it breaks

I like Jev. I also think the quickest way to get burned by a new model is to believe its launch post. So here's the skeptical lesson.

**Typed doesn't mean right.** TypeSafe says Jev can't hallucinate, and the output can't be malformed: you always get a valid probability or a valid option. But the [Hacker News discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49717558) pointed out the obvious problem: a well-formed answer can still be the wrong answer. "Auth" is a perfectly valid owning team for a bug that actually lives in checkout.

**Calibration is about groups.** A calibrated 0.8 means that across many questions scored 0.8, about 80% turn out yes. It says nothing about whether this particular customer wants a human. Confidence is a routing signal, not proof.

Betting taught me this the expensive way. I ran a [martingale experiment](/martingale-tracker) this year: 104 settled bets went 56–48 and finished about $50 up on roughly $4,900 staked. Any single bet told me nothing about whether my reads were good; even 104 bets is a small sample. Judging a model's probabilities works the same way. You need a lot of decisions, grouped by the probability the model gave, before you can say whether a 0.8 really means 80%.

**Benchmarks are the vendor's.** The speed and cost comparisons pit a model that returns a few numbers against models generating full responses. That's a fair comparison for decision tasks, but it's not the same as "better." TypeSafe's own launch notes that some of its workflow evals were built by the team that built the model. Independent work so far is mixed: [one evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37647) ran 346,009 requests across 37 datasets for under $10 and found Choice probabilities well calibrated, while [another paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02267) found zero-shot model routing was no better than chance.

**Know the limits.** Text and JSON only. English works best. Requests top out around 64k tokens unless you opt into a long-context header the docs literally call `TypeSafe-Danger-Zone-Long-Context`. And "none of the above" doesn't work well as a Choice option; the docs suggest a separate Noul instead.

The practical version: label a few hundred real examples from your product, measure, tune thresholds, pin the model version, log every answer with the version that produced it, and always keep a human path for the middle.

## Cheat sheet

- **Noul** is an `if`: one condition, a probability of yes, thresholds in your code.
- **Choice** is a `switch`: describe every option, check confidence before acting.
- **Score** is a `sort`: describe situations, not degrees; read the probabilities, not just the number.
- **State** is facts; **questions** are framing. Keep them apart.
- Split compound questions and combine answers with code you can test.
- Always have a third path for the ambiguous middle, usually a person.
- Tune on your own data, pin the version, log everything.
