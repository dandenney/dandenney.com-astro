I've spent most of my career building places where people learn to code, from Code School to DataCamp. The best part of learning in the browser was always the loop: try something, get feedback right away, try again. So this is the first of a new kind of post for me, a mini course. Every section has something to poke at and a checkpoint at the end, and your progress saves in this browser.

The subject is [Jev](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev), the first "System One" model from TypeSafe, and decision models in general. I didn't want to explain it with made-up examples, so everything here runs on my own data. Since April I've logged [Rithmm's picks](/betting/rithmmm-picks), 253 of them so far, each with Rithmm's confidence and how the game actually went. I sent the 252 that settled as a win or a loss through Jev and saved every answer. The question running through the whole course is pretty simple: can a decision model help me choose which picks to bet?

## What a decision model is

System 2 is the slow, effortful reasoning, and it's what most of the LLM conversation for the last few years has been about: models that write, plan, and reason out loud. Jev goes the other way. You hand it some text or JSON, ask it questions, and get back typed answers with probabilities your code can branch on. It can't write a sentence, summarize, or chat. The [System One docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/system-one) are upfront about that, and it's kind of the point.

Every question is one of three types, and each maps to something you already write in code:

- A Noul (as in Bernoulli) is a yes/no question. It returns one number between 0 and 1, the probability the answer is yes. It's an `if`.
- A Choice picks one option from a set you define. It returns the winner, a probability for every option, and a confidence. It's a `switch`.
- A Score places something on ordered levels you describe. It returns a position, a probability per level, and a confidence. It's what you `sort` by.

It's also cheap enough that cost barely comes up. My whole run was 504 requests (every pick twice, more on that in a minute) and 330,106 input tokens. At $0.042 per million input tokens, with output tokens free, that's about a penny and a half. It finished in 8 seconds.

## A pick becomes state

A request has two parts. The **state** is the thing being judged: a string, a JSON object, or an array of strings (text only for now). The **questions** are what you want to know about it. Every question sees the same state, each one is answered on its own, and they all come back in one response, so asking four questions doesn't cost four round trips.

The [state docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/state) suggest keeping the state to facts and putting your framing in the questions. For a pick, the facts are easy: the date, sport, matchup, the pick, the odds, and recent form. Nothing about the result or what I bet. Then I asked every pick the same four questions: will it win, does recent form back it, what kind of bet is it, and how strong is the case at these odds.

Here's the twist. I sent every pick twice, once with Rithmm's projection in the state ("Tigers 5.9, Angels 3.2") and once without it.

<RequestInspector />

Rithmm's projection is an opinion about the game, but sitting in the state it reads like a fact. With it, Jev's pass/lean/play Score landed on "play" for 69 picks. Without it, Jev never said "play" once. Look at the Tigers moneyline: recent form 2/10, a lean of 0.07 on the facts alone, and 1.21 once Rithmm's projected runs show up. It lost. Adding the projection also made Jev worse at telling winners from losers overall, which we'll get to in a couple of sections.

If you want to compare Jev against another model, the lesson is to keep the other model out of the request and compare the two numbers in your own code.

## Reading the three answers

The Noul is the simplest. "Does recent form support this pick?" came back 0.84 for a receptions over with 9/10 recent form, and 0.10 for a Sky moneyline with 1/10. That one number turns out to be one of the most useful things in the whole run, so keep it in mind.

The Choice is where Jev embarrassed me a little. I asked what kind of bet each pick was and compared the answer to the labels in my own data. The first time I checked, Jev "missed" 40 of them. It hadn't. I'd filed 39 run lines, game totals, and a moneyline as "combo" player props. Jev was right, my labels were wrong, and I fixed them. A few of them:

| Pick | My old label | Jev |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blue Jays +1.5 | combo over | spread |
| Athletics @ Mariners Over 7.5 Total Runs | combo over | game total |
| Tigers @ Reds Under 9.5 | combo under | game total |
| Blue Jays Moneyline | combo over | moneyline |

It also caught one more: a total bases pick I'd typed as an Under when I actually bet the Over. After fixing both, Jev matches my labels on all 252.

The Score is the probability-weighted average of the level positions, so with pass at 0, lean at 1, and play at 2, a 0.9 means "mostly lean." The [Score docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/score) say to describe situations instead of degrees, which is why my levels read like "Lean; some support, but a small stake at most" instead of just "1." The catch is that the average hides how the probability is spread.

<ScoreSplit />

That's what `confidence` is for. The [confidence docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/confidence) put it at 1 when all the probability sits on one level and 0 when it's spread about evenly. Check it before your code acts on the score.

## Thresholds are break-even

A Noul gives you a probability and leaves the decision to you. The [Noul docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/noul) suggest starting with 0.7 for yes and 0.3 for no, sending the middle to a person, and raising the bar when a false yes is expensive.

Betting hands you the line for free, because the odds already tell you what being wrong costs. At -150 you risk 150 to win 100, so you need to win 150 out of 250, or 60%, just to break even. At +120 it's 100 out of 220, about 45%. Every pick has its own threshold, and your code can work it out:

```ts
const breakEven = odds < 0 ? -odds / (-odds + 100) : 100 / (odds + 100);

if (answers.will_hit.noul > breakEven + margin) placeBet(pick);
```

<ThresholdLab />

At zero margin, Jev on the facts alone picks 86 of the 248 picks and goes 57–29 for +19.42 units, compared with +6.43 for betting all of them. With Rithmm's projection in the state, it bets 135 and finishes +16.57. Rithmm's own number cleared break-even on every single pick, so its version of the rule is "bet everything."

One thing I was careful about: zero margin is just break-even, so I didn't tune it. Dragging the slider until the units peak is exactly the kind of fitting the docs warn about ("avoid fitting thresholds closely to a handful of test cases"). It's fun to look at, but the best margin on these 248 picks won't be the best margin on the next 248.

## Did the numbers mean anything?

There are two different questions you can ask about any set of probabilities, and a model can pass one and fail the other:

- Calibration: when it says 60%, do those picks hit about 60% of the time?
- Ranking: do the winners get higher numbers than the losers?

An [independent evaluation of Jev](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37647) found that split on yes/no questions: its probabilities ranked well but were poorly placed against a fixed 0.5 cutoff. Here's how it looks on my picks.

<CalibrationChart />

Rithmm's points sit on a flat line around 60% no matter what it said. Its most confident group (67% to 74%) hit 62.5%, about the same as its least confident group. That's a ranking skill of 0.49, a coin flip, and its probabilities were actually a touch less accurate than just using the break-even rates from the odds.

Jev on the facts alone climbs. Its least confident group hit 56% and its most confident group hit 69%. It was too cautious overall (averaging 54% on picks that hit 58%), but it put them in roughly the right order. If you're wondering what "ranking skill" means in practice, this is the easiest way to see it.

## Where it breaks

I like what I saw. I also think the quickest way to get burned by a new model is to believe the first good chart, so here's what I don't trust yet.

### Jev might have seen the results

Every one of these games happened between April and October, and Jev 1.14 became available on October 14. If any of these results ended up in its training data, the "will it win" numbers would look smart for the wrong reason. The gap is small, though. Jev averaged 54.5% on picks that hit and 52.8% on picks that missed, which doesn't look like a model peeking. And a lot of the signal is sitting right there in the input: the raw recent form number (7/10 as 0.7) ranks winners at 0.56 all by itself, and Jev's "does form back this pick?" Noul ranks at 0.59. I still can't rule it out.

### The sample is small

57–29 sounds great. Fewer than 1 in 200 random 86-bet slices of these picks do that well, so it probably isn't pure luck. But the range on Jev's ranking skill runs from 0.51 to 0.65, and the bottom of that range is barely better than a coin flip.

### Typed doesn't mean right

The launch post says Jev "can't hallucinate" and "never makes type errors," and both held up here: every answer came back well-formed. But "0.62 that this bet wins" is a valid answer whether or not Jev knows anything about baseball.

### The benchmarks are the vendor's

TypeSafe's own launch post notes its workflow evals were made by its own team, "so some bias could exist." Independent work is mixed so far. [One evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37647) ran 346,009 requests across 37 datasets for under $10 and found Choice probabilities well calibrated, while [another paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02267) found neither model it tested beat chance at zero-shot model routing.

### Pin the version

I used `jev-1.14.0`, not `jev-latest`. The alias will move, and every number in this post could move with it.

### So, can it help me choose?

On these 248 picks, a plain break-even rule on Jev's facts-only read beat betting everything, and Rithmm's own number couldn't. I'm not betting on that until it holds up on games Jev couldn't have seen.

### What's next

The only clean test is going forward: score each pick before the game, log the answer with the model version, and check back after a few hundred more. That's what I'm building next.

## Cheat sheet

- A Noul is an `if`: one condition, one probability, and the threshold lives in your code.
- A Choice is a `switch`. Describe every option and check confidence before you act.
- A Score is a `sort`. Describe each level as a situation and read the probabilities along with the number.
- The state holds facts. Another model's projection is an opinion, so compare it in your code.
- In betting, the threshold is break-even, and the odds hand it to you.
- Check calibration and ranking separately, on your own data, with enough of it to mean something.
- Pin the model version and log it with every answer.
